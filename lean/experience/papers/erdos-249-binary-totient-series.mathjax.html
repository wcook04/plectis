Problem note

Integral Relations among Totient Sections

Erdős #249

Introduction

Splitting the totient sequence into its even and odd subsequences, then splitting once more, gives seven sequences:

\[\varphi(n);\qquad \varphi(2n),\ \varphi(2n+1);\qquad \varphi(4n),\ \varphi(4n+1),\ \varphi(4n+2),\ \varphi(4n+3).\]

The identities

\[\varphi(4n)=2\varphi(2n),\qquad \varphi(4n+2)=\varphi(2n+1)\]

reduce this list to five sequences, which are linearly independent over \(\Q\). Every rational linear identity among the seven therefore follows from these two substitutions. The substitutions also preserve integral coefficients, so they describe the integral relations as well.

The same description holds at every finite depth and in every base. For an integer \(k\ge2\), the totient sections are the sequences \(n\mapsto\varphi(k^jn+r)\), where \(j\ge0\) and \(0\le r<k^j\). Throughout, \(n\) ranges over \(\N=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}\) and \(\varphi(0)=0\). To describe relations we retain a separate index for each pair \((j,r)\), even when two pairs give the same sequence. For example, \(\varphi(4n+2)\) and \(\varphi(2n+1)\) are equal sequences but occupy different positions in the list above.

The sections form the \(k\)-kernel of the totient sequence. A sequence is \(k\)-regular when the \(\Z\)-module generated by its \(k\)-kernel is finitely generated [1]. Coons proved that the totient sequence is not \(k\)-regular for any \(k\ge2\) [2]. We describe the finitely generated modules obtained by stopping at a fixed level. The theorem below specifies a basis and expresses each remaining section as a scalar multiple of a basis element; these expressions give a test for every linear identity at that level. The level-zero family contains only \(\varphi(n)\) and has dimension one.

Theorem 1.1 (A basis through each finite level). Let \(k\ge2\) and \(e\ge1\) be integers, write \(F^{(k)}_{j,r}(n)=\ph(k^jn+r)\), and put

\[V_{k,e}=\operatorname{span}_{\Q} \{\,F^{(k)}_{j,r}:0\le j\le e,\ 0\le r<k^j\,\}.\]

Then \(\dim_{\Q}V_{k,e}=k^e+1\), and

\[\mathcal B_{k,e} =\{F^{(k)}_{0,0},F^{(k)}_{1,0}\} \cup\{\,F^{(k)}_{j,r}:1\le j\le e,\ 1\le r<k^j,\ k\nmid r\,\}\]

is a basis. Every omitted section reduces to a basis element by an explicit scalar: \(F^{(k)}_{j,0}=k^{j-1}F^{(k)}_{1,0}\) for \(j\ge1\), and if \(r=k^tu\) with \(t=\max\{s:k^s\mid r\}\ge1\) and \(k\nmid u\), then

\[F^{(k)}_{j,r}=C_k(t,u)\,F^{(k)}_{j-t,u}, \qquad C_k(t,u)=k^t\prod_{\substack{p\mid k\\ p\nmid u}}\Bigl(1-\tfrac1p\Bigr).\]

The condition \(k\nmid r\) allows residues with \(\gcd(k,r)>1\). For instance, \(r=1\) and \(r=2\) are both retained in base six, and the reductions involving them are

\[\varphi(36n+6)=2\varphi(6n+1),\qquad \varphi(36n+12)=4\varphi(6n+2).\]

The multipliers differ because \(2\) divides \(6n+2\) but not \(6n+1\). The product formula also allows repeated prime factors of \(k\), so there is no squarefreeness assumption on the base.

Martin’s 2006 theorem on simultaneous totient inequalities already implies independence for every finite family of pairwise nonproportional affine forms with positive integral slopes [4]. Its conclusion is stronger than the independence used here. The point of the present description is the explicit reduction of the sections and the integral relation basis in Corollary 3.1. We also give a direct proof that constructs a nonsingular evaluation matrix. It uses the CRT–Dirichlet separation argument in Yazdani’s proof of Theorem 2, credited there to Shallit [12]. The step that needs care is to keep the progression for the selected affine form reduced while imposing prime divisors on all the others.

Section 2 proves the basis theorem. Section 3 starts with the six reductions in base six and derives the integral relation basis. In Section 4 we use prime isolation again, for bounded functions of totient residues. The series

\[S=\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{\varphi(n)}{2^n}\]

has unbounded coefficients. Its irrationality, asked in Erdős Problem #249 [10], remains open. The coefficient basis supplies no irrationality conclusion for this value. Appendix A gives further coefficient consequences; Appendices B and C collect the tail identities, comparison sequences and still-unproved cancellation estimates relevant to \(S\). Their longer proofs are in The Binary Totient Series.

Proof of the basis theorem

Scalar reductions

There are two parts to the proof. The product formula reduces every omitted section to a retained one; independence then shows that no further reductions are possible. In the first part, the multiplier depends only on which prime divisors of \(k\) divide the argument. For a zero residue, \(kn\) and \(k^jn\) have the same prime divisors when \(n>0\). Hence \(\varphi(k^jn)=k^{j-1}\varphi(kn)\); both sides vanish at \(n=0\). For an omitted positive residue \(r=k^tu\) with \(t\ge1\) and \(k\nmid u\), we have \(t<j\) because \(0<r<k^j\). Put \(m=k^{j-t}n+u\). A prime \(p\mid k\) divides \(m\) exactly when it divides \(u\). The product formula for \(\varphi(k^tm)/\varphi(m)\) therefore contributes \(k^t\) and one factor \(1-1/p\) for each prime \(p\mid k\) absent from \(u\). This gives \(C_k(t,u)\) and proves that the stated family spans \(V_{k,e}\).

Affine independence

Independence follows from Martin’s theorem. For any ordering of pairwise nonproportional affine forms and any \(C>0\), it gives a set of positive lower density on which each successive totient ratio exceeds \(C\). In a proposed nonzero relation, place a nonzero coefficient \(c_1\) first and take \(C>\max(1,\sum_{i>1}|c_i|/|c_1|)\). The first term then dominates the sum of all remaining terms in absolute value, a contradiction [4]. This also proves independence after any finite prefix is deleted.

We give a direct proof by constructing a square matrix of evaluations with nonzero determinant. Let \(L_i(n)=a_i n+b_i\) for \(1\le i\le s\), where \(a_i>0\) and \(a_i b_j-a_j b_i\ne0\) for \(i\ne j\). A shift of \(n\) makes all the \(b_i\) positive and leaves these cross determinants unchanged. Write \(g_i=\gcd(a_i,b_i)\) and \(A_i=L_i/g_i\). If \(g_i>1\), the values of \(L_i\) have a fixed divisor, so it is the primitive form \(A_i\), not \(L_i\), that we will make prime. We will choose a prime \(\ell\) and points \(n_1,\ldots,n_s\) for which

\[\begin{equation} \label{eq:diagonal-evaluation} E=\bigl(\varphi(L_j(n_i))\bigr)_{1\le i,j\le s} \equiv \begin{pmatrix} \varphi(g_1)&0&\cdots&0\\ 0&\varphi(g_2)&\cdots&0\\ \vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\ 0&0&\cdots&\varphi(g_s) \end{pmatrix}\pmod\ell, \qquad \ell\nmid\prod_i\varphi(g_i). \end{equation}\]

A rational relation among the sequences would give a vector in the kernel of \(E\). The displayed congruence makes its determinant nonzero, so no such relation can exist.

For row \(i\), we require \(L_j(n_i)\) to have a prime divisor \(q_{ij}\equiv1\pmod\ell\) whenever \(j\ne i\). Then \(q_{ij}-1\) divides \(\varphi(L_j(n_i))\), giving the off-diagonal zeros in eq:diagonal-evaluation. For the diagonal entry, we require \(A_i(n_i)\) to be a prime congruent to \(2\) modulo \(\ell\). Choose an odd \(\ell\) dividing none of the slopes \(a_i\), the products \(g_i\varphi(g_i)\) or the nonzero cross determinants. For each fixed \(i\), Dirichlet’s theorem supplies distinct primes \(q_{ij}\equiv1\pmod\ell\) (\(j\ne i\)) outside the same finite exceptional set. We impose

\[A_i(n)\equiv2\pmod\ell,\qquad L_j(n)\equiv0\pmod{q_{ij}}\quad(j\ne i).\]

Each congruence is soluble because its slope is coprime to its modulus. CRT combines them into \(n=n_0+Ru\), where \(R=\ell\prod_{j\ne i}q_{ij}\).

Dirichlet’s theorem must now be applied to the values of \(A_i\), rather than to \(n\). These values form the progression

\[A_i(n_0+Ru)=A_i(n_0)+(a_i/g_i)Ru.\]

It remains to check that its initial value and step are coprime. Primitivity excludes the prime factors of \(a_i/g_i\), and the residue \(2\) excludes the odd prime \(\ell\). A prime \(q_{ij}\) cannot divide \(A_i(n_0)\): it would then divide both \(L_i(n_0)\) and \(L_j(n_0)\), hence

\[a_iL_j(n_0)-a_jL_i(n_0)=a_i b_j-a_j b_i,\]

contrary to its choice. This accounts for every prime factor of the step. We may therefore choose \(p=A_i(n_i)\) prime, as large as needed, and in particular with \(p\nmid g_i\). Then

\[\varphi(L_i(n_i))=\varphi(g_i)(p-1) \equiv\varphi(g_i)\not\equiv0\pmod\ell,\]

while all the other entries of row \(i\) vanish modulo \(\ell\). We choose the rows separately, applying Dirichlet’s theorem once more for each selected form \(A_i\). Thus no simultaneous primality assertion is required. The construction proves eq:diagonal-evaluation with every \(n_i\) beyond any prescribed threshold.

The two zero-residue sections

The affine-independence argument does not separate \(\varphi(n)\) and \(\varphi(kn)\), because their arguments are proportional. We use two different values of their ratio. On \(n=km+1\) the ratio is \(\varphi(k)\), so a proposed relation contains only one zero-residue term after this restriction. The positive-residue sections become the forms

\[k^{j+1}m+k^j+r\quad(k\nmid r),\]

together with \(km+1\). These are pairwise nonproportional: equality of two intercept-to-slope ratios would give \(r/k^j=r'/k^{j'}\), which for \(j'>j\) forces \(k\mid r'\). No positive-residue form is proportional to \(km+1\). Affine independence eliminates every positive-residue coefficient and leaves \(A+B\varphi(k)=0\), where \(A,B\) multiply \(\varphi(n),\varphi(kn)\). We now evaluate the remaining relation at \(n=k\) to obtain \(A+Bk=0\). Since \(\varphi(k)<k\), the two equations force \(A=B=0\). Finally,

\[\lvert\mathcal B_{k,e}\rvert =2+\sum_{j=1}^e(k^j-k^{j-1})=k^e+1,\]

which completes the proof of Theorem 1.1. For independence on a tail, use a sufficiently large power of \(k\) in the last evaluation.

Integral relations

The reductions give a second way to test a linear identity, by substitution. Consider base six at level two, where the family has \(43\) indices. The \(37\) retained sections are \(F_{0,0},F_{1,0}\), the five \(F_{1,r}\) (\(1\le r\le5\)), and the \(30\) sections \(F_{2,r}\) with \(1\le r<36\) and \(6\nmid r\). The omitted sections satisfy

\[\begin{aligned} F_{2,0}&=6F_{1,0}, & F_{2,6}&=2F_{1,1}, & F_{2,12}&=4F_{1,2},\\ F_{2,18}&=3F_{1,3}, & F_{2,24}&=4F_{1,4}, & F_{2,30}&=2F_{1,5}. \end{aligned}\]

Thus an integral combination of the \(43\) sections vanishes precisely when these six substitutions leave all \(37\) retained coefficients zero. The six differences obtained by moving the right-hand sides to the left give every integral relation. Indeed, subtracting the original coefficient of each omitted section times its difference removes all six omitted coordinates. The remaining retained coordinates must then vanish by Theorem 1.1. Writing \(x_{j,r}\) for the original coefficient of \(F_{j,r}\), the coefficient at \(F_{1,2}\) after substitution is \(x_{1,2}+4x_{2,12}\). Meanwhile \(x_{2,12}\) is the coefficient of \(E_{2,12}-4E_{1,2}\) in the expression for the relation, where \(E_{j,r}\) is the generator of the free abelian group at index \((j,r)\). The two kinds of coefficient have different roles: the retained coefficients test whether the combination vanishes, while the omitted coefficients give its expression as a sum of relations. At base two and level two those relations are \(E_{2,0}-2E_{1,0}\) and \(E_{2,2}-E_{1,1}\). The same elimination works at every omitted index.

Corollary 3.1 (Integral coordinates and all integral relations). Let \(k\ge2\) and \(e\ge1\). The retained family is a \(\Z\)-basis of the module generated by the sections through level \(e\). Index the sections by their level and residue, retaining distinct indices even when they define equal sequences. For each omitted index \(i\), write the scalar reduction as \(F_i=a_iF_{j(i)}\), where \(j(i)\) is retained and \(a_i\) is a nonnegative integer. In the free abelian group with one generator \(E_i\) for each of these indices, the vectors

\[R_i=E_i-a_iE_{j(i)}\qquad(i\text{ omitted})\]

form a \(\Z\)-basis of the kernel of evaluation \(E_i\mapsto F_i\). In particular, every integral relation has a unique integral expression in these elementary relations, and their rank is \(\sum_{j=1}^{e-1}k^j\).

Proof. Since \(\prod_{p\mid k,\,p\nmid u}p\) divides \(k\), every scalar \(C_k(t,u)\) is an integer. The retained family therefore spans the section module over \(\Z\), and its rational independence makes it a \(\Z\)-basis.

For any vector \(x=\sum_i x_iE_i\), subtracting \(\sum_{i\text{ omitted}}x_iR_i\) leaves only retained coordinates. At a retained index \(j\), the new coefficient is

\[x_j+\sum_{\substack{i\text{ omitted}\\j(i)=j}}a_i x_i.\]

Thus the substitutions give the integral coordinates explicitly. If \(x\) evaluates to zero, these new coefficients all vanish by independence, so \(x=\sum_{i\text{ omitted}}x_iR_i\). Conversely, the coefficient at omitted index \(i\) in \(\sum_h c_hR_h\) is \(c_i\) itself, so we recover every coefficient without division. This proves both independence and uniqueness. Counting omitted indices gives \((1+k+\cdots+k^e)-(k^e+1)=\sum_{j=1}^{e-1}k^j\). ◻

The assertion concerns the module generated by the sections. It need not contain every integer-valued sequence in its rational span: for \(k=2\) and \(e\ge2\), the sequence \(\varphi(4n+3)/2\) is integer-valued and has coefficient \(1/2\) in the retained basis. Thus this module is not saturated in the group of integer-valued sequences.

An alternative is to test equality at finitely many inputs. Return to base six and level two. Order the retained columns as above, with residues increasing within each level, and evaluate at \(n=0,\ldots,36\) to obtain a \(37\times37\) matrix. Its determinant is \(17\) modulo \(101\), so it is nonsingular over \(\Q\) and detects every nonzero retained combination at one of these inputs. The script scripts/totient_kernel_normal_form.py calculates the coordinates and the first such input using integer arithmetic. The equality test uses the integer evaluations. Reducing the proposed combination modulo \(101\) would miss \(101F_{2,2}\), for example, although its value at \(n=0\) is \(101\).

Bounded functions of totient residues

A similar use of congruences produces a nonzero totient residue between arbitrarily long blocks of zero residues. We use this to classify the rational series obtained from bounded functions of dyadic totient residues. The proof rests on a tail estimate: one zero block makes the tail vanish under the assumption of rationality, and the other leaves a nonzero term too small for that assumption. In the first sum below, residues are represented by their least nonnegative values. These coefficients need not be binary digits.

Theorem 4.1 (Series of totient residues). For every \(m\ge3\),

\[\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{\ph(n)\bmod m}{2^n}\notin\Q.\]

For \(k\ge1\) and \(f:\Z/2^k\Z\to\Q\), the series \(\sum_{n\ge1}f(\ph(n)\bmod2^k)2^{-n}\) is rational exactly when \(f\) is constant on the even residue classes. If that constant is \(c\), its value is \(3f(1)/4+c/4\).

Modulo two the sum is \(3/4\), since \(\varphi(n)\) is even for \(n\ge3\). More generally only \(f(1)\) and the even residue classes occur in the second assertion. The following coefficient pattern is the basis of the proof:

\[\underbrace{0,\ldots,0}_{L\text{ terms}},\quad a_N\ne0,\quad \underbrace{0,\ldots,0}_{L\text{ terms}}.\]

The coefficients outside these blocks may have either sign. Their uniform bound is what makes the following argument possible.

Lemma 4.2 (An isolated nonzero coefficient between long zero blocks). Let \(a_n\in\Z\) satisfy \(|a_n|\le C\). Suppose that for arbitrarily large \(L\) there is \(N>L\) such that \(a_N\ne0\) and \(a_{N+t}=0\) for \(0<|t|\le L\). Then \(\sum_{n\ge1}a_n2^{-n}\) is irrational.

Proof. Suppose that the sum has denominator \(q\ge1\). Multiplying it by \(2^j\) and removing its finite integer prefix shows that every scaled tail \(T_j=\sum_{t\ge1}a_{j+t}2^{-t}\) belongs to \(q^{-1}\Z\). Choose a pattern with \(C2^{-L}<1/q\). The block on the right gives

\[|T_N|\le C\sum_{t>L}2^{-t}=C2^{-L}<1/q.\]

The only element of \(q^{-1}\Z\) in this interval is zero, so \(T_N=0\). Now start before the left block. The next \(L\) coefficients vanish, and the entire contribution after \(a_N\) has just been shown to be zero. Consequently

\[T_{N-L-1}=a_N2^{-L-1},\qquad 0<|T_{N-L-1}|\le C2^{-L-1}<1/q,\]

contradicting \(T_{N-L-1}\in q^{-1}\Z\). ◻

For example, the indicator of the squares satisfies the lemma. An eventually periodic sequence with infinitely many nonzero terms does not have isolated coefficients at arbitrarily large distances. The uniform bound \(C\) is essential to this argument.

Proof of Theorem 4.1. Fix a unit \(s\pmod m\) and a positive integer \(L\). Choose distinct primes \(q_t\equiv1\pmod m\), all exceeding \(L\), for \(0<|t|\le L\). The congruences

\[p\equiv s\pmod m,\qquad p\equiv-t\pmod{q_t}\quad(0<|t|\le L)\]

define a reduced progression, since \(s\) is a unit modulo \(m\) and \(0<|t|<q_t\). CRT and Dirichlet’s theorem therefore allow us to choose an arbitrarily large prime \(p>L+1\) on this progression, with \(m\mid\varphi(p+t)\) at every neighbour and \(\varphi(p)\equiv s-1\pmod m\) at the centre.

For a rational-valued function \(f\) on the residue classes, subtract \(f(0)\) and multiply by a common denominator of its values. Since \(\sum_{n\ge1}2^{-n}=1\), these operations preserve whether the sum is rational. If \(f(s-1)\ne f(0)\), the resulting bounded integer sequence has an isolated nonzero coefficient for every \(L\), so the lemma applies. For the least-residue map we take \(s=-1\) and obtain the nonzero residue \(m-2\) when \(m\ge3\). When \(m=2^k\), each even residue \(r\) is of the form \(s-1\) with \(s\) a unit, so nonconstancy on the even classes gives an irrational sum by the same argument. Conversely, if \(f\) is constant there, all terms after \(n=2\) equal \(c\) and we obtain \(3f(1)/4+c/4\). ◻

The first assertion is also an application of Erdős’s sparse-series criterion [6], and of Kaneko, Suzuki and Tachiya [7]. The nonzero residues occur on a set of density zero: an integer in that set has no prime divisor \(p\equiv1\pmod m\). Wong gave the case in which the base equals the modulus [5]. The isolated-coefficient argument above also treats signed values of \(f-f(0)\), for which those nonnegative sparse-series criteria need an additional spacing hypothesis. The extension to other bases treats every integer \(t\ge2\) and proves rational linear independence of \(1\) and \(\sum_n(\varphi(n)\bmod m)t^{-dn}\), \(d\ge1\).

Further consequences for the sections

The finite-level theorem also describes the full dyadic kernel. The notation below specialises the base to two.

Corollary A.1 (Dyadic basis). The family

\[\{\ph_{0,0},\ph_{1,0}\} \cup\{\ph_{j,r}:j\ge1,\ 0<r<2^j,\ r\text{ odd}\}, \qquad \ph_{j,r}(n)=\ph(2^jn+r),\]

is a basis for the rational span of the full dyadic kernel. Every rational relation is generated by the scalar reductions. The complete level-zero truncation has dimension one.

Proof. Each finite subset of this family is contained in some \(\mathcal B_{2,e}\). Theorem 1.1 gives independence, and its reductions give spanning. At level zero the sole section \(\varphi(n)\) is nonzero, since \(\varphi(1)=1\). ◻

The affine argument also permits periodic coefficients by restriction to their common residue classes.

Corollary A.2 (Periodic coefficients). Let \(L_1,\ldots,L_s\) be pairwise nonproportional affine forms with integer coefficients and positive slopes. If \(w_1,\ldots,w_s\) are rational-valued periodic sequences, then

\[\sum_i w_i(n)\ph(L_i(n))=0\quad\text{for all sufficiently large }n \quad\Longrightarrow\quad w_i(n)=0\quad\text{for every }i,n.\]

Proof. We choose a common period \(Q\) and restrict to \(n=r+Qt\). The coefficients are now the constants \(w_i(r)\), and the cross determinants are \(Q(a_i b_j-a_j b_i)\ne0\). By shifting \(t\) beyond the given threshold we can apply affine independence, obtaining \(w_i(r)=0\) for every \(i\). We repeat this for each residue \(r\); periodicity then gives the conclusion even at the indices preceding the threshold. ◻

Proportional forms are excluded for a reason: the identity \(\varphi(4n+2)=\varphi(2n+1)\) would otherwise contradict the conclusion. Martin’s theorem gives the same corollary on each progression [4].

Coons’s proof of nonregularity counts the poles of \(\zeta(s-1)/\zeta(s)\) [2]. For an integer-valued sequence, finite dimension of the rational kernel span is equivalent to finite generation of its integral kernel module: choose finitely many evaluations that embed the span in \(\Q^d\); the module embeds in \(\Z^d\), whose subgroups are finitely generated (compare [3]). Theorem 1.1 recovers this nonregularity and gives the ranks of its finite truncations. For an account of the earlier separation method, see Allouche, Shallit and Yassawi [13].

These facts have a functional consequence. A \(k\)-Mahler equation has the form \(\sum_{i=0}^d P_i(z)F(z^{k^i})=0\), with \(P_i\in\Q[z]\) and \(P_0\ne0\). The height theorem of Adamczewski, Bell and Smertnig implies that a \(k\)-Mahler series with integer coefficients of polynomial growth is \(k\)-regular [16]. Since \(\varphi(n)\le n\), its logarithmic height is at most \(\log n\), so \(F_\varphi(z)=\sum_{n\ge1}\varphi(n)z^n\) has no such equation. Bell and Smertnig’s multiplicative classification gives the same conclusion [14]. Bell, Bruin and Coons treat related algebraic and \(D\)-finite series, the latter satisfying a linear differential equation with polynomial coefficients [15]. These statements concern the function, and do not determine \(F_\varphi(1/2)\).

The section operators \(T_i g(n)=g(kn+i)\) satisfy

\[T_iF_{j,r}=F_{j+1,k^ji+r},\qquad V_{k,e+1}=V_{k,e}+\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}T_iV_{k,e}.\]

To obtain the second identity, write every residue at level \(j+1\) as \(k^ji+r\). For a positive retained residue, the resulting section is again retained. For the two zero-residue generators, use \(T_iF_{0,0}=F_{1,i}\) and \(T_iF_{1,0}=F_{2,ki}\) followed by the zero-residue or scalar reduction. We thus obtain a positive integer multiple of a basis element in every case, although the increasing dimensions prevent any finite truncation from being invariant. The growth formula using the joint spectral radius for finite-dimensional regular sequences [3] therefore does not apply to \(\varphi\).

The binary totient series

The proof of Theorem 4.1 uses boundedness where a long zero block makes the tail small. For \(S\), the estimate \(0\le\varphi(n)\le n\) proves convergence, but forcing \(2^L\mid\varphi(N+L)\) with \(N+L>1\) already requires \(2^L\le N+L-1\). The tail bound \((N+L+2)2^{-L}\) then exceeds \(1\). Thus this divisibility construction does not supply the small nonzero tail needed to exclude a rational value. We consider instead a finite test for differences of scaled tails.

Put \(R_N=\sum_{j\ge1}\varphi(N+j)2^{-j}\) and \(\Delta_h(N)=R_{N+h}-R_N\). Subtracting the finite-prefix identities for \(2^{N+h}S\) and \(2^NS\) gives

\[\begin{equation} \label{eq:tail-phase} \Delta_h(N)\equiv2^N(2^h-1)S\pmod\Z. \end{equation}\]

If \(S=a/(2^cv)\), with \(v\) odd, then \(\Delta_h(N)\) is integral for \(N\ge c\) and \(h=\varphi(v)\). To disprove rationality it therefore suffices to find a nonintegral difference beyond every threshold for each positive \(h\).

For \(h,N,L\in\N\), define the integer

\[D_{h,N,L}=\sum_{j=0}^{L-1} \bigl(\varphi(N+h+1+j)-\varphi(N+1+j)\bigr)2^{L-1-j}, \qquad B=N+h+L+2.\]

Since \(0\le R_M\le M+2\), we have

\[\begin{equation} \label{eq:tail-error} 2^L\Delta_h(N)-D_{h,N,L}=R_{N+h+L}-R_{N+L},\qquad |2^L\Delta_h(N)-D_{h,N,L}|\le B. \end{equation}\]

The two tails are nonnegative, so their difference is bounded in absolute value by the larger tail bound, not the sum of the bounds. An integral \(\Delta_h(N)\) would put \(D_{h,N,L}\) within distance \(B\) of a multiple of \(2^L\). Hence the finite inequality

\[\begin{equation} \label{eq:r3-certificate} B<D_{h,N,L}\bmod2^L<2^L-B \end{equation}\]

forces \(\Delta_h(N)\notin\Z\). Conversely, hold \(h,N\) fixed and suppose that \(\|\Delta_h(N)\|_{\R/\Z}>0\). Choose \(L\) so that \(2^L\|\Delta_h(N)\|_{\R/\Z}>2B\): the left side grows exponentially, whereas \(B=N+h+L+2\) grows linearly. The same error estimate gives eq:r3-certificate. This detects every nonintegral difference at some depth; it does not produce such a difference for each \(h\). For example, \(D_{1,12,16}=-143140\) has residue \(53468\) modulo \(2^{16}\), and \(B=31\), so it excludes denominators dividing \(2^{12}\).

Equivalent residue conditions

We write \(K(h,N,L)\) for eq:r3-certificate.

Theorem B.1 (Propagation of one nonintegral tail difference). Fix \(d\ge1\) and \(N\ge0\). If \(\Delta_d(N)\notin\Z\), then every sufficiently late pair \(\{t,t+1\}\) contains an \(m\) such that \(K(md,N,md)\) holds. Consequently

\[\exists t\ge1:\ K(td,N,td) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\Delta_d(N)\notin\Z.\]

Requiring this for every \(d\ge1,N\ge0\) is equivalent to \(S\notin\Q\).

Here the depth is no longer free: it must equal the shift. The recurrence \(\Delta_{(t+1)d}(N)\equiv2^d\Delta_{td}(N)+\Delta_d(N)\pmod\Z\) prevents two consecutive large shifts from both failing the test. Two failures would force the fixed nonintegral \(\Delta_d(N)\) arbitrarily close to an integer. The estimates are in Appendix A of the long record. A single pair \(d,N\) need not imply irrationality; the final assertion requires every positive \(d\) and every \(N\ge0\).

For a hypothetical denominator \(2^cv\), a second test uses the modulus \(M=(2^H-1)/v\), where \(H\) is a positive multiple of \(\varphi(v)\). Write \(B_{H,c}=\sum_{j=0}^{H-1}\varphi(c+1+j)2^{H-1-j}\).

Equivalent formulation B.2 (A residue test modulo a divisor of \(2^H-1\)). The series \(S\) is irrational if and only if, for every \(c\ge0\) and odd \(v\ge1\), some positive multiple \(H\) of \(\ph(v)\) satisfies

\[\begin{equation} \label{eq:canonical-gap} c+H+1<(-B_{H,c})\bmod M<M-(c+H+1). \end{equation}\]

The proof and residue recurrence use \(B_{H,c}=M(vR_c)-\Delta_H(c)\) and the linear tail bound. The required residues have not been shown to exist for every choice of the parameters in either test. A separate Farey-interval calculation at depth \(240\) excludes \(S=a/q\) for \(0<q\le Q_0\approx7.96\times10^{34}\).

Comparison sequences

Large ranks alone cannot settle the series question. There is an integer sequence \(c\) with \(0\le c(n)\le n\), unchanged odd totients and \(|c(n)-\varphi(n)|\le2\) at even indices, for which \(\sum c(n)2^{-n}=5/4\). Its integral carry still has dyadic rank at least \(2^e-1\) through every positive level. The construction and its limitations explain why it fails the exact prime-dilation laws of \(\varphi\). The same section retains the cofinal LCM-diagonal criterion, its endpoint sign conditions and the limits of finite integer combinations of shifts.

The signed interpolation argument gives a different family of comparison sequences. For each integer \(\beta\ge2\), their sums can take any value in an interval about \(\sum\varphi(n)\beta^{-n}\), while a prescribed prefix and every odd coefficient stay fixed. The errors may be bounded by any positive \(f(n)\to\infty\), and the changed even indices have upper Banach density zero. On each fixed arithmetic progression, the difference of the associated Dirichlet series is entire. At roots of unity, the difference of the generating functions extends smoothly in the radial direction, with any prescribed finite collection of radial derivatives vanishing. Thus the Dirichlet singularities are preserved, although regular parts and zeros may change; finitely many radial derivatives do not determine the full radial germ. This ordinary proof has not been formalised in Lean and gives no cancellation estimate for the unchanged totient sequence.

Rational approximation imposes a different requirement. The identity

\[\Theta_2:=\sum_{d\ge1}\frac{\mu(d)}{(2^d-1)^2}=S-\frac12\]

was posted by Amiram Eldar, together with its coprimality interpretation, on 15 March 2026 [8], and by Steve Fan on 16 May 2026 [9]. Consider the rational quotients

\[Q(e,Y)=\frac{\bigl(\sum_{d\le Y}\mu(d)/(2^d-1)^{e+2}\bigr)^2} {\sum_{d\le Y}\mu(d)/(2^d-1)^{2e+2}}.\]

Theorem B.3 (A lower bound for the rank-one quotients). For \(e\ge1\) and \(Y\ge4\), the denominator of \(Q(e,Y)\) is positive, and the unique minimiser is \((e,Y)=(1,5)\). Every admissible quotient and every nonempty finite positive weighted average of such quotients satisfies

\[Q-\Theta_2>\frac{21}{320}.\]

The uniform bound \(Q-\Theta_2>1/15\) is false already at \((1,5)\).

The proof first makes the denominator uniformly positive. A geometric bound handles all \(e\ge2\); at \(e=1\), seven-term prefixes leave only \(Y=4,5,6\) to compare directly. The averaging assertion concerns averages of the quotients themselves. It leaves open signed combinations, quotients formed after combining the sums, and higher-rank kernels. A proposed family \(p_j/q_j\) in lowest terms would need \(0<q_j|\Theta_2-p_j/q_j|\to0\), which convergence alone does not imply.

An exponential-sum question

For every \(h\ge1\) and every \(X_0\), suppose that some integers \(X\ge\max(X_0,1)\) and \(L\ge h\) satisfy

\[\begin{equation} \label{eq:r3-harmonic} 16(2X+h+L+2)\le2^L,\qquad \operatorname{Re}\sum_{N=X}^{2X-1} \exp(2\pi iD_{h,N,L}/2^L)\le\frac9{10}X. \end{equation}\]

These inequalities would prove irrationality. Indeed, if every residue failed eq:r3-certificate, each phase would be within \(1/16\) of an integer, so every real part would be at least \(\cos(\pi/8)>9/10\). Some \(N\) in the block must therefore satisfy eq:r3-certificate. For a hypothetical rational value, we choose its period and then a sufficiently late block, contradicting eq:tail-phase.

To separate the counting estimates from the cancellation estimate, we single out one totient in each window. Fix \(h,s\ge1\), \(L\ge s+h\), \(X\ge1\) and \(0<\eta<1\), and put \(t=L-s+1\) and \(E_N=\exp(2\pi iD_{h,N,L}/2^L)\). For \(X\le N<2X\), let \(p_N\) be the largest prime factor of \(N+t\) and \(m_N=(N+t)/p_N\). Define

\[\begin{aligned} \mathcal A&=\{N\in[X,2X):m_N\le\lfloor\sqrt X/2\rfloor, \ p_N>2\lfloor\sqrt X\rfloor\},\\ \mathcal G&=\{N\in\mathcal A:\varphi(m_N)\ge\eta m_N\}. \end{aligned}\]

All indices are integers. On \(\mathcal A\) we have \(p_N>m_N\), so \(\varphi(N+t)=\varphi(m_N)(p_N-1)\). Its coefficient in the phase is \((2^h-1)/2^t\). Write

\[z_N=\exp\!\left(\frac{2\pi i(2^h-1)\varphi(m_N)(p_N-1)}{2^t}\right), \qquad w_N=E_N/z_N,\]

and let \(\bar z_m\) be the average over the indices in \(\mathcal A\) with cofactor \(m\), taking the average to be zero for an empty set. We seek the four bounds

\[\begin{equation} \label{eq:r3-four-bounds} \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Re}\sum_{N\in\mathcal G}w_N(z_N-\bar z_{m_N}) &\le\tfrac{14}{25}X,\\ \left|\sum_{N\in\mathcal G}w_N\bar z_{m_N}\right| &\le\tfrac1{100}X,\\ \left|\sum_{N\in\mathcal A\smallsetminus\mathcal G}E_N\right| &\le\tfrac1{100}X,\\ \left|\sum_{\substack{X\le N<2X\\N\notin\mathcal A}}E_N\right| &\le\tfrac8{25}X. \end{aligned} \end{equation}\]

Before taking real parts and absolute values, the four sums add to \(\sum E_N\), so their bounds imply the real-part estimate \(9X/10\) in eq:r3-harmonic. Subtracting \(\bar z_m\) cancels the unweighted sum within its cofactor group, but the factors \(w_N\) may vary within that group. Thus centering alone does not bound the first sum. The second sum must also be estimated with those same weights. No independence has been assumed. The conclusion concerns the real part; it does not give a \(21X/25\) bound for the absolute value of the full sum.

Problem C.1 (Four estimates for the first-harmonic sum). For each \(h\ge1\), do there exist \(s\ge1\) and \(0<\eta<1\) such that, for every \(X_0\), there are \(X,L\) with

\[\max(X_0,1)\le X,\qquad h\le L-s,\qquad 16(2X+h+L+2)\le2^L,\]

for which those four bounds hold?

The parameters \(s\) and \(\eta\) may depend on \(h\), but must be fixed before the threshold \(X_0\) is given. All four estimates must then hold for one common choice of \(X,L\). For each fixed \(h,s\), take the least admissible \(L\) satisfying the size inequality in eq:r3-harmonic and \(L\ge s+h\). The smooth-number and small-cofactor estimates give the last two bounds for all sufficiently large \(X\) when \(0<\eta<1/(300\log4)\). The elementary excluded-cofactor estimate is \((3\eta\log4+o(1))X\); it needs no prime number theorem. The first two bounds in eq:r3-four-bounds remain open. No optimality is asserted for the sufficient margins in eq:r3-four-bounds.

Alternatively, at \(s=26\), \(\eta=1/1000\) and minimal depth one has \(|\mathcal G|>67X/100\) for large \(X\). The single open estimate \(\operatorname{Re}\sum_{N\in\mathcal G}E_N\le603X/1000\), for each \(h\) on arbitrarily large blocks, would suffice by the same argument. The ordinary comparison argument gives larger first harmonics at all sufficiently large admissible scales, distinguishing its rational sequences from the cancellation required of \(\varphi\). Its proof is included with the interpolation construction.

Balasubramanian, Giri and Srivastav give correlation estimates applicable to \(\varphi(n)/n\), uniformly for \(|h|\le x/2\) [11]. Here the modulus grows with \(L\), and the residual weight depends on other totient values in the window. That correlation theorem supplies neither of the two remaining estimates. Establishing them for the actual totient is the unresolved arithmetic step.
