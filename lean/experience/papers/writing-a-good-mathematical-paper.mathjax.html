Compact writing guide

Writing a Good Mathematical Paper

Exposition method

Enable a reader who knows the field to state the result, explain why the proof works and use it correctly. Spend words where they remove a specific difficulty.

1. Decide what the paper contributes. Choose a coherent principal result and an intended reader. Say what becomes possible or understood because of the result, how it differs from the nearest prior work, and what remains open. For a conditional result, explain how restrictive the hypothesis is. An abstract should identify the actual result and mechanism; theorem count and generality alone do not establish importance.

2. Read the kind of mathematics you are writing. Read nearby papers in the same subject and genre, including complete local arguments. Study terminology, hypothesis placement, notation, proof order and attribution. Also seek a proof with the same explanatory difficulty: a choice, a limit or an estimate. Check the edition and passage. Adapt the reason for a choice; write your own sentences and supply your own mathematical warrant.

3. Check the statement before polishing its prose. Check domains, quantifiers, signs, endpoints, constants, parameter dependencies and both directions of any claimed equivalence. State assumptions where the claim is made. Compare titles, abstracts and conclusions with the full statement. Separate witnesses need not be a common witness: over the natural numbers, \(\forall n\,\exists x\;(x>n)\) does not imply \(\exists x\,\forall n\;(x>n)\). An exposition pass that exposes a proof gap must refer it for mathematical review; elegant wording cannot close it.

4. Organise by logical dependence. Present the question, the result and the proof’s main idea before technical detail accumulates. Use an example where it prepares the reader for an unfamiliar operation. State a lemma’s purpose before its proof; place prerequisites before use. Let headings name the object, result or operation. Give a reader entering at a theorem enough information to find its assumptions and proof. Choose length to suit the argument; there is no universal page quota.

5. Explain the hard transition. Name the quantity to control and the accuracy required before deriving an estimate. Explain which constraint selects a parameter or change of variables. For an interval certificate, compare the error with both endpoint gaps. For a small-integer contradiction, justify integrality, nonvanishing and \(|N|<1\) separately. Spend detail on the decisive inference. A proof paragraph should make clear what the operation establishes and what remains to prove.

6. Separate construction from completion. Account for each choice and preserved condition. A classification needs both construction and exhaustiveness. An enclosure alone does not show that every target is attained. For example, \(r_n=x-S_n\), \(0\le r_n\le\varepsilon_n\) and \(\varepsilon_n\to 0\) give \(S_n\to x\); repeated choice alone does not. State which parameters are fixed and justify the limit of the whole summand, including any growing product. To interchange a limit and a sum, identify an applicable theorem and check its hypotheses.

7. Make examples and figures do work. Choose a small instance that reveals a definition, invariant, obstruction or necessary hypothesis. Show the operation and its consequence, including an endpoint case when the convention matters. Explain what the example leaves unproved. A finite check supports an infinite claim only through a proved reduction. Check that a witness belongs to the claimed object. Label a diagram’s objects, maps and conventions, and keep its explanation nearby.

8. Use the field’s words and purposeful notation. Keep established terms when their definitions fit, such as “Stieltjes moment sequence”. Replace private labels by the actual condition; \(u_n/2^n\to 0\) is weaker than subexponential growth. Define symbols before use, keep their roles stable and remove names that save no work. Translate borrowed indices, coordinates and normalisations. Distinguish a sum, its partial sums and its remainders, and a value from the interval enclosing it.

9. Make sentences express the argument. Replace “We proceed with the estimate” by a reason: “Orthogonality makes the first sum zero; it remains to bound the second.” Make connectives and referents unambiguous. After “similarly”, check that the same hypotheses apply. Punctuate displayed equations as parts of sentences. Use parallel syntax for parallel claims and keep conditions attached to their consequences. Remove throat-clearing and inflated significance; retain precise terms without manufacturing variety.

10. Credit the exact input at its use. Distinguish applied theorems, adapted constructions, historical attribution, terminology and expository models. Check the original’s hypotheses, version and passage. State what changes when an argument is adapted. Credit supplied ideas and corrections locally. A writing analogy supplies no proof. Report only reading and checks actually performed.

11. State what has actually been established. Separate logical scope from evidence. A conditional implication may have an ordinary or formal proof; a computation or conjecture needs its own label. Keep unproved mathematical inputs beside the dependent result. A checked nearby lemma does not formalise the whole proof; missing formalisation does not invalidate an ordinary proof. Give finite computations their scope and reproduction details in a compact verification section. A test can eventually detect every nonintegral fixed value without certifying integrality when it fails. It still does not produce the witnesses required by a quantified theorem.

12. Preserve useful detail and informative failures. Let the short paper sustain its principal argument and the longer record make the rest recoverable. Move detail only after checking its destination; reconcile statements, assumptions and evidence in both directions. Preserve the attempted implication, assumptions, witness and missing ingredient of a useful failed route. Distinguish a flawed step, a failed method and a counterexample to a theorem. Motivate the finished proof without inventing its discovery history. Cite a specific section or theorem for omitted arguments; keep build commands and source identifiers with the verification record.

13. Review changes as claims, then as prose. Compare old and new meanings, including explanatory words beside unchanged formulas. Correct overclaims and stale underclaims. Removing shorthand must preserve its hypotheses; a familiar term must meet its definition. Accept, repair, reject or defer each substantive proposal for a stated reason. Generalise a lesson only as far as its evidence supports, retaining its limit. Keep useful rejected revisions recoverable.

14. Read the delivered paper from a cold start. Render the final sources. Inspect formulas, captions, page breaks, bibliography and cross-reference destinations. Read without supplying steps from memory: can the intended reader reconstruct the hard inference, apply the result in an example and identify an excluded case? Record actual difficulties. A successful build is not a comprehension test. Stop adding prose when it removes no identifiable difficulty and improves neither accuracy nor recovery of the argument.

Further reading. Halmos, How to write mathematics, §§3–5 (1970); Knuth et al., Mathematical Writing, §§1–3 (1988); Gowers, Examples first! (2007); Tao, On writing. Worked cases, sources and revision history: the long companion.
