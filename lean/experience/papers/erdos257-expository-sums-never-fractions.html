The question: which infinite sums are irrational?

Every finite sum of fractions is a fraction. An infinite sum can be one too: 12+14+18+…=1.\frac12+\frac14+\frac18+\dots=1. Now consider S=13+115+1255+165535+…,S=\frac13+\frac1{15}+\frac1{255}+\frac1{65535}+\dots, where the denominators are 22−1,24−1,28−1,216−1,…2^2-1,2^4-1,2^8-1,2^{16}-1,\dots. The exponents double at each step. We will prove that this sum cannot equal any fraction.

Multiply by 255255, the third denominator: 255S=85+17+1+25565535+…=103+1257+….\begin{aligned} 255S&=85+17+1+\frac{255}{65535}+\dots\\ &=103+\frac1{257}+\dots. \end{aligned} The first three terms have become the integer 103103, leaving a positive remainder. If S=p/qS=p/q, with integers p,qp,q and q>0q>0, multiplying by an integer and subtracting an integer leaves an integer multiple of 1/q1/q. A positive remainder must therefore be at least 1/q1/q. We will repeat the construction to make the whole remainder smaller than this fixed gap, whatever denominator qq is proposed.

This example belongs to a larger family. Its fractions are obtained by taking a power of 22, subtracting 11, and taking the reciprocal:

Every series SAS_A converges: for n≥1n\ge1, 0<12n−1≤12n−1,∑n=1∞12n−1=2.0<\frac1{2^n-1}\le\frac1{2^{n-1}},\qquad \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1{2^{n-1}}=2. A basic property of real numbers says that an increasing, bounded sequence has a finite limit (Appendix A.1). The issue is whether this limit is rational.

Erdős’s question and the theorem

In a 1988 account, Erdős wrote [3] (excerpt):

“let n1<n2<…n_1<n_2<\dots. Is it then true that ∑12nk−1\sum \frac{1}{2^{n_k}-1} is irrational, …”

The indices k=1,2,…k=1,2,\dots count the chosen exponents nkn_k. In these terms, we prove:

Theorem 1. Let n1<n2<…n_1<n_2<\dots be an infinite sequence of positive integers. If ∑k=1∞1/nk<∞\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}1/n_k<\infty, then ∑k=1∞12nk−1\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac1{2^{n_k}-1} is irrational.

Taking A={n1,n2,…}A=\{n_1,n_2,\dots\}, this series is exactly SAS_A, and the additional hypothesis is exactly ∑n∈A1/n<∞\sum_{n\in A}1/n<\infty. The question for arbitrary infinite AA remains open [4].

The condition concerns the reciprocals of the exponents. The conclusion concerns the fractions with denominators 2n−12^n-1. For the doubling exponents, the distinction is visible in one table:

chosen exponent nn

The second row sums to 11. The theorem therefore says that the third row sums to an irrational number. The hypothesis is an additional restriction on AA; it is not needed merely to make SAS_A converge.

We will use the hypothesis in this form: beyond a sufficiently large exponent, the remaining reciprocals 1/n1/n together add up to less than any positive target we specify. This remaining sum is called a tail.

The square exponents A={1,4,9,16,…}A=\{1,4,9,16,\dots\} also satisfy the condition. Indeed, for m≥2m\ge2, 1m2<1m(m−1)=1m−1−1m,\frac1{m^2}<\frac1{m(m-1)}=\frac1{m-1}-\frac1m, so the comparison sum cancels: (1−12)+(12−13)+(13−14)+…+(1N−1−1N)=1−1N<1.(1-\tfrac12)+(\tfrac12-\tfrac13)+(\tfrac13-\tfrac14) +\dots+(\tfrac1{N-1}-\tfrac1N)=1-\tfrac1N<1. Thus ∑m=2N1/m2<1\sum_{m=2}^{N}1/m^2<1. Hence ∑m=1∞1/m2\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}1/m^2 converges, and the theorem proves that 1+115+1511+165535+…1+\tfrac1{15}+\tfrac1{511}+\tfrac1{65535}+\dots is irrational.

By contrast, choosing every positive integer gives the divergent harmonic series in the hypothesis. Group its terms as 11; 1/21/2; 1/3+1/41/3+1/4; 1/5+1/6+1/7+1/81/5+1/6+1/7+1/8; and so on. For example, 15+16+17+18≥4⋅18=12.\frac15+\frac16+\frac17+\frac18\ge4\cdot\frac18=\frac12. Every subsequent block doubles the number of terms and halves their smallest value, so again contributes at least 1/21/2. These repeated contributions make the partial sums unbounded. Even exponents also fail the condition, since their reciprocal sum is half the harmonic series. The theorem makes no assertion for these choices. Infinitude is essential too: A={2,4}A=\{2,4\} has a finite reciprocal sum, but SA=13+115=25S_A=\tfrac13+\tfrac1{15}=\tfrac25. (Question 1.)

Erdős proved the theorem in 1968 under the extra assumption that no two chosen exponents share a divisor greater than 11, and stated that this assumption could be removed without supplying the details [2]. We explain a proof without that assumption.

The proof has three stages. First, rationality forces a fixed lower bound on certain positive remainders. Next, the denominators 2n−12^n-1 let us calculate those remainders explicitly. Finally, a small average guarantees at least one choice whose total remainder is below that lower bound. The doubling exponents show the construction at work before the averaging is needed. The main proof ends in Section 7.2. Appendices A.1–A.4 justify the supporting infinite-sum and divisibility facts; they can be read as needed or after the main proof. The other-base extension and formal-proof correspondence are further reading.

A rational number has a fixed positive gap

Start with 2/52/5. Multiplying it by an integer and subtracting an integer always gives an integer multiple of 1/51/5: 3⋅25−1=15,4⋅25−1=35,5⋅25−2=0,1⋅25−1=−35.3\cdot\frac25-1=\frac15,\quad 4\cdot\frac25-1=\frac35,\quad 5\cdot\frac25-2=0,\quad 1\cdot\frac25-1=-\frac35. There is no such value strictly between 00 and 1/51/5. The denominator fixes a smallest possible positive result.

The same reasoning applies to any fraction. From now on write S=SAS=S_A for the series belonging to our chosen set AA, and suppose S=p/qS=p/q, where p∈ℤp\in\mathbb Z and qq is a positive integer. For integers u,ju,j, uS−j=up−jqq.uS-j=\frac{up-jq}{q}. Its numerator is an integer. If uS−j>0uS-j>0, then up−jq≥1up-jq\ge1, and therefore uS−j≥1q.uS-j\ge\frac1q. Positivity is necessary: zero and negative values do not contradict rationality.

To prove our sum irrational, we will therefore suppose that S=p/qS=p/q and construct integers u,ju,j, which may depend on qq, satisfying 0<uS−j<1q.\begin{equation} \label{eq:goal} 0<uS-j<\frac1q. \end{equation}

A complete example: the doubling exponents

Return to the opening example, A={2,4,8,16,…}A=\{2,4,8,16,\dots\}. We now know why 255255 cleared its first three terms: it is 28−12^8-1, and the exponents 2,4,82,4,8 all divide 88. The special feature was that kk both eliminated all earlier terms and left the next exponent at least 2k2k. A general set AA need not have that spacing. The next section explains how averaging replaces it.

Why averaging works

Larger multipliers need not help.

For the square exponents A={1,4,9,16,…}A=\{1,4,9,16,\dots\}, both k=4k=4 and k=8k=8 are divisible by the first two selected exponents 1,41,4. The choices of kk matter, not just how many initial rows they make zero.

Average complete attempts.

Different rows may be small in different columns; choosing a separate column for each row would not give one multiplier for the original series. Instead average every row across the same columns, then add those row averages. This gives the average of the complete column totals: both calculations add the same entries and divide by the same number MM. In symbols, 1M∑m=1MR(mQ)=∑n∈A1M∑m=1Mℓn(mQ).\begin{equation} \label{eq:average-main} \frac1M\sum_{m=1}^{M}R(mQ) =\sum_{n\in A}\frac1M\sum_{m=1}^{M}\ell_n(mQ). \end{equation}

For infinitely many rows we first use finite portions and then let them grow; convergence preserves the equality (Appendix A.4). If this average is below 1/q1/q, at least one whole column has total below 1/q1/q. Otherwise every column total would be at least 1/q1/q, making their average at least 1/q1/q too.

What the two estimates will do.

We will divide the rows into three groups. An initial finite group stays zero. A further finite group can complete its cycles if we choose MM appropriately; we will bound each such row’s average by 1/n1/n. For the infinitely many remaining rows, we need a bound valid for every MM: we will prove 2Q/n2Q/n. Once QQ is fixed, we can make the sum of these bounds as small as needed by moving far enough down the rows: the remaining reciprocals 1/n1/n have arbitrarily small total. Section 7 will choose the two row cutoffs and then MM so that both nonzero groups have small total averages.

Consequences and limits

For every integer b≥2b\ge2, the same method proves A infinite,∑n∈A1n<∞⇒∑n∈A1bn−1 is irrational.A\text{ infinite},\quad\sum_{n\in A}\frac1n<\infty \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \sum_{n\in A}\frac1{b^n-1}\text{ is irrational}. Replace the multiplier by bk−1b^k-1. The fractional parts are (bkmod⁡n−1)/(bn−1)(b^{k\operatorname{mod} n}-1)/(b^n-1), and their cycle averages are still below 1/n1/n. Appendix A.5 checks both estimates.

The theorem covers arbitrary selections from the square-exponent series 1+115+1511+…1+\tfrac1{15}+\tfrac1{511}+\dots. If infinitely many remain, their sum is irrational: deleting terms cannot increase the reciprocal-exponent sum ∑m≥11/m2<∞\sum_{m\ge1}1/m^2<\infty, so Theorem 1 still applies. If only finitely many remain, their sum is rational. Thus a selection from this series has rational sum exactly when it is finite; no pattern in the retained exponents is required. Replacing 22 by any integer b≥2b\ge2 gives the same conclusion for the same retained exponents.

The reciprocal-sum condition is sufficient, not necessary. These particular remainders R(k)R(k) need not become arbitrarily small for every infinite AA. When every positive exponent is selected, the terms with n>kn>k alone give R(k)≥∑n>k2k−12n−1>∑n>k2k−12n=1−2−k≥12.R(k)\ge\sum_{n>k}\frac{2^k-1}{2^n-1} >\sum_{n>k}\frac{2^k-1}{2^n} =1-2^{-k}\ge\frac12. Thus these remainders can never be arbitrarily small. Nevertheless, Erdős proved that the full sum is irrational in 1948 [1]. Failure of our method does not imply rationality. Tao and Teräväinen proved irrationality for the prime exponents [5], another case with divergent reciprocal sum. The short paper [6] gives further sufficient conditions; the long record [7] develops the proofs and the obstructions to extending them. The question for every infinite set AA remains open.

Convergence and tails

For nonnegative terms ana_n, the partial sums ∑n=1Nan\sum_{n=1}^{N}a_n increase. If they are bounded above, let LL be their least upper bound: the smallest real number at least every partial sum. For every ε>0\varepsilon>0, some partial sum exceeds L−εL-\varepsilon; otherwise L−εL-\varepsilon would be a smaller upper bound. Every later partial sum then lies in (L−ε,L](L-\varepsilon,L], so the series converges to LL. Its tail after NN equals L−∑n=1NanL-\sum_{n=1}^{N}a_n and tends to zero. Why the sampled remainders form a cycle

The elementary cancellation fact is: if positive integers c,uc,u are coprime and cc divides dudu, then cc divides dd. The example in Section 6.2 used c=3,u=2c=3,u=2. For general coprime c,uc,u, the same argument uses integers a,ba,b with au+bc=1au+bc=1. Here is why they exist. Among the positive values au+bcau+bc, allowing a,ba,b to be any integers, choose the smallest, vv; there is at least one since u=1⋅u+0⋅cu=1\cdot u+0\cdot c is such a combination. Adding the row averages

For finitely many rows n≤Hn\le H, adding the row averages equals averaging the column totals: both operations add the same entries and divide by MM. Keep QQ and MM fixed.
