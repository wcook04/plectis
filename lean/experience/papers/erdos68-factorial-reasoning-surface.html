Which prime powers survive reduction

Problem 1.1 (Erdős #68). Is S=∑n≥2(n!−1)−1S=\sum_{n\ge2}(n!-1)^{-1} irrational?

Erdős states the question on p. 102 of his 1988 survey and, in the same passage, records the expectation that ∑n1/(n!+t)\sum_n1/(n!+t) is irrational, indeed transcendental, for every integer tt [1]. Here the sum starts sufficiently far out that n!+t>0n!+t>0; changing that starting index adds or removes only a rational number. In particular, no term with zero denominator is included. The expectation for all integer shifts is not proved here. Numbering follows Bloom’s Erdős problem catalogue [2].

Put dn=n!−1,HM=∑n=2M1dn,LM=lcm⁡(d2,…,dM),AM=∑n=2MLMdn,d_n=n!-1,\qquad H_M=\sum_{n=2}^{M}\frac1{d_n},\qquad L_M=\operatorname{lcm}(d_2,\ldots,d_M),\qquad A_M=\sum_{n=2}^{M}\frac{L_M}{d_n}, so that HM=AM/LMH_M=A_M/L_M. Throughout, den⁡(x)\operatorname{den}(x) is the positive denominator of a rational number in lowest terms, and vp(a)v_p(a) is the exponent of the prime pp in a positive integer aa. The first question is which prime power present in LML_M remains in den⁡(HM)\operatorname{den}(H_M). For the largest exponent the answer follows by specialising Louwsma and Martino’s valuation formula for an elementary symmetric sum . Indeed, ∑i1/xi=(∑i∏j≠ixj)/∏ixi\sum_i1/x_i=(\sum_i\prod_{j\ne i}x_j)/\prod_i x_i for positive integers xix_i. Their lemma evaluates the numerator’s valuation; subtracting the product’s valuation gives the reciprocal-sum form. The proof below uses the least common multiple instead of the product.

The need to check cancellation is already visible in 1/3+1/15=2/51/3+1/15=2/5: the common denominator contains 33, but the reduced denominator does not. By contrast, 1/9+1/45=2/151/9+1/45=2/15 only lowers the exponent of 33 from 22 to 11. The theorem below tests whether the largest exponent is preserved; a failure of that test need not remove the prime completely. The formula is standard, whereas the two factorial-prefix examples below and the later tail comparison concern this particular series.

The construction proves cofinality of first occurrences, but not the inequality involving qq needed for the additional two-factor test in §4. The sufficient tail inequality there does not require choosing a prime qq. Wilson reflection limits what can be inferred from a large prime factor alone: for odd 2≤n<q2\le n<q with q∣n!−1q\mid n!-1, the identity (q−1−n)!n!≡(−1)n+1(mod⁡q)(q-1-n)!\,n!\equiv(-1)^{n+1}\pmod q gives the reflected congruence at q−n−1q-n-1. The growth of the common denominator

A non-polynomial comparison.

For n!+2n−1n!+2^n-1, Luca and Shparlinski [19] eliminate the exponential term using three hits; multiplicative order enters the counting. Their valuation-layer identity [19] records repeated prime-power divisibility. These are related tools, not inputs to Theorem 2.4, and no extension to general non-polynomial perturbations is asserted.

Within the terminal-block estimate just proved, maximising α−α3/6\alpha-\alpha^3/6 gives 22/32\sqrt2/3. This is an optimisation of that one-parameter bound, not an optimality theorem for all block selections or all gcd arguments. The discarded factorial term has size N3/2N^{3/2}, so a finite normalised ratio need not be close to the limiting lower bound.

Theorem 2.4 concerns LNL_N, not den⁡(HN)\operatorname{den}(H_N). Theorem 1.2 and the cancellations at 139139 and 25932593 show why these cannot be interchanged: a prime present in the common denominator may be absent after reduction. The two examples establish this possibility, not an asymptotic estimate for cancellation.

Even two consecutive denominators obstruct clearing by the full lcm. For N≥3N\ge3, the gcd of (N−1)!−1(N-1)!-1 and N!−1N!-1 divides N−1N-1, whereas (N−1)!−1(N-1)!-1 is coprime to N−1N-1. Thus those denominators are coprime, and LN(S−HN)>((N−1)!−1)(N!−1)(N+1)!−1→∞.L_N(S-H_N)> \frac{((N-1)!-1)(N!-1)}{(N+1)!-1}\longrightarrow\infty. This argument does not use the stronger asymptotic growth theorem.

The direction of denominator control matters. Write HN=ÂN/QNH_N=\widehat A_N/Q_N in lowest terms, reserving ANA_N above for the numerator over the full common denominator. Under the hypothetical identity S=a/qS=a/q, with a∈ℤa\in\mathbb{Z} and q≥1q\ge1, positivity of the tail gives qQN(S−HN)=aQN−qÂN∈ℤ>0.qQ_N(S-H_N)=aQ_N-q\widehat A_N\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}. A direct clearing contradiction therefore requires an upper estimate that makes qQN(S−HN)<1qQ_N(S-H_N)<1 for some NN; equivalently, it needs QNQ_N to be small relative to the reciprocal tail. A lower bound on QNQ_N points in the opposite direction. Indeed, the rational limit 11 and the approximants 1−1/QN1-1/Q_N have arbitrarily large reduced denominators without any irrationality. The inequality dN(S−HN)<1d_N(S-H_N)<1 alone would not repair the argument, because dNd_N generally does not clear the earlier summands of HNH_N. The prime-power analysis describes which factors survive reduction, but it does not yet supply the required upper control on QNQ_N.

For linear forms in pp-adic zeta values, with p≥5p\ge5 a fixed prime and nn the index of the form, Lai, Lupu and Sprang quantify a saving of this kind. They bound the denominators of the coefficients by powers of lcm⁡(1,…,n)\operatorname{lcm}(1,\ldots,n), show that the bound may be divided by a product Φn\Phi_n of prime powers, and compute the growth rate of Φn\Phi_n . That saving enters the inequality [15] under which the rescaled forms, which have integer coefficients, meet the irrationality criterion that they quote from Lai [16]. Along one unbounded subsequence, the forms must be nonzero and their pp-adic absolute values, multiplied by the largest ordinary absolute value of an integer coefficient, must tend to zero. The coefficient bound is archimedean; the bound on the value is pp-adic. The conclusion is that at least one of the finitely many pp-adic numbers in the forms is irrational, not that each is irrational. The corresponding saving for HNH_N is the ratio LN/QNL_N/Q_N.

This ratio is an integer. Merely naming it supplies neither its growth nor a small linear form; the analogy is about what an eventual estimate would have to accomplish. Theorem 1.2 decides, for each prime p∣LNp\mid L_N, whether pp divides this ratio; no asymptotic lower bound for the ratio is established here. This paragraph compares the two methods; no pp-adic theorem is applied to SS.

Relations among the criteria

The two finite exclusions in §6 use only the carry criterion and a rational enclosure. The coefficient constructions do not enter either calculation. They instead produce integer linear forms ℛ(c)=M(c)S+k\mathcal R(c)=M(c)S+k, k∈ℤk\in\mathbb{Z}. At fixed moment, changing coefficients changes only kk; primitive cofactors on the stated progression reproduce the explicit progression vector rather than a new family.

For a rational value S=a/qS=a/q, such a form is integral whenever q∣M(c)q\mid M(c). To extend this method beyond finite exclusions, one therefore needs nonintegral remainders with moments covering every positive denominator in this divisibility sense. The common-denominator lower bound and the prime-power survival test do not supply the needed upper bound for a remainder relative to its integer gap. Likewise, the equivalent carry, digit and interval criteria still require events at arbitrarily large indices. A finite calculation can rule out denominators without proving any of those unbounded assertions.
