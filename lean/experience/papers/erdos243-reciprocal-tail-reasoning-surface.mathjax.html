Introduction

Sylvester’s sequence \(2,3,7,43,1807,\ldots\) is generated by \(a_{n}=a_{n-1}^{2}-a_{n-1}+1\) and has reciprocal sum \(1\); the recurrence is exactly the statement that the tail beyond \(a_{n-1}\) equals \(1/(a_{n-1}-1)\). Erdős Problem #243 asserts that this is the only way a sequence of that growth can have a rational reciprocal sum:

See [4] and [5]. Bloom’s catalogue records the problem as open [8]. Below we index the same recurrence as \(a_{n+1}=a_n^{2}-a_n+1\), which is the shift the formal sources use; the two forms are the same statement. Write \(\operatorname{syl}(a)=a^{2}-a+1\), the .

The hypothesis is asymptotic and the conclusion is exact, so the argument must convert an analytic rate into an integer obstruction. The conversion used here is the classical one: clear denominators along the sequence, so that rationality makes a tail integral, and then centre that integer at the value it would take on Sylvester’s sequence. What remains is a single integer error \(E\), and the whole question is whether \(E\) can avoid vanishing.

Section \(\ref{long243:sec:cubicrate}\) carries out that conversion at one prescribed rate and settles the assertion of Problem \(\ref{long243:res:problem}\) on the resulting class of sequences. Section \(\ref{long243:sec:priorwork}\) places the theorem against the published sufficient conditions, states the evidence class of every result here, and guides the rest of the note.

Keywords. irrationality; Ahmes series; Sylvester’s sequence; unit fractions; Lean 4. MSC 2020. 11J72 (primary); 11B37, 11D68, 68V20 (secondary).

An unconditional irrationality theorem

This section proves the note’s lead theorem and the arithmetic exclusion it rests on. It is self-contained: the integer state it uses is constructed here in six lines, and Section \(\ref{long243:sec:state}\) returns to that construction at leisure.

The quantifier in Theorem \(\ref{long243:res:cubicexclusion}\) is the one that the irrationality theorem needs. It excludes agreement along prefixes whose proportion of exceptions tends to zero, along every subsequence of prefix lengths, which is more than the exclusion of eventual equality. The two restrictions that meet on the same cubic are integrality, which supplies the rising-factorial shape and the scale, and the denominator transport, which imposes an arithmetic square condition; the scale calculation and the two residue words that close the argument can be checked on the page.

The canonical integer tail.

Suppose \(\sum_{n\ge1}1/a_n=p/q\) with \(p,q\ge1\), and write \(x_n=\sum_{k\ge n}1/a_k\) for the tail. Put \[D_n=q\prod_{k<n}a_k,\qquad C_n=D_nx_n .\] Then \(D_n\) is a positive integer, and \[C_n=D_n\Bigl(\frac pq-\sum_{k<n}\frac1{a_k}\Bigr) =p\prod_{k<n}a_k-q\sum_{k<n}\ \prod_{\substack{j<n\\ j\ne k}}a_j\] is a positive integer as well. From \(x_n=1/a_n+x_{n+1}\), \[C_{n+1}=D_{n+1}x_{n+1}=a_nD_n\Bigl(x_n-\frac1{a_n}\Bigr)=a_nC_n-D_n, \qquad D_{n+1}=a_nD_n,\] which is \(\eqref{long243:eq:cubicorbit}\). Section \(\ref{long243:sec:state}\) constructs the same triple with its centred error \(E_n=D_n-(a_n-1)C_n\), which the present section does not need. Rationality is used here and nowhere else below.

Proof. Put \(F_n=\Gamma(n+\lambda)/\Gamma(n)\), so that \(F_{n+1}/F_n=1+\lambda/n\) exactly and \(F_n\sim n^\lambda\). Write \(\varepsilon_n=o(n^{-\lambda})\) for the error in \(\eqref{long243:eq:regularrate}\) and \(z_n=C_n/F_n\), so that \(z_{n+1}/z_n=1+\varepsilon_n/(1+\lambda/n)\). Since \(\lambda>1\) the errors are absolutely summable, so the product converges and \(z_n\to K\) for some \(K>0\), with \(z_n-K=o(n^{1-\lambda})\) by estimating the tail of the convergent logarithmic product. For \(F\), the identity \(\Gamma(n+1+\lambda)/\Gamma(n+1)-\Gamma(n+\lambda)/\Gamma(n) =\lambda\,\Gamma(n+\lambda)/\Gamma(n+1)\) iterates to \[\Delta^jF_n=\lambda(\lambda-1)\cdots(\lambda-j+1)\, \frac{\Gamma(n+\lambda)}{\Gamma(n+j)},\] whose right side is \(O(n^{\lambda-j})\). Choose an integer \(j>\lambda\); then \(\Delta^jF_n\to0\), so \(\Delta^jC_n\to0\). Position.

Theorem \(\ref{long243:res:cubicrate}\) carries no rationality hypothesis and no state hypothesis, and it is the only unconditional irrationality statement in this note. Its rate class lies inside the growth hypothesis of Problem \(\ref{long243:res:problem}\), because \(a_n^2/a_{n+1}=1+3/n+o(n^{-3})\) forces \(a_{n+1}/a_n^2\to1\). On that class the assertion of Problem \(\ref{long243:res:problem}\) therefore holds, by exclusion of a rational reciprocal sum, and the recurrence is never exhibited; Sylvester’s own sequence, whose ratio \(a_n^2/a_{n+1}\) is \(1+O(1/a_n)\), lies outside the class. The rate \(1+3/n\) sits strictly beyond the two sufficient hypotheses recorded in Section \(\ref{long243:sec:priorwork}\): it fails Koizumi’s \(1+o(1/n)\) condition, and \(\sum_n\gamma_n\) diverges, so Duverney’s convergent-defect hypothesis does not apply. Theorem \(\ref{long243:res:cubicexclusion}\) is independent of any growth or positivity assumption on \((a_n)\) beyond \(\eqref{long243:eq:cubicorbit}\), and the later sections of this note decide nothing at the cubic rate.

Prior work, and the status of the note

Two published sufficient conditions.

Two published results reach the original problem under analytic hypotheses that are assumed nowhere in this note. Their hypotheses are conditions on the sequence \((a_n)\) itself. Duverney’s is not comparable with the state-level hypotheses used here; the Erdős–Straus one, read through the dictionary of the next paragraph, is implied by nonnegativity of \(E\), the hypothesis of Theorem \(\ref{long243:res:descent}\), so that criterion already covers the descent of Section \(\ref{long243:sec:descent}\) (see ). Erdős and Straus proved that if \(\lim a_n/a_{n-1}^{2}=1\), the reciprocal sum is rational, and \(a_n\) does not satisfy the recurrence, then \[\limsup_{n\to\infty}\ \frac{[a_1,\ldots,a_n]}{a_{n+1}} \left(\frac{a_{n+1}^{2}}{a_{n+2}}-1\right)>0,\] where \([a_1,\ldots,a_n]\) is the least common multiple [1]. This is the indexing in the original theorem. Erdős’s 1988 survey, followed by the current catalogue summary, instead prints \(a_n^2/a_{n+1}-1\) in the second factor while retaining the same prefix-LCM quotient; that displayed summary is off by one and is not followed here [5][8]. Koizumi records the convenient sufficient rate \(a_n^2/a_{n+1}=1+o(1/n)\) under which the Erdős–Straus criterion settles the problem [7].

Duverney proved a conditional signed form of the problem itself: for signs \(\epsilon_n\in\{-1,1\}\), if \(\sum_{n\ge0}(a_{n+1}/a_n^{2}-1)\) converges, then the reciprocal sum with numerators \(\epsilon_n\) is rational if and only if \[a_{n+1}=a_n^2-(\epsilon_{n+1}/\epsilon_n)a_n +\epsilon_{n+2}/\epsilon_{n+1}\] for all large \(n\) [2]. Absolute convergence is a stronger sufficient specialisation, not the hypothesis printed in that corollary. The all-positive specialisation is the form relevant here.

Badea’s positive-term criterion is adjacent but different: for positive rational terms \(b_n/a_n\), eventual strict inequality \[a_{n+1}>\frac{b_{n+1}}{b_n}a_n^2-\frac{b_{n+1}}{b_n}a_n+1\] forces irrationality, while rationality under the corresponding non-strict inequality forces eventual equality [3]. These positive-term hypotheses do not cover the mixed-sign state orbits studied here. Neither result is formalised here, and the results below do not recover either of them.

Koizumi’s work on doubly exponential sequences [7] bears directly on the state system used below, and supplies the coordinates in which several of the statements of this note are most naturally read. For a rational \(r=p/q\) with pseudo-greedy expansion \((a_n)\), remainders \((x_n)\) and gap sequence \(\varepsilon_n=x_n^{-1}+1-a_n\), his Lemma 4  [7] produces integers \(c_n>0\), \(d_n\) and \(e_n\) with \(x_n=c_n/d_n\) and \(\varepsilon_n=e_n/c_n\), satisfying \[a_n=\frac{d_n-e_n}{c_n}+1,\qquad c_{n+1}=c_n-e_n,\qquad d_{n+1}=a_nd_n ,\] and the proof of that lemma also gives \(c_{n+1}=a_nc_n-d_n\). The growth hypothesis in Problem \(\ref{long243:res:problem}\) is calibrated by two facts about Sylvester’s sequence. It satisfies \(a_n\approx c_0^{2^{n}}\) with \(c_0=1.2640847\ldots\), and shifting it further produces sequences with \(a_n\approx C^{2^{n}}\) for arbitrarily large \(C\) whose reciprocals still sum to a rational number [6]. Problem #243 is open. The negative case has narrowed, and it is worth being precise about what is left.

Each exclusion holds only in its stated regime. Global height and old-factor overlap

The main arithmetic gap is global. Define the cumulative digit LCM and its overlap quotient by \[L_0=D_0,\qquad L_{n+1}=\lcm(L_n,a_n),\qquad M_n=\frac{D_n}{L_n}.\] Since \(D_n=D_0\prod_{j<n}a_j\), the quotient is integral and \(M_nL_n=D_n\). It records the multiplicity lost when the full product scale is compressed to an LCM.

The direct analytic question

An affirmative answer closes Problem #243 by Theorem \(\ref{long243:res:mass}\). Since \(\delta_n\to0\), convergence is equivalently expressible as boundedness of the partial products \(\prod_{n<N}(1+\delta_n)\); this is the same criterion in multiplicative form. A negative answer requires a sequence satisfying the full growth and rationality hypotheses. A locally admissible state orbit is insufficient.
