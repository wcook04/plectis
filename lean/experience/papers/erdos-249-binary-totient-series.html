A basis and all its relations

We write φ\varphi for Euler’s totient function and use ℕ={0,1,2,…}\mathbb{N}=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}, with φ(0)=0\varphi(0)=0. For k≥2k\ge2, the kk-kernel consists of the sequences n↦φ(kjn+r)n\mapsto\varphi(k^jn+r) with j≥0j\ge0 and 0≤r<kj0\le r<k^j. Some of these sections are scalar multiples of others: for example, φ(4n+2)=φ(2n+1)\varphi(4n+2)=\varphi(2n+1) and φ(4n)=2φ(2n)\varphi(4n)=2\varphi(2n). Thus the seven sections through level two in base two are spanned by n↦φ(n),φ(2n),φ(2n+1),φ(4n+1),φ(4n+3).n\longmapsto\varphi(n),\quad\varphi(2n),\quad\varphi(2n+1),\quad \varphi(4n+1),\quad\varphi(4n+3). Are these five sequences independent, and do the analogous reductions account for every relation in every base? The next theorem answers both questions; Corollary 1.3 gives the integral relation module.

An integer sequence is kk-regular if the ℤ\mathbb{Z}-module generated by its kk-kernel is finitely generated [1]. Coons proved that the totient sequence is not kk-regular for any k≥2k\ge2 . Martin’s Theorem 1 already implies the affine independence used below [4]. The theorem below identifies the finite-level basis and all integral relations.

Let k≥2k\ge2 and e≥1e\ge1 be integers, write Fj,r(k)(n)=φ(kjn+r)F^{(k)}_{j,r}(n)=\varphi(k^jn+r), and put Vk,e=span⁡ℚ{Fj,r(k):0≤j≤e,0≤r<kj}.V_{k,e}=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{Q}} \{\,F^{(k)}_{j,r}:0\le j\le e,\ 0\le r<k^j\,\}. Proof of the basis theorem

The assertion concerns the integer combinations of the original sections, not every integer-valued sequence in their rational span. The generated module is therefore not saturated in the group of integer-valued sequences: an integer-valued sequence outside the module can have a nonzero integer multiple inside it.

Relations with periodic coefficients

Pairwise nonproportionality is essential here. For instance, n+1n+1 and n+2n+2 satisfy the hypothesis. In contrast, 4n+24n+2 and 2n+12n+1 are proportional, and φ(4n+2)=φ(2n+1)\varphi(4n+2)=\varphi(2n+1) gives a nontrivial relation. Periodicity imposes no bound on the common period: it permits any finite periodic pattern, because one can work on each residue class separately.

Corollary 1.4 (Periodic coefficients). Let L1,…,LsL_1,\ldots,L_s be pairwise nonproportional affine forms with integer coefficients and positive slopes. Proof. Write Li(n)=ain+biL_i(n)=a_i n+b_i and choose a common positive period QQ for the wiw_i. On a progression n=r+Qtn=r+Qt, with 0≤r<Q0\le r<Q, their values are the constants wi(r)w_i(r). The restricted affine forms have cross determinants Q(aibj−ajbi)≠0Q(a_i b_j-a_j b_i)\ne0. Shifting tt beyond the stated threshold preserves these determinants and makes all arguments positive. Applied on each progression, Martin’s Theorem 1 gives the same conclusion [4]. The same argument preserves the basis after deletion of a finite prefix. For the two sections with residue zero, large n≡1(mod⁡k)n\equiv1\pmod k and large powers of kk replace the two evaluations used above.

Scope.

This completes the basis argument. It describes relations among coefficient subsequences, not the arithmetic nature of S=∑n≥1φ(n)2−nS=\sum_{n\ge1}\varphi(n)2^{-n}.

Supplementary results on the binary totient series

The independent results below distinguish bounded-residue series, finite exclusions for SS, and conditions for irrationality. None is needed for Theorem 1.1; in particular, the conditions in Sections 3 and 5 are not assumptions of that theorem. Generating-function context and the formal-source guide are in Appendix A.

Bounded residues and rationality

After reducing φ(n)\varphi(n) modulo a fixed integer, the coefficients are bounded. A single nonzero coefficient between long zero blocks then contradicts rationality by an elementary tail estimate.

Theorem 2.1 (Residue series and dyadic observables). For every m≥3m\ge3, ∑n≥1φ(n)mod⁡m2n∉ℚ.\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{\varphi(n)\operatorname{mod} m}{2^n}\notin\mathbb{Q}. For k≥1k\ge1 and f:ℤ/2kℤ→ℚf:\mathbb{Z}/2^k\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Q}, the series ∑n≥1f(φ(n)mod⁡2k)2−n\sum_{n\ge1}f(\varphi(n)\operatorname{mod}2^k)2^{-n} is rational exactly when ff is constant on the even residue classes. If that constant is cc, its value is 3f(1)/4+c/43f(1)/4+c/4.

The restriction m≥3m\ge3 is necessary: modulo 22 the only nonzero coefficients occur at n=1,2n=1,2, and the sum is 1/2+1/4=3/41/2+1/4=3/4. For the second assertion, only f(1)f(1) and its values on the even classes can affect the sum. The other odd classes are never attained by φ(n)\varphi(n).

The next lemma applies, for example, to the indicator of the squares: its coefficients are bounded, and sufficiently large squares have no other square within any prescribed distance. An eventually periodic sequence with infinitely many nonzero terms cannot satisfy the isolated-coefficient hypothesis. Most importantly, the bound CC must be fixed before the zero blocks are chosen; the unbounded coefficients φ(n)\varphi(n) do not satisfy this hypothesis.

Further context and formal sources

Affine independence in the earlier literature

The separation method has an earlier source: Yazdani’s proof of Theorem 2, explicitly credited there to Shallit, uses the Chinese remainder theorem and Dirichlet’s theorem to distinguish kernel sections modulo a fixed modulus [10]. For a later account see Allouche, Shallit and Yassawi [11]. Here one separates a chosen column from every other column of a finite evaluation matrix. Nonsingularity, not merely distinctness of infinitely many sections, gives linear independence.

Martin’s Theorem 1 gives another proof of the affine independence conclusion [4]. For any ordering of the nonproportional forms and any C>0C>0, their successive totient ratios exceed CC on a set of positive lower density. In a proposed relation ∑iciφ(Li(n))=0\sum_i c_i\varphi(L_i(n))=0, put a nonzero coefficient first and choose C>max⁡(1,∑i>1|ci|/|c1|)C>\max(1,\sum_{i>1}|c_i|/|c_1|). At the resulting values of nn, the first term has larger absolute value than the sum of all the others, a contradiction. The proof in Section 1.1 instead constructs a finite nonsingularity certificate for each chosen family.

Regularity, Mahler equations and section maps

The totient is not kk-regular, in the sense of Allouche and Shallit [1], for any k≥2k\ge2: Coons proved that the ℤ\mathbb{Z}-module generated by its kk-kernel is not finitely generated, by counting the poles of ζ(s−1)/ζ(s)\zeta(s-1)/\zeta(s) in the strip 0≤Re⁡s≤10\le\operatorname{Re}s\le1 . For integer-valued sequences this is equivalent to infinite dimension of the rational span: if the span has dimension dd, some dd evaluations map it injectively to ℚd\mathbb{Q}^d and the module into ℤd\mathbb{Z}^d (compare the evaluation argument in [3]). Theorem 1.1 therefore recovers Coons’s nonregularity theorem [2] and gives the dimension at every finite level.

The generating-function conclusion concerns functional equations, not just the dimensions of section spans. A kk-Mahler equation for a formal power series FF has the form ∑i=0dPi(z)F(zki)=0,d≥0,Pi(z)∈ℚ[z],P0≠0.\sum_{i=0}^{d}P_i(z)F(z^{k^i})=0, \qquad d\ge0,\quad P_i(z)\in\mathbb{Q}[z],\quad P_0\ne0. The height criterion of Adamczewski, Bell and Smertnig already implies that an integer-coefficient kk-Mahler series with polynomially bounded coefficients is kk-regular . Indeed, the logarithmic height of an integer aa is log⁡max⁡(1,|a|)\log\max(1,|a|). Since 0≤φ(n)≤n0\le\varphi(n)\le n, nonregularity rules out such an equation for Fφ(z)=∑n≥1φ(n)znF_\varphi(z)=\sum_{n\ge1}\varphi(n)z^n in every base k≥2k\ge2; no multiplicativity hypothesis is needed for this deduction. The multiplicative classification in Bell and Smertnig’s later preprint also gives this conclusion [12]. Bell, Bruin and Coons give related classifications for algebraic or DD-finite generating functions [13]; DD-finite means satisfying a linear differential equation with polynomial coefficients. These theorems constrain the whole generating function. They do not by themselves determine the arithmetic nature of its single value Fφ(1/2)F_\varphi(1/2).

For example, the next dyadic level has rank 99 rather than 1515: φ(8n+2)=φ(4n+1)\varphi(8n+2)=\varphi(4n+1), φ(8n+4)=2φ(2n+1)\varphi(8n+4)=2\varphi(2n+1) and φ(8n+6)=φ(4n+3)\varphi(8n+6)=\varphi(4n+3) are its positive-residue reductions.

For a kk-regular sequence that is not eventually zero, its growth exponent is the base-kk logarithm of the joint spectral radius of the section matrices in a basis of the kernel span. A redundant spanning family gives only an upper bound [3]. This finite-dimensional theorem does not apply to φ\varphi. Its section maps nevertheless have the explicit form below.
