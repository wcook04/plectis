Introduction

Splitting the totient sequence into its even and odd subsequences, then splitting once more, gives seven sequences: φ(n);φ(2n),φ(2n+1);φ(4n),φ(4n+1),φ(4n+2),φ(4n+3).\varphi(n);\qquad \varphi(2n),\ \varphi(2n+1);\qquad \varphi(4n),\ \varphi(4n+1),\ \varphi(4n+2),\ \varphi(4n+3). Two identities remove two of them: φ(4n)=2φ(2n),φ(4n+2)=φ(2n+1).\varphi(4n)=2\varphi(2n),\qquad \varphi(4n+2)=\varphi(2n+1). The five remaining sequences are linearly independent over ℚ\mathbb{Q}. Thus every rational linear identity among the seven follows from these two substitutions. If the original coefficients are integers, so are the coefficients obtained after substitution.

The same description holds at every finite depth and in every base. For an integer k≥2k\ge2, the totient sections are the sequences n↦φ(kjn+r)n\mapsto\varphi(k^jn+r), where j≥0j\ge0 and 0≤r<kj0\le r<k^j. Throughout, nn ranges over ℕ={0,1,2,…}\mathbb{N}=\{0,1,2,\ldots\} and φ(0)=0\varphi(0)=0. To describe relations we retain a separate index for each pair (j,r)(j,r), even when two pairs give the same sequence. For example, φ(4n+2)\varphi(4n+2) and φ(2n+1)\varphi(2n+1) are equal sequences but occupy different positions in the list above.

The sections form the kk-kernel of the totient sequence. A sequence is kk-regular when the ℤ\mathbb{Z}-module generated by its kk-kernel is finitely generated [1]. Coons proved that the totient sequence is not kk-regular for any k≥2k\ge2 . The theorem below gives more explicit information: which sections to retain, how to reduce each of the others, and hence how to test a linear identity. The level-zero family contains only φ(n)\varphi(n) and has dimension one.

Theorem 1.1 (A basis through each finite level). Let k≥2k\ge2 and e≥1e\ge1 be integers, write Fj,r(k)(n)=φ(kjn+r)F^{(k)}_{j,r}(n)=\varphi(k^jn+r), and put Vk,e=span⁡ℚ{Fj,r(k):0≤j≤e,0≤r<kj}.V_{k,e}=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{Q}} \{\,F^{(k)}_{j,r}:0\le j\le e,\ 0\le r<k^j\,\}. Every omitted section reduces to a basis element by an explicit scalar: Fj,0(k)=kj−1F1,0(k)F^{(k)}_{j,0}=k^{j-1}F^{(k)}_{1,0} for j≥1j\ge1, and if r=ktur=k^tu with t=max⁡{s:ks∣r}≥1t=\max\{s:k^s\mid r\}\ge1 and k∤uk\nmid u, then Fj,r(k)=Ck(t,u)Fj−t,u(k),Ck(t,u)=kt∏p∣kp∤u(1−1p).F^{(k)}_{j,r}=C_k(t,u)\,F^{(k)}_{j-t,u}, \qquad C_k(t,u)=k^t\prod_{\substack{p\mid k\\ p\nmid u}}\Bigl(1-\tfrac1p\Bigr).

The condition k∤rk\nmid r allows residues with gcd⁡(k,r)>1\gcd(k,r)>1. For instance, r=1r=1 and r=2r=2 are both retained in base six, and the reductions involving them are φ(36n+6)=2φ(6n+1),φ(36n+12)=4φ(6n+2).\varphi(36n+6)=2\varphi(6n+1),\qquad \varphi(36n+12)=4\varphi(6n+2). The second multiplier includes the factor 22 already present in 6n+26n+2. Repeated prime factors of kk cause no further exception to the product formula, so the theorem also applies to nonsquarefree bases.

Martin’s 2006 theorem on simultaneous totient inequalities already implies independence for every finite family of pairwise nonproportional affine forms with positive integral slopes [4]. Its conclusion is stronger than the independence used here. The point of the present description is the explicit reduction of the sections and the integral relation basis in Corollary 3.1. We also give a direct proof that constructs a nonsingular evaluation matrix. It uses the CRT–Dirichlet separation argument in Yazdani’s proof of Theorem 2, credited there to Shallit [12]. The step that needs care is to keep the progression for the selected affine form reduced while imposing prime divisors on all the others.

Section 2 proves the basis theorem. Section 3 starts with the six reductions in base six and derives the integral relation basis. In Section 4 we use prime isolation again, for bounded functions of totient residues. The series S=∑n≥1φ(n)2nS=\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{\varphi(n)}{2^n} has unbounded coefficients. Its irrationality, asked in Erdős Problem #249 [10], remains open. The coefficient basis supplies no irrationality conclusion for this value. Appendix A gives further coefficient consequences; Appendices B and C collect the tail identities, comparison sequences and still-unproved cancellation estimates relevant to SS. Their longer proofs are in The Binary Totient Series.

Proof of the basis theorem

Scalar reductions

Multiplication by a power of kk changes a totient by a predictable factor once we know which primes dividing kk are already present. For a zero residue, knkn and kjnk^jn have the same prime divisors when n>0n>0. Hence φ(kjn)=kj−1φ(kn)\varphi(k^jn)=k^{j-1}\varphi(kn); both sides vanish at n=0n=0. For an omitted positive residue r=ktur=k^tu with t≥1t\ge1 and k∤uk\nmid u, we have t<jt<j because 0<r<kj0<r<k^j. Put m=kj−tn+um=k^{j-t}n+u. A prime p∣kp\mid k divides mm exactly when it divides uu. The product formula for φ(ktm)/φ(m)\varphi(k^tm)/\varphi(m) therefore contributes ktk^t and one factor 1−1/p1-1/p for each prime p∣kp\mid k absent from uu. This is Ck(t,u)C_k(t,u), and proves spanning.

Affine independence

For independence, Martin’s theorem is enough. For any ordering of pairwise nonproportional affine forms and any C>0C>0, it gives a set of positive lower density on which each successive totient ratio exceeds CC. The two zero-residue sections

It remains to separate φ(n)\varphi(n) and φ(kn)\varphi(kn), whose affine arguments are proportional. Bounded functions of totient residues

The prime-isolation construction has a second use. It can place a nonzero totient residue between arbitrarily long blocks of zero residues. For a series with bounded integer coefficients, such a pattern forces irrationality: the right block makes a scaled tail small, and the left block isolates a single nonzero term. We first give the resulting classification, then the tail argument. Residues in the following sum are their least nonnegative representatives; they are coefficients of a binary series and need not be binary digits.

Theorem 4.1 (Residue series and dyadic observables). For every m≥3m\ge3, ∑n≥1φ(n)mod⁡m2n∉ℚ.\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{\varphi(n)\operatorname{mod} m}{2^n}\notin\mathbb{Q}. For k≥1k\ge1 and f:ℤ/2kℤ→ℚf:\mathbb{Z}/2^k\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Q}, the series ∑n≥1f(φ(n)mod⁡2k)2−n\sum_{n\ge1}f(\varphi(n)\operatorname{mod}2^k)2^{-n} is rational exactly when ff is constant on the even residue classes. If that constant is cc, its value is 3f(1)/4+c/43f(1)/4+c/4.

Further consequences for the sections

The finite-level theorem also describes the full dyadic kernel. The notation below specialises the base to two.

Corollary A.1 (Dyadic basis). The family {φ0,0,φ1,0}∪{φj,r:j≥1,0<r<2j,r odd},φj,r(n)=φ(2jn+r),\{\varphi_{0,0},\varphi_{1,0}\} \cup\{\varphi_{j,r}:j\ge1,\ 0<r<2^j,\ r\text{ odd}\}, \qquad \varphi_{j,r}(n)=\varphi(2^jn+r), is a basis for the rational span of the full dyadic kernel. Proof. Each finite subset of this family is contained in some ℬ2,e\mathcal B_{2,e}. Theorem 1.1 gives independence, and its reductions give spanning. At level zero the sole section φ(n)\varphi(n) is nonzero, since φ(1)=1\varphi(1)=1. ◻

The affine argument also permits periodic coefficients by restriction to their common residue classes.

Proportional forms are excluded for a reason: the identity φ(4n+2)=φ(2n+1)\varphi(4n+2)=\varphi(2n+1) would otherwise contradict the conclusion. Martin’s theorem gives the same corollary on each progression [4].

Coons’s proof of nonregularity counts the poles of ζ(s−1)/ζ(s)\zeta(s-1)/\zeta(s) . For an integer-valued sequence, finite dimension of the rational kernel span is equivalent to finite generation of its integral kernel module: choose finitely many evaluations that embed the span in ℚd\mathbb{Q}^d; the module embeds in ℤd\mathbb{Z}^d, whose subgroups are finitely generated (compare [3]). Theorem 1.1 recovers this nonregularity and gives the ranks of its finite truncations. For an account of the earlier separation method, see Allouche, Shallit and Yassawi .

The binary totient series

The proof of Theorem 4.1 uses boundedness where a long zero block makes the tail small. Comparison sequences

Large ranks alone cannot settle the series question. There is an integer sequence cc with 0≤c(n)≤n0\le c(n)\le n, unchanged odd totients and |c(n)−φ(n)|≤2|c(n)-\varphi(n)|\le2 at even indices, for which ∑c(n)2−n=5/4\sum c(n)2^{-n}=5/4. Its integral carry still has dyadic rank at least 2e−12^e-1 through every positive level. The construction and its limitations explain why it fails the exact prime-dilation laws of φ\varphi. The same section retains the cofinal LCM-diagonal criterion, its endpoint sign conditions and the limits of finite integer combinations of shifts.

The ordinary signed interpolation argument permits any value in an interval about ∑φ(n)β−n\sum\varphi(n)\beta^{-n}, for each integer β≥2\beta\ge2, while preserving a prescribed prefix and every odd coefficient, with errors bounded by any positive f(n)→∞f(n)\to\infty. The changed even indices have upper Banach density zero. On each fixed progression the Dirichlet defect is entire; at roots of unity the generating-function defect has smooth radial data and any prescribed finite jets vanish. This argument has not been formalised in Lean. It preserves Dirichlet singularities; regular parts and zeros may change, and finite jets do not determine an entire radial germ. It therefore supplies no cancellation estimate for the unchanged totient.

Rational approximation imposes a different requirement. The identity Θ2:=∑d≥1μ(d)(2d−1)2=S−12\Theta_2:=\sum_{d\ge1}\frac{\mu(d)}{(2^d-1)^2}=S-\frac12 was posted by Amiram Eldar, together with its coprimality interpretation, on 15 March 2026 [8], and by Steve Fan on 16 May 2026 [9]. An exponential-sum question

For every h≥1h\ge1 and every X0X_0, suppose that some integers X≥max⁡(X0,1)X\ge\max(X_0,1) and L≥hL\ge h satisfy 16(2X+h+L+2)≤2L,Re⁡∑N=X2X−1exp⁡(2πiDh,N,L/2L)≤910X.\begin{equation} \label{eq:r3-harmonic} 16(2X+h+L+2)\le2^L,\qquad \operatorname{Re}\sum_{N=X}^{2X-1} \exp(2\pi iD_{h,N,L}/2^L)\le\frac9{10}X. \end{equation} These inequalities would prove irrationality. Indeed, if every residue failed [eq:r3-certificate], each phase would be within 1/161/16 of an integer, so every real part would be at least cos⁡(π/8)>9/10\cos(\pi/8)>9/10. A certificate must therefore occur on the block, and [eq:tail-phase] excludes every rational denominator by choosing its period and then a sufficiently late block.

To separate the counting estimates from the cancellation estimate, we single out one totient in each window. Problem C.1 (Four estimates for the first-harmonic sum). For each h≥1h\ge1, do there exist s≥1s\ge1 and 0<η<10<\eta<1 such that, for every X0X_0, there are X,LX,L with max⁡(X0,1)≤X,h≤L−s,16(2X+h+L+2)≤2L,\max(X_0,1)\le X,\qquad h\le L-s,\qquad 16(2X+h+L+2)\le2^L, for which those four bounds hold?

All four estimates must hold on the same block with the same parameters. For each fixed h,sh,s, take the least admissible LL satisfying the room inequality and L≥s+hL\ge s+h. The smooth-number and small-cofactor estimates give the last two bounds for all sufficiently large XX when 0<η<1/(300log⁡4)0<\eta<1/(300\log4). The elementary excluded-cofactor estimate is (3ηlog⁡4+o(1))X(3\eta\log4+o(1))X; it needs no prime number theorem. The group-mean and weighted mean-subtracted estimates remain open. No optimality is asserted for the sufficient margins in [eq:r3-four-bounds].

Alternatively, at s=26s=26, η=1/1000\eta=1/1000 and minimal depth one has |𝒢|>67X/100|\mathcal G|>67X/100 for large XX. The ordinary comparison argument gives larger first harmonics at all sufficiently large admissible scales, distinguishing its rational sequences from the cancellation required of φ\varphi. Its proof is included with the interpolation construction.

Balasubramanian, Giri and Srivastav give correlation estimates applicable to φ(n)/n\varphi(n)/n, uniformly for |h|≤x/2|h|\le x/2 . Here the modulus grows with LL, and the residual weight depends on other totient values in the window. That correlation theorem supplies neither of the two remaining estimates. Establishing them for the actual totient is the unresolved arithmetic step.
