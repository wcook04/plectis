Two sums from running least common multiples

The 55-smooth integers are the positive integers whose prime factors belong to {2,3,5}\{2,3,5\}. Their running least common multiples begin 1,2,6,12,60,60,120,360,360,…1,2,6,12,60,60,120,360,360,\ldots. For a finite set of primes PP, let 𝒮P\mathcal S_P consist of 11 and all positive integers whose prime factors belong to PP, and put L⁡(x)=lcm⁡{u∈𝒮P:u≤x},H⁡P(x)=∏p∈Pp⌊log⁡px⌋(x≥1).\operatorname{L}(x)=\operatorname{lcm}\{u\in\mathcal S_P:u\le x\},\qquad \operatorname{H}_P(x)=\prod_{p\in P}p^{\lfloor\log_p x\rfloor}\quad(x\ge1). Every integer in this finite set divides H⁡P(x)\operatorname{H}_P(x). Conversely, the set contains the largest power of each p∈Pp\in P below or equal to xx, so their product divides its least common multiple. Thus L⁡(x)=H⁡P(x)\operatorname{L}(x)=\operatorname{H}_P(x). This value changes only at a positive prime power, where it is multiplied by that prime. Summing its distinct reciprocals gives 𝒟P=1+∑t=pnp∈P,n≥11H⁡P(t).\mathcal D_P=1+\sum_{\substack{t=p^n\\p\in P,\ n\ge1}}\frac1{\operatorname{H}_P(t)}. Positive powers of distinct primes never coincide, and geometric growth of the successive running LCMs gives convergence. We use ℕ={0,1,2,…}\mathbb{N}=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}.

Theorem 1.1 (the sum over distinct running LCMs for {2,3,5}\{2,3,5\}). The number 𝒟{2,3,5}\mathcal D_{\{2,3,5\}} is irrational.

If the sum were rational, its normalised tails would take only finitely many values. We show that each such value determines both the next block of jumps and the following tail. Repetition of a tail would then force the blocks to be eventually periodic, contrary to the irrational frequency of powers of 33. To recover a block from its tail, we use five affine maps whose images are separated by a bound obtained from the spacing of powers of 55.

Erdős asserted irrationality of 𝒟P\mathcal D_P for every finite PP with |P|≥2|P|\ge2 in a letter dated 1 January 1973, without printing a proof [3]. Thus the theorem above is a special case of that earlier assertion. We claim no priority for its conclusion. The companion’s general argument uses an isolated interchange of jumps on a torus. That argument is retained there as an ordinary proof awaiting formalisation. The proof below is independent of that argument.

The question in Erdős Problem #269 concerns the sum with multiplicity, ℛP=∑u∈𝒮P1L⁡(u),\mathcal R_P=\sum_{u\in\mathcal S_P}\frac1{\operatorname{L}(u)}, as recorded in [1] and [2]. For example, L⁡(5)=L⁡(6)=60\operatorname{L}(5)=\operatorname{L}(6)=60 for P={2,3,5}P=\{2,3,5\}, giving 2/602/60 in ℛP\mathcal R_P and 1/601/60 in 𝒟P\mathcal D_P. The theorem treats the sum over distinct values; the problem asks about the sum with multiplicity. Our arguments leave the latter unresolved for finite PP with |P|≥3|P|\ge3. For P={p}P=\{p\} both sums equal p/(p−1)p/(p-1).

Section 2 proves the theorem. We then retain the multiplicities and examine what changes: the tails satisfy an integer recurrence, but their values are unbounded. Sections 3 and 4 derive that recurrence and a residue criterion for the repeated sum. For two primes, Fan’s factorisation [7] reduces both sums to a Hecke–Mahler value and yields transcendence (Section 5). Appendix A collects the elementary running LCM identities, and Appendix B explains why the three-prime kernel has no finite separated factorisation.

Recovering the prime-power jumps

For a rational number, long division has only finitely many possible remainders, and each remainder determines the next digit and remainder. The same two facts will prove useful here. Rationality bounds the number of tail values; the main work is to show that a tail determines the next block of prime-power jumps.

As hh grows, the interval above narrows towards the fixed value BXℓBX_\ell. If that value is nonintegral, a sufficiently narrow interval contains no integer. The upper-bound property of KK is essential: choosing the zero function would make every residue inequality automatic. The companion’s residue analysis treats smaller bounds and identifies when more general bounds give the same criterion. Finite searches cover only finitely many multipliers and starts.

The repeated three-prime question can therefore be written in three equivalent forms.

Problem 4.3 (the repeated three-prime target). Prove that S=ℛ{2,3,5}S=\mathcal R_{\{2,3,5\}} is irrational.

Problem 4.4 (no reduced tail is an integer). Prove that for every a≥1a\ge1 and every integer B≥1B\ge1 coprime to 3030, BXa∉ℤ.\begin{equation} \label{eq:tail-nonintegrality} BX_a\notin\mathbb Z. \end{equation}

Problem 4.5 (residue inequalities after every starting index). Prove [eq:escape].

An integral BXaBX_a makes SS rational by [eq:prefix-lattice]; Theorem 3.3 proves the converse implication. Theorem 4.2 gives the third form. All three remain unproved for SS. Two-prime transcendence

For two primes we can retain the multiplicities and still evaluate the sum through one Hecke–Mahler value. Fan gave the factorisation of the repeated sum and its transcendence consequence in his post of 26 June 2026 [7]. The following calculation also expresses the sum over distinct running LCMs in that value.

Identities for the running LCM and multiplicities

Let p,q,rp,q,r be pairwise distinct primes. Write H⁡=H⁡{p,q,r}\operatorname{H}=\operatorname{H}_{\{p,q,r\}} and K⁡(i,j,k)=H⁡(piqjrk)−1\operatorname{K}(i,j,k)=\operatorname{H}(p^iq^jr^k)^{-1} throughout this appendix and the next. The following identities underlie the grouping of the repeated series in Section 3.

Proposition A.1 (the running least common multiple). Let p,q,rp,q,r be pairwise distinct primes and x≥1x\ge1. Then the running least common multiple is given by L⁡(x)=H⁡(x)\operatorname{L}(x)=\operatorname{H}(x).

Proof. We have u∣H⁡(x)u\mid\operatorname{H}(x) for every supported u≤xu\le x. For the reverse divisibility, take the maximal powers of p,q,rp,q,r below xx: they belong to the prefix and are pairwise coprime, so their product divides its LCM. ◻

Proposition A.2 (cells and jumps). The running LCM is constant when the three integer logarithms are constant. A jump in exactly one logarithm multiplies it by the corresponding prime. The first nn positive powers of each prime, together with 11, form 3n+13n+1 distinct points.

Proof. The running LCM is a product of the three maximal pure powers, which proves the constancy and jump assertions. Unique factorisation separates the 3n3n positive pure powers from one another and from 11. ◻

The three-prime kernel

We ask whether the two-prime factorisation can be replaced, for three primes, by a finite sum of products separating one exponent from the other two. The kernel K⁡(i,j,k)=H⁡(piqjrk)−1\operatorname{K}(i,j,k)=\operatorname{H}(p^iq^jr^k)^{-1} rules this out. Diagonal rescaling reduces the question to a matrix with entries 11 and 1/r1/r; separate choices of row and column indices then give nonzero minors of every order.

Example B.1. For (p,q,r)=(2,3,5)(p,q,r)=(2,3,5), the leading two-by-two determinant is −1/15-1/15: its four entries are 1,1/6,1/2,1/601,1/6,1/2,1/60, so it equals 1/60−1/121/60-1/12.

This nonzero two-by-two minor rules out rank one. More generally, a sum of dd separated products would give every matrix layer rank at most dd. To exclude every finite dd, we therefore seek nonzero minors of arbitrarily large order. Their rows and columns may depend on the order; the same choices will work in every layer kk.

Approximation.

Truncating the original kernel to i<Ni<N gives a sum of NN separated terms. The bound H⁡(x)>x3/(pqr)\operatorname{H}(x)>x^3/(pqr) makes the truncation error tend to zero both uniformly and in ℓ1(ℕ3)\ell^1(\mathbb N^3). The rescaled matrix in the rank proof has uniform distance (1−1/r)/2(1-1/r)/2 from the matrices of finite rank. Rescaling preserves exact rank but removes the decay responsible for the first approximation. The companion’s approximation theorem proves both assertions for their stated norms. Neither the rank theorem nor the approximation bound implies irrationality of the scalar sum.

Verification, reproducibility and sources

The five-map proof is in the distinct-value development and its block-series criterion. Its statement has no recorded Comparator comparison. The evidence record fixes the source versions; the named input for two-prime transcendence is stated beside that theorem.

The programs for sums over distinct values calculate the interval endpoints and finite-state examples using exact arithmetic; the proof in Section 2 derives its constants directly.

The companion’s account of this proof also compares one-jump and dyadic bounds. Its finite-state extension uses intervals depending on earlier blocks. The saved exact computations satisfy that criterion for every set of three primes at most 3131 and for 292292 of the 330330 four-prime sets in that range. The general criterion and those computations are recorded as remarks awaiting formalisation. For {2,3,5,7}\{2,3,5,7\} two admissible automaton words give the same affine map at every subsequent refinement, preventing that automaton from recovering its first block. This identity does not establish a collision between two words realised by the arithmetic sequence.

The companion’s source inventory also covers the ordinary general and subseries arguments. Its historical note records the 26 September 2026 AI check and the corrections made from it; independent human mathematical review has not been reported.

Further comparisons

The classical criteria of Erdős–Straus [9], Hančl–Tijdeman [10] and Diananda–Oppenheim [8] are compared with the bounded-base series in the companion. It also compares the bounded-ratio argument with Erdős–Taylor [11] and Fan [12], and the restrictions on integer recurrences with the polynomial Cantor-series results of Hančl–Tijdeman [13]. Koutsoukou-Argyraki and Li’s Isabelle development [19] formalises classical irrationality criteria. It does not treat the present repeated series.

For transcendence, the relevant comparisons are the finite-difference conditions of Luca–Ouaknine–Worrell [14], the echoing conditions in [15], the fixed-base complexity theorem of Adamczewski–Bugeaud [20], and multivariate Mahler theory [21]. Quadratic growth of mam_a supplies none of the missing cancellation or recurrence hypotheses. The companion exhibits nonzero boundary terms in a proposed third-difference calculation and distinguishes variable-base digits from fixed-base expansions. Work on fixed-prime semigroups [16][17] and other Ahmes-series problems [18] provides context; no estimate from those papers enters our dyadic counting argument.
