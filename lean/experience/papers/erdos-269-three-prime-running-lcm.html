Irrational Distinct-Height Sums for Finite Prime Sets

Erdős #269

Two sums from running least common multiples

The main result is the distinct-height irrationality theorem of Section 2. It applies to every finite prime set containing at least two primes. It does not settle the irrationality of the repeated-value series that motivated it. We introduce both sums here, keeping that distinction visible before the proof.

For a finite prime set PP, let 𝒮P\mathcal S_P consist of the positive integers whose prime factors lie in PP, including 11. We use ℕ={0,1,2,…}\mathbb{N}=\{0,1,2,\ldots\} for exponent indices. For x≥1x\ge1, put L⁡(x)=lcm⁡{u∈𝒮P:u≤x},ℛP=∑u∈𝒮PL⁡(u)−1.\operatorname{L}(x)=\operatorname{lcm}\{u\in\mathcal S_P:u\le x\},\qquad \mathcal R_P=\sum_{u\in\mathcal S_P}\operatorname{L}(u)^{-1}. Erdős asked whether ℛP\mathcal R_P is irrational when |P|≥2|P|\ge2 [1]. For P={p,q,r}P=\{p,q,r\}, write H⁡(x)=p⌊log⁡px⌋q⌊log⁡qx⌋r⌊log⁡rx⌋,K⁡(i,j,k)=H⁡(piqjrk)−1.\operatorname{H}(x)=p^{\lfloor\log_p x\rfloor}q^{\lfloor\log_q x\rfloor} r^{\lfloor\log_r x\rfloor},\qquad \operatorname{K}(i,j,k)=\operatorname{H}(p^iq^jr^k)^{-1}. Prime-power divisibility gives L⁡=H\operatorname{L}=\operatorname{H} (Proposition 5.1). The running LCM is therefore constant between consecutive prime powers pn,qn,rnp^n,q^n,r^n (n≥1n\ge1), and at each of them it is multiplied by the corresponding prime (Proposition 5.2). Counting each of its values once gives the distinct-height sum 𝒟P=1+∑tH⁡(t)−1,\mathcal D_P=1+\sum_{t}\operatorname{H}(t)^{-1}, where tt runs over the prime powers pn,qn,rnp^n,q^n,r^n with n≥1n\ge1. The two sums differ because the running LCM repeats: 55 and 66 both have running LCM 6060, which contributes 2/602/60 to ℛ{2,3,5}\mathcal R_{\{2,3,5\}} and 1/601/60 to 𝒟{2,3,5}\mathcal D_{\{2,3,5\}}. In a letter dated 1 January 1973, Erdős called the irrationality of ℛP\mathcal R_P a conjecture he could not prove and wrote that he could show the distinct-height sum to be irrational; the letter prints no argument [3]. Erdős and Graham state only the question for ℛP\mathcal R_P , and we have not found a proof in print (the companion’s Section 11.1 lists the sources searched). Section 2 proves the irrationality of 𝒟P\mathcal D_P for every finite PP with |P|≥2|P|\ge2.

Fan used the identity L⁡=H\operatorname{L}=\operatorname{H} to separate the two-prime kernel [7]. With three primes, even a finite sum of separated products is impossible (Section 3). That theorem is about the kernel and says nothing about the arithmetic of its sum. Sections 5 and 6 turn to the repeated {2,3,5}\{2,3,5\} sum and state exactly what its irrationality still requires.

Irrationality for finite prime sets

Let PP be a finite set of primes with |P|≥2|P|\ge2 and put H⁡P(t)=∏r∈Pr⌊log⁡rt⌋\operatorname{H}_P(t)=\prod_{r\in P}r^{\lfloor\log_rt\rfloor}. The proof of Proposition 5.1 applies to any finite set of primes: the running LCM of the PP-smooth integers up to tt is H⁡P(t)\operatorname{H}_P(t), and it changes only at the prime powers rnr^n (r∈Pr\in P, n≥1n\ge1), where it is multiplied by rr. List these prime powers as t1<t2<⋯t_1<t_2<\cdots, let qkq_k be the prime of tkt_k, and put Qk=q1⋯qk=H⁡P(tk)Q_k=q_1\cdots q_k=\operatorname{H}_P(t_k). Then 𝒟P=1+∑k≥11Qk.\mathcal D_P=1+\sum_{k\ge1}\frac1{Q_k}. For P={2,3,5}P=\{2,3,5\} the prime powers 2,3,4,5,8,9,16,25,27,32,…2,3,4,5,8,9,16,25,27,32,\ldots carry the heights 2,6,12,60,120,360,720,3600,10800,21600,…2,6,12,60,120,360,720,3600,10800,21600,\ldots, so 𝒟{2,3,5}=1.7796169832947490504…\mathcal D_{\{2,3,5\}}=1.7796169832947490504\ldots.

Theorem 2.1 (the distinct-height sums). For every finite set PP of primes with |P|≥2|P|\ge2, the number 𝒟P\mathcal D_P is irrational.

For |P|=2|P|=2 the theorem is contained in Theorem 4.1: 𝒟{p,q}\mathcal D_{\{p,q\}} is an affine function of one Hecke–Mahler value, which is transcendental by Loxton and van der Poorten [6] and Bugeaud and Laurent . For |P|≥3|P|\ge3 every Hecke–Mahler theorem we found has a single slope.

The proof compares two tails of the series. A rational value makes every scaled tail an integer, so two tails closer than the scale are equal. The tails after tkt_k and after tkpnt_kp^n, for a suitable nn, are that close. Their words of primes differ only by rearranging short runs, and equality of the tails forces an integer invariant ff to agree on each run and its rearrangement. A run of two primes violates this, and a point of a torus on exactly two coordinate walls produces one.

The torus.

Let 𝕋=∏q∈Pℝ/(log⁡q)ℤ\mathbb T=\prod_{q\in P}\mathbb{R}/(\log q)\mathbb{Z} and Θ(s)=(smod⁡log⁡q)q∈P\Theta(s)=(s\operatorname{mod}\log q)_{q\in P} for s∈ℝs\in\mathbb{R}. For θ∈𝕋\theta\in\mathbb T a qq-crossing is a real tt with θq+t∈(log⁡q)ℤ\theta_q+t\in(\log q)\mathbb{Z}, and W(θ)W(\theta) is the word of the primes of the positive crossings in increasing order of time. For θk=Θ(log⁡tk)\theta_k=\Theta(\log t_k) the positive qq-crossings are the times log⁡(qj/tk)\log(q^j/t_k) with qj>tkq^j>t_k, so W(θk)=qk+1qk+2⋯W(\theta_k)=q_{k+1}q_{k+2}\cdots, and crossings of different primes never coincide.

Let T0T_0 be the closure of Θ(ℝ)\Theta(\mathbb{R}), a subtorus containing every θk\theta_k. Every forward orbit {θ+Θ(t):t≥t0}\{\theta+\Theta(t):t\ge t_0\} with θ∈T0\theta\in T_0 is dense in T0T_0, by Kronecker’s theorem or a one-line pigeonhole argument (the companion’s Section 4.1).

In the coordinates φq=θq/log⁡q\varphi_q=\theta_q/\log q the torus is ℝP/ℤP\mathbb{R}^P/\mathbb{Z}^P, and by Kronecker’s theorem T0T_0 is the image of the smallest rational subspace L⊆ℝPL\subseteq\mathbb{R}^P containing ω=(1/log⁡q)q∈P\omega=(1/\log q)_{q\in P}. The annihilator of LL is a rational subspace spanned by the integer vectors cc with ∑qcq/log⁡q=0\sum_qc_q/\log q=0. If it contained a nonzero vector supported on at most two primes a,ba,b, it would contain an integer one, and log⁡a/log⁡b\log a/\log b would be rational, which is false because no power of aa is a power of bb. So each φq\varphi_q is nonzero on LL and any two are linearly independent on LL. The case {2,3,5}\{2,3,5\}, checked in Lean

Theorem 2.6 (the distinct-height sum for {2,3,5}\{2,3,5\}). The number 𝒟{2,3,5}\mathcal D_{\{2,3,5\}} is irrational.

The Lean proof takes a different route (companion, Sections 4.2 and 4.3): each dyadic block acts on the normalised tail by one of five affine maps with disjoint images, so a tail determines every later block, and a rational value would make the blocks eventually periodic, which the powers of 33 prevent. With an automaton recording how recently each prime appeared, the same method proves every set of three primes at most 3131 by exact computation, and it fails for {2,3,5,7}\{2,3,5,7\}, where two words of blocks have the same affine map (companion, Sections 4.4 and 4.5).

Exact rank is not an approximation obstruction.

Truncating the original kernel to i<Ni<N gives a sum of NN separated terms. The bound H⁡(x)>x3/(pqr)\operatorname{H}(x)>x^3/(pqr) makes the truncation error tend to zero both uniformly and in ℓ1(ℕ3)\ell^1(\mathbb N^3). By contrast, the rescaled matrix in the rank proof has uniform distance (1−1/r)/2(1-1/r)/2 from the matrices of finite rank. Rescaling preserves exact rank but removes the decay responsible for the first approximation. The companion proves both assertions, including the distinction between finite restrictions and the full infinite matrix, in Section 5.1.

The two-prime comparison

Fan’s post of 26 June 2026 gives the repeated two-prime factorisation, its quadratic expression in one Hecke–Mahler value and its transcendence conclusion [7]. Counting the contributions at powers of each prime also gives the affine formula for the distinct-height sum below. The calculation fixes the normalisations and distinguishes the two sums.

Theorem 4.1 (both two-prime sums). Let p<qp<q be distinct primes. Put θ=log⁡p/log⁡q\theta=\log p/\log q and A=∑n≥0p−nq−⌊nθ⌋A=\sum_{n\ge0}p^{-n}q^{-\lfloor n\theta\rfloor}. Guide to the formal sources

There are four separate arguments. The proof of Theorem 2.1 uses integral tails, rearranged blocks, a closed subtorus and an Euler characteristic count, and has no Lean proof. The proof of Theorem 2.6 uses dyadic blocks, the disjoint images of five affine maps and the irrationality of log⁡2/log⁡3\log2/\log3; its Lean proof follows the same route. The rank proof uses the height formula, row and column rescaling, and a threshold determinant. The arithmetic argument uses the shell coefficients, the tail recurrence, denominator clearing and least positive residues. The Lean proof of Theorem 6.2 establishes the equivalence of the residue condition with irrationality; it does not establish the residue condition itself.
