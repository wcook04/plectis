Introduction

The Sylvester recurrence an+1=an2−an+1a_{n+1}=a_n^2-a_n+1 gives a rational reciprocal sum by telescoping: 1an−1=1an+1an+1−1.\frac1{a_n-1}=\frac1{a_n}+\frac1{a_{n+1}-1}. For an increasing sequence of positive integers that eventually satisfies this recurrence, the tail sum at every sufficiently large index is 1/(an−1)1/(a_n-1). Erdős and Graham asked whether every rational reciprocal sum with an+1∼an2a_{n+1}\sim a_n^2 arises in this way.

Problem 1.1 (Erdős Problem 243). Let 1≤a1<a2<⋯1\le a_1<a_2<\cdots be a sequence of integers with limn→∞anan−12=1and∑1an∈ℚ.\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{a_n}{a_{n-1}^{2}}=1 \qquad\text{and}\qquad \sum\frac{1}{a_n}\in\mathbb{Q} . Then an=an−12−an−1+1a_n=a_{n-1}^{2}-a_{n-1}+1 for all sufficiently large nn.

Erdős and Graham [5] asked this question; see also Erdős [6] and Bloom’s catalogue [11]. We prove the following irrationality result for a restricted class of sequences with an+1∼an2a_{n+1}\sim a_n^2.

Theorem 1.2 (cubic-rate irrationality). A strictly increasing sequence of positive integers with an2/an+1=1+3n+o(n−3)a_n^2/a_{n+1}=1+\frac3n+o(n^{-3}) has irrational reciprocal sum.

Under the eventual Sylvester recurrence, an2/an+1−1=O(1/an)a_n^2/a_{n+1}-1=O(1/a_n), which is much smaller than 3/n3/n. Theorem 1.2 therefore treats a restricted case of the problem by excluding rationality altogether. The general quadratic limit an+1∼an2a_{n+1}\sim a_n^2 remains insufficient for any argument given here.

For example, set a1=8a_1=8 and an+1=⌈nan2n+3⌉.a_{n+1}=\left\lceil\frac{n a_n^2}{n+3}\right\rceil. This gives 8,16,103,5305,…8,16,103,5305,\ldots. From an+1≥an2/4a_{n+1}\ge a_n^2/4 we obtain an≥4⋅22n−1a_n\ge4\cdot2^{2^{n-1}} by induction. The error introduced by the ceiling is less than one, and hence 0≤1+3n−an2an+1<16an2=o(n−3).0\le 1+\frac3n-\frac{a_n^2}{a_{n+1}}<\frac{16}{a_n^2}=o(n^{-3}). The theorem applies to this explicitly defined sequence.

If the sum is rational, clearing denominators gives positive integer tail numerators CnC_n. Their growth is cubic, so we compare them with Fn=n(n+1)(n+2)F_n=n(n+1)(n+2), for which Fn+1/Fn=1+3/nF_{n+1}/F_n=1+3/n. The ratio estimate gives Cn=AFn+o(n)C_n=AF_n+o(n). Subtracting the recurrences for CnC_n and AFnAF_n then shows that the first difference of the error tends to zero. It follows that Δ4Cn→0\Delta^4C_n\to0. Since these fourth differences are integers, they eventually vanish, and Cn=AFn+BC_n=AF_n+B.

We next use the denominator recurrence to constrain this polynomial. It gives a square condition at every root of a cubic modulo almost every prime. Chebotarev’s theorem implies that the corresponding element of the cubic number field is a square. Trace identities leave two possible polynomials; four consecutive terms modulo seven exclude both. The proof in Section 3 follows this order.

For the recurrence criteria we use the integer tail numerators of Koizumi [10]. In Section 4, a stable gcd and the Chinese remainder theorem exclude a rational tail whose quotient a1⋯an−1/ana_1\cdots a_{n-1}/a_n has bounded upward increments. An elementary infinite product argument gives the separate summability criterion of Section 5. We then clear the tails by a least common multiple and count crossings of new maxima (Section 6). The resulting weighted criterion isolates an arithmetic estimate still missing from the general problem. The companion paper also treats nonintegral rates and proves a stronger cubic disagreement theorem. Its detailed proof locations and the formal sources are listed in Appendix B.

Proof of cubic-rate irrationality

Suppose, towards a contradiction, that the reciprocal sum in Theorem 1.2 is rational. Construct Cn,DnC_n,D_n as in Section 2. Equation [eq:tail-estimate] transfers the rate without changing its precision: Cn+1Cn=1+3n+o(n−3).\begin{equation} \label{eq:cubic-ratio} \frac{C_{n+1}}{C_n}=1+\frac3n+o(n^{-3}). \end{equation} We first show that CnC_n is eventually a polynomial, using [eq:cubic-ratio] and integrality. Bounded increases

We return to the rational tails of Section 2. The Sylvester example had a constant integer numerator. Finite total relative increase

An infinite product gives another way to bound a numerator. This argument needs only the scalar update Cn+1=Cn−EnC_{n+1}=C_n-E_n and positivity on a discrete set. A factorial modulus for integral recursion

A constant-negative error En=−mE_n=-m with C0=cC_0=c gives Cn=c+nmC_n=c+nm and the identity Dn+m=(an−1)Cn.\begin{equation} \label{eq:shape} D_n+m=(a_n-1)C_n. \end{equation} In the case m=c=1m=c=1, where [eq:shape] reads Dn+1=(an−1)(n+1)D_n+1=(a_n-1)(n+1), each multiplier is determined by its predecessor. The recursion is an+1=num⁡(n,an)/(n+2)a_{n+1}=\operatorname{num}(n,a_n)/(n+2), where num⁡(n,a)=(n+1)a2−(n+2)a+(n+3),\operatorname{num}(n,a)=(n+1)a^{2}-(n+2)a+(n+3), the forced numerator. We stop at the first nonintegral quotient. This procedure checks divisibility only; it does not impose the positivity conditions required of an infinite reciprocal-tail sequence. The formal exact-division condition requires exact division at each step.

Example A.1 (a nonintegral second step). Starting from a0=3a_0=3, the first numerator num⁡(0,3)=6\operatorname{num}(0,3)=6 is divisible by 22, giving a1=3a_1=3. The next numerator num⁡(1,3)=13\operatorname{num}(1,3)=13 is not divisible by 33, so the recursion stops.

Direct iteration can produce very large intermediate integers. To decide whether the first hh divisions are exact, however, it suffices to know the initial value modulo (h+1)!(h+1)!. Computing a pseudo-greedy orbit through residues modulo a shrinking product modulus is Koizumi’s method [10]; for the forced numerator that product is a factorial.

The first hh steps of the recursion an+1=num⁡(n,an)/(n+2)a_{n+1}=\operatorname{num}(n,a_n)/(n+2), starting at index zero, are integral for a0=aa_0=a if and only if they are integral for a0=ba_0=b.

Proof. Define M(0,i)=1M(0,i)=1 and M(h+1,i)=(i+2)M(h,i+1)M(h+1,i)=(i+2)M(h,i+1). Thus M(h,0)=(h+1)!M(h,0)=(h+1)!.
