Excluding the Bounded Negative Part

Erdős #243

Introduction

Conventions and reading route.

Write z+=max⁡(z,0)z_+=\max(z,0), take empty products and least common multiples to be 11, and allow deletion of a finite prefix. In a higher-order rate such as 1+3/n+o(n−3)1+3/n+o(n^{-3}), retain the original index nn: deleting terms does not authorise renumbering that rate. A means eventual equality an+1=an2−an+1a_{n+1}=a_n^2-a_n+1, not a finite reciprocal expansion. For the main argument read the next corollary, the integer-state theorem, Section  and the transfer in Section . Sections – give alternative criteria; Section  records ordinary consequences whose full proofs are in the companion reasoning paper.

For an>1a_n>1, the recurrence an+1=an2−an+1a_{n+1}=a_n^2-a_n+1 is singled out by the telescope 1an−1=1an+1an+1−1.\frac1{a_n-1}=\frac1{a_n}+\frac1{a_{n+1}-1}. Thus a Sylvester tail has the exact value 1/(an−1)1/(a_n-1) beginning at index nn. The question is whether rationality and the asymptotic relation an+1∼an2a_{n+1}\sim a_n^2 force that exact recurrence. The following result isolates a sufficient one-sided bound on the departure from it.

Corollary 1.1 (original-coordinate bounded defect). Let a1<a2<⋯a_1<a_2<\cdots be positive integers, an+1/an2→1a_{n+1}/a_n^2\to1, and ∑n≥11/an∈ℚ\sum_{n\ge1}1/a_n\in\mathbb{Q}. Put Pn=∏j<najP_n=\prod_{j<n}a_j. If limsupn→∞Pnan(an2an+1−1)<+∞,\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{P_n}{a_n} \left(\frac{a_n^2}{a_{n+1}}-1\right)<+\infty, then an+1=an2−an+1a_{n+1}=a_n^2-a_n+1 for all sufficiently large nn.

The upper limit is allowed to be −∞-\infty; the condition means an eventual finite upper bound, not a bound on the absolute value. No monotonicity or summability of the growth defect is assumed.

The proof is given in Section . The full original-coordinate implication, including the canonical analytic transfer, is also formalised as the original-coordinate bounded-defect theorem. Its hypothesis is an eventual upper bound on the displayed product defect, equivalent here to the finite-upper-limit condition. The integer-state theorem below isolates the arithmetic step; it is not the whole extent of the formalisation.

The obstruction.

Clear the rational reciprocal tail as Cn/DnC_n/D_n; these are the denominator-cleared tail variables of Koizumi’s pseudo-greedy formulation [10]. On a Sylvester tail, Dn=(an−1)CnD_n=(a_n-1)C_n; the integer error En=Dn−(an−1)CnE_n=D_n-(a_n-1)C_n measures the failure of this identity. Its update Cn+1=Cn−EnC_{n+1}=C_n-E_n turns a bound on negative error into a bound on upward increments. Small increments alone are insufficient. The arithmetic input is that, after the common divisor stabilises, every old multiplier must remain coprime to every later reduced numerator. The Chinese remainder theorem then produces a block of forbidden heights that no permitted jump can cross. A numerator tending to infinity would have to cross it. Section  adapts this crossing argument when only jumps setting new records are charged.

Write ctr⁡(a,D,C)=D−(a−1)C\operatorname{ctr}(a,D,C)=D-(a-1)C. The following integer-state theorem implies Corollary ; the ordinary tail transfer is proved in Section . Its centring hypothesis is redundant under normalised vanishing and is retained to match the source statement.

Theorem 1.2 (bounded negative part). Let a,C,D:ℕ→ℕa,C,D:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N} and E:ℕ→ℤE:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{Z} satisfy

  1. an>1a_n>1 and Cn>0C_n>0 for every nn;

  2. the exact dynamics Cn+1+Dn=anCnC_{n+1}+D_n=a_nC_n and Dn+1=anDnD_{n+1}=a_nD_n;

  3. En=ctr⁡(an,Dn,Cn)E_n=\operatorname{ctr}(a_n,D_n,C_n) for every nn;

  4. : |En|<Cn|E_n|<C_n for all large nn;

  5. : −B≤En-B\le E_n for all large nn, for some integer B≥0B\ge0;

  6. : for every integer K≥1K\ge1 there is an NN with K|En|<CnK\,|E_n|<C_n for all n≥Nn\ge N.

Then En=0E_n=0 for all sufficiently large nn: this is the eventual bounded-negative rigidity.

The Lean proof shifts the orbit past the centring and lower-bound thresholds and applies the bounded-negative-part rigidity, with centring and bound at every index.

Problem 1.3 (Erdős #243). Let 1≤a1<a2<⋯1\le a_1<a_2<\cdots be a sequence of integers with limn→∞anan−12=1and∑1an∈ℚ.\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{a_n}{a_{n-1}^{2}}=1 \qquad\text{and}\qquad \sum\frac{1}{a_n}\in\mathbb{Q} . Then an=an−12−an−1+1a_n=a_{n-1}^{2}-a_{n-1}+1 for all sufficiently large nn.

The question is recorded by Erdős and Graham [5] and by Erdős [6], and Bloom’s catalogue lists it as Problem #243 [11].

The additional hypothesis in Theorem  is the lower bound on EnE_n. The growth and rationality assumptions already supply the other state hypotheses after a finite shift. The nonnegative-error case, Badea’s criterion [3] in Koizumi’s coordinates, is integer descent [10]; the theorem allows arbitrary sign changes with bounded negative depth. The published product-weighted criterion, stated as Koizumi’s Corollary 4(1), assumes a nonpositive upper limit [10], and Corollary  allows any finite upper limit. Bado’s preprint, posted in September 2026, assumes two-sided bounded errors [7]; Theorem  needs only an eventual lower bound.

The arithmetic ingredients

Error, absorption and descent

The identities below follow algebraically from Koizumi’s state recurrence [10]. His Lemma 3 gives absorption, and his Proposition 1(2) gives the nonnegative-error descent, credited there to Badea . The integer-remainder method for Cantor series is older [12]. Hančl and Tijdeman use a finite polynomial decomposition and the resulting shifted polynomial sum to characterise rationality of polynomial Cantor series . Their polynomial hypotheses are essential to this comparison; we do not invoke an unrestricted rearrangement of arrays of integer coefficients. These are antecedents of the remainder method, not proofs of the bounded-negative Chinese-remainder obstruction used here.

Write Δn=an+1−(an2−an+1)\Delta_n=a_{n+1}-(a_n^2-a_n+1).

Proposition 3.1 (error identities). For an exact integer state, Cn+1=Cn−En,ΔnCn+1=an2En−En+1.C_{n+1}=C_n-E_n,\qquad \Delta_n C_{n+1}=a_n^2E_n-E_{n+1}.

Proof. Substitute En=Dn−(an−1)CnE_n=D_n-(a_n-1)C_n and use Dn+1=anDnD_{n+1}=a_nD_n, Cn+1=anCn−DnC_{n+1}=a_nC_n-D_n. ◻

Both identities are the update law and the defect identity.

Theorem 3.2 (absorption and descent). For a positive exact state with strict centring, En=0E_n=0 implies En+1=0E_{n+1}=0. For any positive integer state with Cn+1=Cn−EnC_{n+1}=C_n-E_n, eventual nonnegativity of EnE_n implies its eventual vanishing.

Proof. When En=0E_n=0, the second identity makes En+1E_{n+1} a multiple of Cn+1C_{n+1}; strict centring forces that multiple to be zero. In the second assertion, CnC_n is eventually a nonincreasing sequence of positive integers, so it stabilises. ◻

Both assertions are absorption of a vanishing centred state and stabilisation of the nonnegative error.

Corollary 3.3 (two zero errors). If En=En+1=0E_n=E_{n+1}=0 and Cn+1≠0C_{n+1}\ne0, then an+1=an2−an+1a_{n+1}=a_n^2-a_n+1. Thus eventual zero error in a positive exact state implies the eventual Sylvester recurrence.

Proof. The second error identity has nonzero factor Cn+1C_{n+1}. ◻

The local and eventual forms are the local rigidity step and the eventual Sylvester recurrence.

Absorption holds after any eventual centring threshold. On a nonterminal tail it removes all subsequent zeros. Then |En|≥1|E_n|\ge1, so normalised vanishing gives Cn→∞C_n\to\infty.

The first-crossing obstruction

Lemma 3.4 (consecutive multiples). For pairwise coprime integers m0,…,mB−1≥2m_0,\ldots,m_{B-1}\ge2 and every lower bound, there is a larger tt such that mi∣t+im_i\mid t+i for each i<Bi<B.

Proof. Solve t≡−i(mod⁡mi)t\equiv-i\pmod{m_i} by the Chinese remainder theorem, then add multiples of ∏imi\prod_i m_i. ◻

Lemma  matches each modulus mim_i to its own multiple t+it+i inside a window of BB consecutive integers. The same forbidden block and first-crossing contradiction, under a two-sided bound on the error, appear in the proof of Bado’s Theorem 5.1 [7]. The lemma here isolates bounded upward movement, and the gcd stabilisation below makes it applicable with only a lower bound on the error.

Theorem 3.5 (bounded-rise obstruction). Let u:ℕ→ℕu:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N} tend to infinity and let B≥1B\ge1 be an integer with un+1≤un+Bu_{n+1}\le u_n+B for every nn. There is no sequence of pairwise coprime integers mi≥2m_i\ge2 for which gcd⁡(mi,ut)=1\gcd(m_i,u_t)=1 whenever i<ti<t.

Proof. Choose m0,…,mB−1m_0,\ldots,m_{B-1} and use Lemma  to find t>max⁡(u0,…,uB)t>\max(u_0,\ldots,u_B) with mi∣t+im_i\mid t+i. Since un→∞u_n\to\infty, there is a first n>Bn>B with un≥tu_n\ge t. Minimality and the rise bound give t≤un≤un−1+B<t+B.t\le u_n\le u_{n-1}+B<t+B. Hence un=t+iu_n=t+i for some 0≤i<B<n0\le i<B<n, so mi∣unm_i\mid u_n. This contradicts gcd⁡(mi,un)=1\gcd(m_i,u_n)=1. The argument uses first crossing, not monotonicity: the numerator may fall before it reaches the block. ◻

Reduction and stabilisation

A reduced exact tail has positive unu_n, integer vn≥0v_n\ge0, gcd⁡(un,vn)=1\gcd(u_n,v_n)=1, and un+1=anun−vn,vn+1=anvn.u_{n+1}=a_nu_n-v_n,\qquad v_{n+1}=a_nv_n.

Proposition 3.6 (persistent coprimality). In a reduced exact tail, gcd⁡(an,vn)=1\gcd(a_n,v_n)=1. Distinct multipliers are pairwise coprime, and every earlier multiplier is coprime to every later numerator.

Proof. A common prime divisor of an,vna_n,v_n would divide both un+1u_{n+1} and vn+1v_{n+1}. Also, ai∣vta_i\mid v_t for i<ti<t, so reducedness gives gcd⁡(ai,ut)=1\gcd(a_i,u_t)=1, and gcd⁡(at,vt)=1\gcd(a_t,v_t)=1 gives gcd⁡(ai,at)=1\gcd(a_i,a_t)=1. ◻

All three conclusions are the step coprimality, the pairwise coprimality, and the whole-modulus avoidance.

Proposition 3.7 (gcd stabilisation). For a positive exact state, if negative errors have bounded magnitudes along a cofinal set of indices, then Gn=gcd⁡(Cn,Dn)G_n=\gcd(C_n,D_n) eventually stabilises. Division by its stable value gives a reduced exact tail.

Proof. Both updates preserve common divisors, so Gn∣Gn+1G_n\mid G_{n+1}. Moreover Gn∣EnG_n\mid E_n, hence Gn≤−EnG_n\le -E_n at a negative index. The positive divisibility chain is bounded along a cofinal set and therefore bounded everywhere; it eventually stabilises. Division by the stable value preserves both exact updates and leaves the states coprime. ◻

The stable gcd is gcd stabilisation.

In the main proof, division by the stable gcd preserves divergence and a bounded upward increment. Theorem  therefore applies to the reduced numerator: this is the eventual reduced exclusion. With normalised vanishing the same obstruction is the exclusion from normalised vanishing. Sparse changes of the gcd without eventual stabilisation are a different statement; the supporting record retains that distinction and its finite-block consequences.

A scalar finite-mass criterion

Theorem 5.1 (finite negative relative mass). Let CnC_n be positive integers and EnE_n integers satisfying Cn+1=Cn−EnC_{n+1}=C_n-E_n. If ∑n(−En)+Cn<∞,\sum_n\frac{(-E_n)_+}{C_n}<\infty, then En=0E_n=0 eventually. Neither denominator dynamics nor normalised vanishing is required. Positivity and integrality of CnC_n are essential.

Proof. Set δn=(−En)+/Cn\delta_n=(-E_n)_+/C_n. Then CN≤C0∏n<N(1+δn)≤C0exp⁡(∑nδn).C_N\le C_0\prod_{n<N}(1+\delta_n) \le C_0\exp\!\left(\sum_n\delta_n\right). Choose an integer upper bound KK for CnC_n. Each strict rise contributes at least 1/K1/K to ∑δn\sum\delta_n, so there are only finitely many rises. The remaining positive integer sequence is nonincreasing and stabilises, and the update then gives En=0E_n=0. ◻

For comparison, the real sequence Cn=1+1/(n+1)C_n=1+1/(n+1) has En=Cn−Cn+1>0E_n=C_n-C_{n+1}>0 and zero negative mass, but never stabilises. Thus the scalar conclusion is genuinely discrete.

The scalar statement, with no denominator dynamics or normalised-vanishing assumption, is formalised as vanishing from finite scalar negative mass. Its exact-orbit consequence is the Sylvester recurrence from finite scalar negative mass. The same three declarations on an exact reciprocal-tail orbit, with denominator dynamics and normalised vanishing, are the exact-orbit growth bound, vanishing from summable exact-orbit negative mass, and the exact-orbit Sylvester recurrence from summable negative mass. The shorter proof above is included because this implication is an elementary product bound followed by descent of positive integers.

On an exact reciprocal-tail orbit the conclusion gives the Sylvester recurrence. For the gap sequence of the pseudo-greedy expansion, the same criterion, with the same product bound and integer descent, appears in the Erdős Problem a Day working report on Problem #243, dated 12 August 2026 [8]; the statement above is for any positive integer sequence with Cn+1=Cn−EnC_{n+1}=C_n-E_n. The scalar criterion and bounded negative error are distinct quantities to estimate; on the common exact-orbit class with normalised vanishing they are each equivalent to the same endpoint.

A factorial residue reduction for forced orbits

A constant-negative error En=−mE_n=-m with C0=cC_0=c produces Cn=c+nmC_n=c+nm and the shape equation Dn+m=(an−1)Cn.\begin{equation} \label{eq:shape} D_n+m=(a_n-1)C_n. \end{equation} In the case m=c=1m=c=1, where  reads Dn+1=(an−1)(n+1)D_n+1=(a_n-1)(n+1), each multiplier is determined by its predecessor; we call such an orbit . At index nn the numerator of the next multiplier is num⁡(n,a)=(n+1)a2−(n+2)a+(n+3),\operatorname{num}(n,a)=(n+1)a^{2}-(n+2)a+(n+3), the forced numerator, and the divisor is n+2n+2. The orbit survives a step when that division is exact, giving a survival predicate, the survival predicate.

Example B.1 (the shape equation running until it fails). The forced orbit from a=3a=3 reads this way: num⁡(0,3)=6\operatorname{num}(0,3)=6 is divisible by 22 and gives a1=3a_1=3, while num⁡(1,3)=13\operatorname{num}(1,3)=13 is not divisible by 33, so the orbit stops there.

Deciding survival by iteration is expensive because the orbit grows doubly exponentially, and it is unnecessary: survival over a finite horizon depends on the initial value only through a factorial residue. Computing a pseudo-greedy orbit through residues modulo a shrinking product modulus is Koizumi’s method [10]; for the forced numerator that product is a factorial.

Theorem B.2 (factorial residue reduction). For all hh and all integers a≡b(mod⁡(h+1)!)a\equiv b \pmod{(h+1)!}, the orbit from aa survives hh forced updates exactly when the orbit from bb does.

Proof. Let M(0,i)=1M(0,i)=1 and M(h+1,i)=(i+2)M(h,i+1)M(h+1,i)=(i+2)M(h,i+1), an ascending factorial with M(h,0)=(h+1)!M(h,0)=(h+1)!. The numerator is a polynomial with integer coefficients, so congruences transfer; reducing the modulus gives divisibility by i+2i+2 for one exactly when for the other, and cancelling that common factor from both values and modulus leaves the inductive hypothesis at M(h,i+1)M(h,i+1). ◻

Formalised as the factorial residue reduction, over the shrinking-modulus transport, the polynomial congruence, and the exact-division cancellation; the modulus identifications are the ascending-factorial form and the factorial value at the initial index.

At h=1h=1 the modulus is 2!=22!=2, and surviving one update means 2∣a2−2a+32\mid a^{2}-2a+3, which holds exactly for odd aa: for instance num⁡(0,3)=6\operatorname{num}(0,3)=6 but num⁡(0,4)=11\operatorname{num}(0,4)=11. So one step of survival is decided by the parity of aa alone, which is Theorem  at its smallest nontrivial horizon.
