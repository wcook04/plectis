Problem note

Bounded Increments and Rational Reciprocal Sums

Erdős #243

Introduction

For integers an>1a_n>1, the Sylvester recurrence an+1=an2−an+1a_{n+1}=a_n^2-a_n+1 gives the telescoping identity 1an−1=1an+1an+1−1.\frac1{a_n-1}=\frac1{a_n}+\frac1{a_{n+1}-1}. We call a sequence that satisfies this recurrence eventually a Sylvester tail. Its reciprocal sum from any index nn in that tail onwards is 1/(an−1)1/(a_n-1). Erdős Problem #243 asks whether rationality and an+1∼an2a_{n+1}\sim a_n^2 force this recurrence. We prove the following sufficient condition.

Corollary 1.1 (bounded increments of Pn/anP_n/a_n). Let a1<a2<⋯a_1<a_2<\cdots be positive integers, an+1/an2→1a_{n+1}/a_n^2\to1, and ∑n≥11/an∈ℚ\sum_{n\ge1}1/a_n\in\mathbb{Q}. Put Pn=∏j<najP_n=\prod_{j<n}a_j. If limsupn→∞Pnan(an2an+1−1)<+∞,\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{P_n}{a_n} \left(\frac{a_n^2}{a_{n+1}}-1\right)<+\infty, then an+1=an2−an+1a_{n+1}=a_n^2-a_n+1 for all sufficiently large nn.

Lean: original coordinate bounded defect.

The assumption bounds the upward increments of Pn/anP_n/a_n; it does not bound Pn/anP_n/a_n itself. Indeed, Pnan(an2an+1−1)=Pn+1an+1−Pnan.\frac{P_n}{a_n}\left(\frac{a_n^2}{a_{n+1}}-1\right) =\frac{P_{n+1}}{a_{n+1}}-\frac{P_n}{a_n}. The hypothesis is one-sided: it imposes no lower bound on the increments and does not require monotonicity. The growth hypothesis alone says that the ratio of consecutive terms of Pn/anP_n/a_n tends to one; it does not bound their difference.

For a Sylvester sequence starting at a1>1a_1>1, an−1=(a1−1)Pna_n-1=(a_1-1)P_n, so the increments tend to zero. For an=b2n−1a_n=b^{2^{n-1}}, with integer b≥2b\ge2, they are identically zero; since this sequence has no Sylvester tail, the corollary recovers the irrationality of its reciprocal sum. The bound also allows positive limiting increments: the integer sequence a1=4a_1=4, an+1=⌈nan2/(n+1)⌉a_{n+1}=\lceil n a_n^2/(n+1)\rceil, beginning 4,8,43,1387,…4,8,43,1387,\ldots, is such an example. We verify this after the main proof in Section . A ratio tending to one alone does not give the bound: the rounded cubic-rate example in Section  has increments of order n2n^2.

Write the rational tail as Cn/DnC_n/D_n, clearing denominators without reducing at each step. Up to a finite shift and a common positive factor, these are Koizumi’s tail numerators and denominators [10]. The error En=Dn−(an−1)CnE_n=D_n-(a_n-1)C_n measures departure from the Sylvester tail, and Cn+1=Cn−EnC_{n+1}=C_n-E_n turns a lower error bound into an upper increment bound. If the error does not vanish eventually, the numerator tends to infinity and negative errors occur arbitrarily late. Each gcd divides every later error, so the bounded negative magnitudes force the gcd to stabilise. Dividing it out gives coprimality to every earlier multiplier. The Chinese remainder theorem then supplies a forbidden block that bounded upward jumps cannot cross without landing inside.

Here is the precise arithmetic statement. For the rational tails, Section  supplies every condition except (5). Condition (4) follows from (6) with K=1K=1; listing it separately makes the hypothesis used when propagating a zero error explicit.

Theorem 1.2 (bounded negative part). Let a,C,D:ℕ→ℕa,C,D:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N} and E:ℕ→ℤE:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{Z} satisfy

  1. an>1a_n>1 and Cn>0C_n>0 for every nn;

  2. the exact dynamics Cn+1+Dn=anCnC_{n+1}+D_n=a_nC_n and Dn+1=anDnD_{n+1}=a_nD_n;

  3. En=Dn−(an−1)CnE_n=D_n-(a_n-1)C_n for every nn;

  4. eventual strict centring: |En|<Cn|E_n|<C_n for all large nn;

  5. eventually bounded negative part: −B≤En-B\le E_n for all large nn, for some integer B≥0B\ge0;

  6. vanishing relative error: for every integer K≥1K\ge1 there is an NN with K|En|<CnK\,|E_n|<C_n for all n≥Nn\ge N.

Then En=0E_n=0 for all sufficiently large nn.

Lean: eventually bounded negative part eventually zero.

Condition (5) bounds upward steps but permits sign changes and places no independent upper bound on positive errors. Condition (6) is |En|/Cn→0|E_n|/C_n\to0. Absorption needs only the weaker centring bound (4), not −Cn/2≤En<Cn/2-C_n/2\le E_n<C_n/2. The scalar example Cn=n+1C_n=n+1, En=−1E_n=-1 satisfies the update and conditions (5)–(6) without stabilising: the relations involving the denominator are essential.

Problem 1.3 (Erdős #243). Let 1≤a1<a2<⋯1\le a_1<a_2<\cdots be a sequence of integers with limn→∞anan−12=1and∑1an∈ℚ.\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{a_n}{a_{n-1}^{2}}=1 \qquad\text{and}\qquad \sum\frac{1}{a_n}\in\mathbb{Q} . Then an=an−12−an−1+1a_n=a_{n-1}^{2}-a_{n-1}+1 for all sufficiently large nn.

The question is recorded by Erdős and Graham [5] and by Erdős [6], and Bloom’s catalogue lists it as Problem #243 [11].

Sections – contain the main proof: the arithmetic argument, its gcd and CRT lemmas, and the construction from the reciprocal series. The later sections give extensions and state the estimate still needed for the unrestricted problem. Appendix  records the formal correspondences separately.

Error, absorption and descent

The error identity gives both the Sylvester recurrence and propagation of a zero error. Descent then handles an eventually nonnegative error.

Absorption uses strict centring at the successor index. After an eventual centring threshold, a zero therefore propagates to every later index. On a tail that is not eventually Sylvester, no zero can occur beyond that threshold. Then |En|≥1|E_n|\ge1, so vanishing relative error gives Cn→∞C_n\to\infty.

The first-crossing obstruction

Lean: exists shifted consecutive multiples.

Proof. Solve t≡−i(mod⁡mi)t\equiv-i\pmod{m_i} by the Chinese remainder theorem, then add multiples of ∏imi\prod_i m_i. ◻

Lemma  matches each modulus mim_i to its own multiple t+it+i inside a window of BB consecutive integers. The same forbidden block and first-crossing contradiction, under a two-sided bound on the error, appear in the proof of Bado’s Theorem 5.1 [7]. The lemma here isolates bounded upward movement, and the gcd stabilisation below makes it applicable with only a lower bound on the error.

For example, the consecutive integers 6k+2,6k+36k+2,6k+3 are forbidden to a sequence coprime to both 22 and 33. Upward jumps of size at most 22 cannot cross this pair from below without landing in it. The following statement is the same first-crossing argument with a block supplied by the Chinese remainder theorem.

The additional hypothesis in Theorem  is the lower bound on EnE_n. The growth and rationality assumptions already supply the other arithmetic hypotheses after a finite shift. Badea’s criterion assumes instead the eventual growth inequality an+1≥an2−an+1a_{n+1}\ge a_n^2-a_n+1 and forces equality under rationality. Koizumi’s nonnegative-error result is integer descent [10]. These are not the same one-step inequality, although under the standing hypotheses either one, imposed eventually, forces a Sylvester tail and hence the other. The present theorem allows sign changes but bounds the magnitude of negative errors. Koizumi’s Corollary 4(1) assumes a nonpositive upper limit for the product expression [10], and Corollary  allows any finite upper limit. Bado’s preprint, posted in September 2026, assumes two-sided bounded errors [7]; Theorem  needs only an eventual lower bound. Both proofs use a forbidden block from the Chinese remainder theorem. Here the one-sided bound first stabilises the gcd, which makes that argument available for the reduced numerators.

The error identities, absorption and descent follow from Koizumi’s recurrences [10]; he credits the nonnegative-error case to Badea [3]. Earlier remainder methods are discussed in Appendix .

Finite total relative increase

The next condition concerns the sum of all upward increments of CnC_n, each divided by the value before that step. A Sylvester tail has no such increments after a finite index. In contrast, Cn=n+1C_n=n+1 and Cn=(n+1)2C_n=(n+1)^2 both have divergent sums, although their relative increments tend to zero. Summability is thus much stronger than that limit. The denominator recurrence is not needed for the following argument.

Theorem 5.1 (a convergent sum of relative increases). Let CnC_n be positive integers and EnE_n integers satisfying Cn+1=Cn−EnC_{n+1}=C_n-E_n. If ∑n(−En)+Cn<∞,\sum_n\frac{(-E_n)_+}{C_n}<\infty, then En=0E_n=0 eventually. Neither denominator dynamics nor vanishing relative error is required.

Each strict rise contributes at least 1/K1/K to ∑δn\sum\delta_n, so there are only finitely many rises. The remaining positive integer sequence is nonincreasing and stabilises, and the update then gives En=0E_n=0. ◻

With real errors, Cn=1+1/(n+1)C_n=1+1/(n+1) decreases forever with zero relative-increase sum; without positivity, Cn=−n−1C_n=-n-1, En=1E_n=1 does so too. For the gap sequence of the pseudo-greedy expansion, the same criterion, with the same product bound and integer descent, appears in the Erdős Problem a Day working report on Problem #243, dated 12 August 2026 [8]; the statement above is for any positive integer sequence with Cn+1=Cn−EnC_{n+1}=C_n-E_n. For exact reciprocal tails with |En|/Cn→0|E_n|/C_n\to0, each is equivalent to eventual vanishing of EnE_n; neither estimate has been derived here from the unrestricted problem.

A criterion using new maxima of an LCM numerator

Instead of multiplying all earlier denominators, one can clear the rational tail with their least common multiple. We will sum only at steps where the resulting numerator exceeds all its earlier values. This allows some increases to be omitted from the sum and others to receive smaller weights. A factorial residue reduction for forced orbits

A constant-negative error En=−mE_n=-m with C0=cC_0=c produces Cn=c+nmC_n=c+nm and the shape equation Dn+m=(an−1)Cn.\begin{equation} \label{eq:shape} D_n+m=(a_n-1)C_n. \end{equation} In the case m=c=1m=c=1, where  reads Dn+1=(an−1)(n+1)D_n+1=(a_n-1)(n+1), each multiplier is determined by its predecessor; we call such an orbit forced. At index nn the numerator of the next multiplier is num⁡(n,a)=(n+1)a2−(n+2)a+(n+3),\operatorname{num}(n,a)=(n+1)a^{2}-(n+2)a+(n+3), the forced numerator, and the divisor is n+2n+2. The orbit continues for another step exactly when this division is exact. Here survival counts exact divisions; it does not impose the positivity conditions of an infinite reciprocal-tail orbit. The formal survival predicate requires exact division at each step.

Example B.1 (the shape equation running until it fails). The forced orbit from a=3a=3 reads this way: num⁡(0,3)=6\operatorname{num}(0,3)=6 is divisible by 22 and gives a1=3a_1=3, while num⁡(1,3)=13\operatorname{num}(1,3)=13 is not divisible by 33, so the orbit stops there.

Direct iteration can produce very large intermediate integers. To decide whether the first hh divisions are exact, however, it suffices to know the initial value modulo (h+1)!(h+1)!. Computing a pseudo-greedy orbit through residues modulo a shrinking product modulus is Koizumi’s method [10]; for the forced numerator that product is a factorial.

Proof. Define M(0,i)=1M(0,i)=1 and M(h+1,i)=(i+2)M(h,i+1)M(h+1,i)=(i+2)M(h,i+1). Thus M(h,0)=(h+1)!M(h,0)=(h+1)!. We prove the stronger statement that, at index ii, congruent inputs modulo M(h,i)M(h,i) survive the same hh updates. There is nothing to prove for h=0h=0.

For the inductive step, suppose a≡b(mod⁡(i+2)M(h,i+1))a\equiv b\pmod{(i+2)M(h,i+1)}. Since num⁡(i,⋅)\operatorname{num}(i,\cdot) is an integer polynomial, its values at aa and bb are congruent modulo that product. In particular, one is divisible by i+2i+2 if and only if the other is. If neither is divisible, both orbits stop. Otherwise their quotients are congruent modulo M(h,i+1)M(h,i+1), so the induction hypothesis applies to the remaining hh updates at index i+1i+1. Taking i=0i=0 proves the claim. ◻

The formal factorial residue reduction uses the shrinking-modulus induction, polynomial congruence, and cancellation after exact division. Its modulus is identified by the ascending-factorial formula and its factorial value at the initial index.
