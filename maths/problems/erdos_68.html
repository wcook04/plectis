Introduction

Erdős asked whether replacing 𝑛!n! by 𝑛! −1n!−1 preserves the irrationality of the reciprocal sum [1]. The question appears as Problem 68 in Bloom’s catalogue [2].

Problem 1.1 (Erdős Problem #68). Is

𝑆=∑𝑛≥21𝑛!−1S=∑n≥21n!−1

irrational?

The elementary proof that 𝑒e is irrational uses two properties of factorials: they clear every earlier denominator, and they leave a small positive tail. The first property fails here. If 𝐻𝑚 =∑𝑚𝑛=2(𝑛! −1)−1Hm=∑n=2m(n!−1)−1, then already 3!𝐻3 =36/53!H3=36/5. We shall instead construct linear forms 𝑀𝑆 +𝑘MS+k, with 𝑀,𝑘 ∈ℤM,k∈Z, in which several initial summands cancel. A nonintegral form excludes every rational value 𝑆 =𝑎/𝑞S=a/q for which 𝑞 ∣𝑀q∣M.

We classify the integer coefficient vectors that cancel the first 𝐷 −1D−1 weighted sums. Starting with adjacent factorial differences, we correct each vector using those already constructed at its proper divisors. The resulting basis isolates each weighted sum (Theorem 2.1). The triangular change of basis is invertible over ℤZ, so it gives coordinates for every integer solution, not just a way to construct some solutions.

The desired vectors are supported on indices at least two. Temporarily allowing index one makes the basis construction possible; removing it then amounts to one linear Diophantine equation. Its solvability determines the possible values of 𝑀M. The gcd that occurs in this equation is defined by an infinite sequence, but Theorem 3.1 computes it on a specified finite interval.

When cancellation is imposed through 𝐷 =4D=4, the least positive value of 𝑀M is 13801380. We derive a vector attaining this value and bound its remainder between consecutive integers. The classification itself does not assume that 𝑆S is rational. An irrationality proof would require, for each 𝑞 >0q>0, a nonintegral form whose coefficient 𝑀M is divisible by 𝑞q. The companion constructs primitive vectors with this divisibility property; nonintegrality of their remainders remains unproved.

Hančl and Tijdeman’s tail-integrality lemma treats factorial series with integer coefficients [4]. Their factorial-scaled partial sums are integers. Here the scaled partial sum need not be integral. Instead, the weight congruences give R −𝑀𝑆 ∈ℤR−MS∈Z (1); the assumption 𝑆 =𝑎/𝑞S=a/q and 𝑞 ∣𝑀q∣M then makes RR integral. Koepf and Schmersau’s factorial-digit criterion [3] is used in the companion’s digit argument to give an equivalent eventual-pattern condition for 𝑆 −𝑒 +2S−e+2. Failure of that pattern at arbitrarily large indices also remains unproved.

Sections 2 and 3 give the coordinate construction and finite gcd. Section 4 uses them to construct and test a vector attaining the least positive value of 𝑀M. Section 5 states the remaining nonintegrality question, with the secondary arithmetic criteria developed in the companion.

Nonintegrality of the remainder

Fix a vector with 𝑉2 =⋯ =𝑉𝐷 =0V2=⋯=VD=0 and 𝑀 >0M>0. We may choose the cutoff 𝑁 ≥𝐷N≥D anywhere at or beyond its support. Separate the finite signed sum from the positive tail:

𝐴𝑁=𝑁∑𝑑=𝐷+1𝑉𝑑(𝜆)𝑑!−1,R(𝜆)=𝐴𝑁+𝑀(𝑆−𝐻𝑁).AN=∑d=D+1NVd(λ)d!−1,R(λ)=AN+M(S−HN).

The remainder lies above 𝐴𝑁AN. To place it below the next integer, it is enough that the omitted tail be shorter than the gap ⌊𝐴𝑁⌋ +1 −𝐴𝑁⌊AN⌋+1−AN. By (11), a sufficient condition is

Indeed, the positive tail and this comparison give ⌊𝐴𝑁⌋ <R(𝜆) <⌊𝐴𝑁⌋ +1⌊AN⌋<R(λ)<⌊AN⌋+1, excluding every denominator dividing 𝑀M. Failure of this sufficient comparison is inconclusive. Either sign of 𝐴𝑁AN is allowed: its strict gap is one when 𝐴𝑁AN is integral and can be arbitrarily small when 𝐴𝑁AN approaches an integer from below.

First keep the vector fixed and increase only the cutoff. The vector −6𝑒2 +𝑒4−6e2+e4 has 𝑀 =12M=12. More generally, for a fixed vector with 𝑀 >0M>0, the quantities 𝐴𝑁AN increase to RR. If this limit is nonintegral, their gaps to the next integer tend to a positive number, so the test eventually succeeds. If the limit is integral, the eventual gap equals the omitted tail and the test fails. Extending the cutoff therefore eventually detects a nonintegral remainder, but leaves both that remainder and the set of divisors of 𝑀M unchanged.

Changing coefficients while preserving 𝑀M also leaves integrality unchanged. By (1), the remainders of two such vectors differ by an integer. Adding multiples of the 𝑈𝑛Un can simplify the support and weighted sums, as in the example; it cannot turn an integral remainder into a nonintegral one.

To exclude every possible denominator, we must instead choose vectors whose values of 𝑀M have the required divisibility. There are primitive solutions with 0 <𝑀 <𝑁!0<M<N! for which 𝑀M is eventually divisible by any prescribed 𝑞q. The progression construction in the companion gives such a family by turning 𝑉𝑑 =0Vd=0 into roots of a polynomial. At its support endpoint the omitted tail is less than 2/𝑁2/N, but the gap on the right of (12) may shrink too. To prove irrationality by this test, for each 𝑞 >0q>0 we need one vector with 𝑞 ∣𝑀q∣M and a cutoff for which (12) holds. Divisibility and a small tail do not by themselves establish that comparison. No minimal-support assertion is made for this family.

The companion treats the equivalent carry condition and the complementary-denominator tail inequality. It also records two larger finite computations: a non-unit carry at 𝑚 =300000m=300000 excludes every 𝑞 ∣299999!q∣299999!, and a continued-fraction enclosure excludes every 𝑞 <239990q<239990. The factorial-divisibility exclusion does not exclude all denominators with only small prime factors, whose exponents may be larger.
