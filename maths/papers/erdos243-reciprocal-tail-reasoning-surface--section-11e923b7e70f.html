Reciprocal Sums and the Sylvester Recurrence: Further Results and Proofs

Erdős #243 By Will Cook September 2026 Authorship, AI use and citation
PDFOpen the PDF, 61 pages TeXLaTeX source Source on GitHub Problem page

Précis. An increasing sequence of positive integers satisfying 𝑎2𝑛/𝑎𝑛+1 =1 +3/𝑛 +𝑜(𝑛−3)an2/an+1=1+3/n+o(n−3) has an irrational reciprocal sum: rationality would force integer tail numerators to agree eventually with a cubic polynomial, which divisibility, a cubic-field calculation, and congruences modulo seven exclude. For rational reciprocal sums under 𝑎𝑛+1/𝑎2𝑛 →1an+1/an2→1, the record also develops sufficient conditions for the eventual recurrence 𝑎𝑛+1 =𝑎2𝑛 −𝑎𝑛 +1an+1=an2−an+1, controlling either increases of an integer tail numerator or a convergent sum at steps setting new maxima. Examples separate numerical growth bounds from the exact recurrences. The cubic proof is distinguished from the theorems formalised in Lean. None of the additional bounds is derived for every rational reciprocal sum in Erdős #243.

This paper owns the complete problem-specific reasoning surface for Erdős #243, including all registered result families and their boundaries.

It is not authority for the validity of claims tagged Lean, which belongs to the cited kernel-checked source, or a solution to Erdős #243, which remains open.

Constant negative errors

A constant negative error makes the numerator grow linearly. Such behaviour can persist for arbitrarily long finite intervals, even on genuine rational orbits, by the flat-transient theorem in the working report on Problem #243 [9]. We exclude infinite constant and periodic negative tails below. These exclusions supply no bound for the finite transient lengths.

Suppose the error is negative with a constant magnitude, 𝐸𝑛 = −𝑚En=−m for a fixed 𝑚 >0m>0 and every 𝑛n. By Proposition 4.1 the numerator increases by 𝑚m at each step, so 𝐶𝑛 =𝑐 +𝑛𝑚Cn=c+nm with 𝑐 =𝐶0c=C0, and the definition of the error becomes the identity

𝐷𝑛+𝑚=(𝑎𝑛−1)(𝑐+𝑛𝑚).(5.1)(5.1)Dn+m=(an−1)(c+nm).

Together with 𝐷𝑛+1 =𝑎𝑛𝐷𝑛Dn+1=anDn this is a closed system in (𝑎,𝐷)(a,D). We shall prove that it has no infinite natural-number solution with 𝑎𝑛 ≥2an≥2, after examining a finite example.

Example 9.1 (a nonintegral second step). Take 𝑚 =𝑐 =1m=c=1, so that 𝐶𝑛 =1 +𝑛Cn=1+n and (5.1) reads 𝐷𝑛 +1 =(𝑎𝑛 −1)(1 +𝑛)Dn+1=(an−1)(1+n), and take 𝐷0 =1D0=1. Each step is now determined: at 𝑛 =0n=0 the equation reads 2 =(𝑎0 −1) ⋅12=(a0−1)⋅1, so 𝑎0 =3a0=3, and 𝐷1 =𝑎0𝐷0 =3D1=a0D0=3; at 𝑛 =1n=1 it reads 4 =(𝑎1 −1) ⋅24=(a1−1)⋅2, so 𝑎1 =3a1=3, and 𝐷2 =𝑎1𝐷1 =9D2=a1D1=9; at 𝑛 =2n=2 it reads 10 =(𝑎2 −1) ⋅310=(a2−1)⋅3, which has no integer solution, and the orbit stops. Longer prefixes occur for other starting values. For 2 ≤𝑎0 <50002≤a0<5000, the exact residue search in Appendix B gives a maximum of 1717 successful updates, hence 1818 multiplier values including 𝑎0a0.

Theorem 9.2 (no constant negative magnitude). For any 𝑚,𝑐 ∈ℕm,c∈N with 𝑚 >0m>0, there is no pair of sequences 𝑎,𝐷 :ℕ →ℕa,D:N→N with 𝑎𝑛 ≥2an≥2 for all 𝑛n satisfying 𝐷𝑛+1 =𝑎𝑛𝐷𝑛Dn+1=anDn and (5.1). The same holds if the identity is assumed only from some index onwards.

Proof when 𝑐c and 𝑚m are coprime. Assume first gcd(𝑐,𝑚) =1gcd(c,m)=1. Every multiplier must share a prime with 𝑚m, but each such prime can occur in at most one multiplier.

Every 𝑎𝑗aj shares a prime with 𝑚m. Suppose gcd(𝑎𝑗,𝑚) =1gcd(aj,m)=1. Then 𝑚m is invertible modulo 𝑎𝑗aj, so some 𝑛n has 𝑐 +𝑛𝑚 ≡0c+nm≡0, that is 𝑎𝑗 ∣𝐶𝑛aj∣Cn; and 𝑎𝑗 ∣𝐷𝑛aj∣Dn for every 𝑛 >𝑗n>j, since 𝐷D is multiplicative with 𝑎𝑗aj among its factors. Choosing such an 𝑛n beyond 𝑗j and reading (5.1) modulo 𝑎𝑗aj gives 𝑎𝑗 ∣𝑚aj∣m, contradicting gcd(𝑎𝑗,𝑚) =1gcd(aj,m)=1 and 𝑎𝑗 ≥2aj≥2.

A prime divisor of 𝑚m occurs in at most one 𝑎𝑗aj. Suppose 𝑝 ∣𝑚p∣m and 𝑝 ∣𝑎𝑗p∣aj. For 𝑛 >𝑗n>j we have 𝑝 ∣𝐷𝑛p∣Dn, so (5.1) gives (𝑎𝑛 −1)𝐶𝑛 ≡0(mod𝑝)(an−1)Cn≡0(modp). Now 𝐶𝑛 =𝑐 +𝑛𝑚 ≡𝑐Cn=c+nm≡c, and 𝑝 ∤𝑐p∤c because 𝑝 ∣𝑚p∣m and gcd(𝑐,𝑚) =1gcd(c,m)=1; hence 𝑝 ∣𝑎𝑛 −1p∣an−1, so 𝑝 ∤𝑎𝑛p∤an. Thus 𝑝p cannot divide any later multiplier.

This injects infinitely many multipliers into the finite set of prime divisors of 𝑚m, a contradiction. ◻

The case gcd(𝑐,𝑚) =1gcd(c,m)=1 is the exclusion when 𝑐c and 𝑚m are coprime. The special case 𝑚 =𝑐 =1m=c=1, worked through in Example 9.1, where 𝑚m has no prime divisors at all and the first fact is immediately contradictory, is recorded separately as the case 𝑐 =𝑚 =1c=m=1.

Removing the scale. For general 𝑐c, set 𝑔 =gcd(𝑐,𝑚)g=gcd(c,m). Equation (5.1) gives 𝑔 ∣𝐷𝑛g∣Dn. Both identities are homogeneous in 𝐷,𝑐,𝑚D,c,m: dividing them by 𝑔g leaves the multipliers unchanged and gives coprime data, excluded by the previous case. ◻

The formal statements are the exclusion at every scale and the eventual exclusion. The latter follows by shifting the orbit to the first index of constant error.

Theorem 9.2 excludes an error that is eventually constant and negative, at every magnitude and every scale. Constancy fixes both the possible prime divisors and the congruence used at later indices. For varying magnitudes, even when their prime divisors belong to a fixed finite set, this argument does not supply the same later congruence. It therefore does not settle that case.
