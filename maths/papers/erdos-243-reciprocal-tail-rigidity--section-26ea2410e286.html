Reciprocal Sums and the Sylvester Recurrence

Erdős #243 By Will Cook September 2026
PDFOpen the PDF, 21 pages TeXLaTeX source Source on GitHub Problem dossier

Précis. For strictly increasing positive integers with 𝑎2𝑛/𝑎𝑛+1 =1 +3/𝑛 +𝑜(𝑛−3)an2/an+1=1+3/n+o(n−3), the reciprocal sum is irrational. The argument extracts a cubic polynomial from an integer tail numerator, restricts its coefficients using a square in a cubic field, and excludes the remaining cases modulo seven. The unrestricted Erdős–Graham question remains open.

This paper owns the cubic-rate irrationality theorem and its proof; the long paper develops the separate Sylvester-tail criteria and broader recurrence questions.

It is not authority for the validity of claims tagged Lean, which belongs to the cited kernel-checked source, or a solution to Erdős #243, which remains open.

The product 𝑃𝑛Pn counts a prime factor again each time it occurs in an earlier denominator. A least common multiple retains only its largest power, and so can give a smaller clearing denominator. Tijdeman and Yuan extend this type of criterion to positive numerators [4]. Here any finite upper bound suffices under the quadratic-limit assumption. Since 𝐴𝑛 ∣𝑃𝑛An∣Pn, the LCM hypothesis is no stronger than the product hypothesis in Corollary 4.9: if the product expression is at most 𝐵B, the LCM expression is at most max(𝐵,0)max(B,0). When the earlier terms are pairwise coprime the two weights agree; repeated prime factors can make the LCM much smaller. This comparison does not assert the existence of a non-Sylvester rational example satisfying one bound but not the other.

The finite upper limit means an eventual upper bound; it does not require 𝑞q to divide the LCM of the preceding denominators. For positive integer summand numerators 𝑏𝑛bn, the modified error is 𝑉𝑛 =𝑏𝑛𝐿𝑛 −(𝑎𝑛 −1)𝑈𝑛Vn=bnLn−(an−1)Un. The latter conclusion must not be read into the first hypothesis alone. The same section links finite examples separating them; compare Badea [3] and Tijdeman–Yuan [4]. The companion’s Section 3 compares Duverney’s signed-series criterion [2], whose printed condition is one-sided, with the stronger absolute-convergence specialisation used there.
