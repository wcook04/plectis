Short paper

Integer Linear Forms for a Factorial Reciprocal Series

Erdős #68 By Will Cook 31 August 2026; revised 5 October 2026
PDFOpen the PDF, 9 pages TeXLaTeX source Source on GitHub Problem dossier

Précis. For 𝑆 =∑𝑛≥2(𝑛! −1)−1S=∑n≥2(n!−1)−1, the paper classifies finitely supported integer vectors that cancel prescribed initial weighted sums in forms MS+k. An integral basis, one Diophantine equation and a finite gcd determine the possible moments M. At cancellation depth four, the least positive moment is 1380. Irrationality by these forms still requires a nonintegral form with q dividing M for every positive q.

This paper owns the coefficient classification, finite moment gcd and depth-four construction; the long paper develops denominator exclusions, further constructions and method limits.

It is not authority for the validity of claims tagged Lean, which belongs to the cited kernel-checked source, or a solution to Erdős #68, which remains open.

Introduction

Erdős asked whether replacing 𝑛!n! by 𝑛! −1n!−1 preserves the irrationality of the reciprocal sum [1]. The question appears as Problem 68 in Bloom’s catalogue [2].

Problem 1.1 (Erdős Problem #68). Is

𝑆=∑𝑛≥21𝑛!−1S=∑n≥21n!−1

irrational?

The elementary proof that 𝑒e is irrational clears the partial sum by a factorial and bounds the remaining positive tail. Here the first step fails: for 𝐻𝑚 =∑𝑚𝑛=2(𝑛! −1)−1Hm=∑n=2m(n!−1)−1, already 3!𝐻3 =36/53!H3=36/5. We instead construct forms 𝑀𝑆 +𝑘MS+k, with 𝑀,𝑘 ∈ℤM,k∈Z, by cancelling initial weighted sums. If 𝑆 =𝑎/𝑞S=a/q and 𝑞 ∣𝑀q∣M, then 𝑀𝑆 +𝑘MS+k is integral; proving it nonintegral excludes that denominator.

Even these cancellation equations impose more than the expected common-denominator condition. Cancelling the weighted sums indexed by 2,3,42,3,4 forces 115 ∣𝑀115∣M, but a vector supported on indices 𝑛 ≥2n≥2 requires 1380 ∣𝑀1380∣M. We determine the exact additional factor at every depth 𝐷D, and construct a vector attaining the least positive value of 𝑀M.

The proof has two steps. Adjacent factorial differences, corrected at proper divisors, give an integral basis that isolates each weighted sum (Theorem 2.1). Temporarily allowing index one makes this basis triangular; removing that coordinate leaves one Diophantine equation. Its gcd initially involves infinitely many coefficients. Theorem 3.1 reduces it to a specified finite interval, not an observed stabilization.

The classification is independent of rationality. The attaining example 𝑀 =1380M=1380 also passes a separate nonintegrality test. For an irrationality proof, however, the divisibility 𝑞 ∣𝑀q∣M and nonintegrality must hold for the same vector, for every 𝑞 >0q>0. The companion’s progression construction provides the divisibility; its nonintegrality remains unproved.

Hančl and Tijdeman’s tail-integrality lemma treats factorial series with integer coefficients [4]. Their factorial-scaled partial sums are integers. Here the scaled partial sum need not be integral. Instead, the weight congruences give R −𝑀𝑆 ∈ℤR−MS∈Z (1); the assumption 𝑆 =𝑎/𝑞S=a/q and 𝑞 ∣𝑀q∣M then makes RR integral. Koepf and Schmersau’s factorial-digit criterion [3] is used in the companion’s digit argument to give an equivalent eventual-pattern condition for 𝑆 −𝑒 +2S−e+2. Failure of that pattern at arbitrarily large indices also remains unproved.

Sections 2–3 prove the classification; Section 4 constructs and tests the least-𝑀M example. Section 5 separates the remaining nonintegrality question from the freedoms in that construction.

Nonintegrality of the remainder

Fix a vector with 𝑉2 =⋯ =𝑉𝐷 =0V2=⋯=VD=0 and 𝑀 >0M>0, and choose any cutoff 𝑁 ≥𝐷N≥D at or beyond its support. Separate the signed finite sum from the positive tail:

First keep the vector fixed and increase only the cutoff. The vector −6𝑒2 +𝑒4−6e2+e4 has 𝑀 =12M=12. For any fixed vector with 𝑀 >0M>0, 𝐴𝑁AN increases to RR. If RR is nonintegral, the gap tends to a positive number and the test eventually succeeds. If RR is integral, the eventual gap equals the omitted tail, so the strict upper-bound test fails. Thus moving the cutoff detects nonintegrality; it does not create it or change the divisors of 𝑀M that are excluded.

Nor can a change of coefficients at fixed 𝑀M create nonintegrality: by (1), it changes the remainder only by an integer. The 𝑈𝑛Un corrections simplify support and weighted sums, not the fractional part.

To exclude every denominator, 𝑀M must vary. The companion’s progression construction turns cancellation into polynomial root conditions. It gives primitive vectors (their coefficients have gcd one) with 0 <𝑀 <𝑁!0<M<N! and multipliers eventually divisible by every fixed 𝑞q. At the support endpoint the tail is less than 2/𝑁2/N, but the next-integer gap may shrink too. The missing step is therefore one comparison for the same vector: for each 𝑞 >0q>0, find 𝑞 ∣𝑀q∣M and a cutoff satisfying (12). Neither divisibility nor a vanishing tail bound supplies that comparison. No minimal-support assertion is made for this family.

The companion treats the equivalent carry condition and the complementary-denominator tail inequality. It also records two larger finite computations: a non-unit carry at 𝑚 =300000m=300000 excludes every 𝑞 ∣299999!q∣299999!, and a continued-fraction enclosure excludes every 𝑞 <239990q<239990. Both computations were performed outside Lean; their algorithms, exact outputs and scope are given there. The factorial-divisibility exclusion does not exclude all denominators with only small prime factors, whose exponents may be larger. Neither finite restriction establishes the required condition at arbitrarily large indices.
