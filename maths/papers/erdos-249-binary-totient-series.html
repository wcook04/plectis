Précis

For integers 𝑘 ≥2k≥2 and 𝑒 ≥1e≥1, the totient sections n↦φ(k^j n+r) with 0 ≤𝑗 ≤𝑒0≤j≤e and 0 ≤𝑟 <𝑘𝑗0≤r<kj have an explicit rational basis of dimension 𝑘𝑒 +1ke+1. Every section is an explicit integer multiple of a retained one, and these reductions generate all integral relations, with unique coefficients. The proof combines the local formula for Euler's totient with a nonsingular evaluation matrix obtained from the Chinese remainder theorem and Dirichlet's theorem. Supplementary results concern bounded residue series and conditions for irrationality of S=∑ 𝜑(𝑛)2−𝑛φ(n)2−n. They do not establish that S is irrational.

Are these five sequences independent, and do the analogous reductions account for every relation in every base? The next theorem answers both questions; Corollary 1.3 gives the integral relation module.

An integer sequence is 𝑘k-regular if the ℤZ-module generated by its 𝑘k-kernel is finitely generated [1]. Coons proved that the totient sequence is not 𝑘k-regular for any 𝑘 ≥2k≥2 [2]. Martin’s Theorem 1 already implies the affine independence used below [4]. The theorem below identifies the finite-level basis and all integral relations.

Relations with periodic coefficients

Pairwise nonproportionality is essential here. For instance, 𝑛 +1n+1 and 𝑛 +2n+2 satisfy the hypothesis. In contrast, 4𝑛 +24n+2 and 2𝑛 +12n+1 are proportional, and 𝜑(4𝑛 +2) =𝜑(2𝑛 +1)φ(4n+2)=φ(2n+1) gives a nontrivial relation. Periodicity imposes no bound on the common period: it permits any finite periodic pattern, because one can work on each residue class separately.

Bounded residues and rationality

After reducing 𝜑(𝑛)φ(n) modulo a fixed integer, the coefficients are bounded. A single nonzero coefficient between long zero blocks then contradicts rationality by an elementary tail estimate.

Further context and formal sources

Affine independence in the earlier literature

The separation method has an earlier source: Yazdani’s proof of Theorem 2, explicitly credited there to Shallit, uses the Chinese remainder theorem and Dirichlet’s theorem to distinguish kernel sections modulo a fixed modulus [10]. For a later account see Allouche, Shallit and Yassawi [11]. Here one separates a chosen column from every other column of a finite evaluation matrix. Nonsingularity, not merely distinctness of infinitely many sections, gives linear independence.

Martin’s Theorem 1 gives another proof of the affine independence conclusion [4]. For any ordering of the nonproportional forms and any 𝐶 >0C>0, their successive totient ratios exceed 𝐶C on a set of positive lower density. In a proposed relation ∑𝑖𝑐𝑖𝜑(𝐿𝑖(𝑛)) =0∑iciφ(Li(n))=0, put a nonzero coefficient first and choose 𝐶 >max(1,∑𝑖>1|𝑐𝑖|/|𝑐1|)C>max(1,∑i>1|ci|/|c1|). At the resulting values of 𝑛n, the first term has larger absolute value than the sum of all the others, a contradiction. The proof in Section 1.1 instead constructs a finite nonsingularity certificate for each chosen family.

Regularity, Mahler equations and section maps

The totient is not 𝑘k-regular, in the sense of Allouche and Shallit [1], for any 𝑘 ≥2k≥2: Coons proved that the ℤZ-module generated by its 𝑘k-kernel is not finitely generated, by counting the poles of 𝜁(𝑠 −1)/𝜁(𝑠)ζ(s−1)/ζ(s) in the strip 0 ≤Re⁡𝑠 ≤10≤Re⁡s≤1 . For integer-valued sequences this is equivalent to infinite dimension of the rational span: if the span has dimension 𝑑d, some 𝑑d evaluations map it injectively to ℚ𝑑Qd and the module into ℤ𝑑Zd (compare the evaluation argument in [3]). Theorem 1.1 therefore recovers Coons’s nonregularity theorem [2] and gives the dimension at every finite level.
