Why certain sums of 1/(2𝑛 −1)1/(2n−1) are irrational

Erdős #257 By Will Cook 12 October 2026 Authorship, AI use and citation
PDFOpen the PDF, 16 pages TeXLaTeX source Source on GitHub Problem page

Summary. Erdős asked whether infinitely many of the fractions 1, 1/3,1/7,1/151/3,1/7,1/15, …, each of the form 1/(2𝑛 −1)1/(2n−1) and each used at most once, can ever add up to a fraction. The paper proves that the chosen fractions add up to an irrational number whenever 1/𝑛1/n, added up over the chosen exponents n, is finite; for the exponents 2, 4, 8, 16, … this makes 1/3+1/15+1/255+1/65535+… irrational. Sections 1 to 8 explain the proof with examples; Appendix A supplies supporting details and formal-proof correspondence. The finite reconstruction here is an ordinary proof; the linked Lean and Comparator receipts establish the formal theorem and its own proof. For an arbitrary infinite choice the question is still open.

This paper covers an account of Theorem A.1 of the #257 short paper at base 2 for a reader new to the problem: a guided proof through worked examples, with supporting details, exercises and the formal-proof correspondence in appendices.

It does not establish the validity of the Lean theorem it quotes, which belongs to the cited kernel-checked source, or a solution to Erdős #257, which remains open.

The question: which infinite sums are irrational?

Every finite sum of fractions is a fraction. An infinite sum can be one too:

12+14+18+⋯=1.12+14+18+⋯=1.

Now consider

𝑆=13+115+1255+165535+…,S=13+115+1255+165535+…,

Multiply by 255255, the third denominator:

The first three terms have become the integer 103103, leaving a positive remainder. If 𝑆 =𝑝/𝑞S=p/q, with integers 𝑝,𝑞p,q and 𝑞 >0q>0, multiplying by an integer and subtracting an integer leaves an integer multiple of 1/𝑞1/q. A positive remainder must therefore be at least 1/𝑞1/q. Erdős’s question and the theorem

In a 1988 account, Erdős wrote [3] (excerpt):

“let 𝑛1 <𝑛2 <…n1<n2<…. Is it then true that ∑12𝑛𝑘−1∑12nk−1 is irrational, …”

The indices 𝑘 =1,2,…k=1,2,… count the chosen exponents 𝑛𝑘nk. In these terms, we prove:

Theorem 1. Let 𝑛1 <𝑛2 <…n1<n2<… be an infinite sequence of positive integers. If ∑∞𝑘=11/𝑛𝑘 <∞∑k=1∞1/nk<∞, then

∞∑𝑘=112𝑛𝑘−1∑k=1∞12nk−1

is irrational.

Taking 𝐴 ={𝑛1,𝑛2,…}A={n1,n2,…}, this series is exactly 𝑆𝐴SA, and the additional hypothesis is exactly ∑𝑛∈𝐴1/𝑛 <∞∑n∈A1/n<∞. The question for arbitrary infinite 𝐴A remains open [4].

The condition concerns the reciprocals of the exponents. The conclusion concerns the fractions with denominators 2𝑛 −12n−1. For the doubling exponents, the distinction is visible in one table:

By contrast, choosing every positive integer gives the divergent harmonic series in the hypothesis. Group its terms as 11; 1/21/2; 1/3 +1/41/3+1/4; 1/5 +1/6 +1/7 +1/81/5+1/6+1/7+1/8; and so on. For example,

15+16+17+18≥4⋅18=12.15+16+17+18≥4⋅18=12.

Every subsequent block doubles the number of terms and halves their smallest value, so again contributes at least 1/21/2. These repeated contributions make the partial sums unbounded. Even exponents also fail the condition, since their reciprocal sum is half the harmonic series. The theorem makes no assertion for these choices. Infinitude is essential too: 𝐴 ={2,4}A={2,4} has a finite reciprocal sum, but 𝑆𝐴 =13 +115 =25SA=13+115=25. (Question 1.)

Erdős proved the theorem in 1968 under the extra assumption that no two chosen exponents share a divisor greater than 11, and stated that this assumption could be removed without supplying the details [2]. We explain a proof without that assumption.

The proof has three stages. First, rationality forces a fixed lower bound on certain positive remainders. Next, the denominators 2𝑛 −12n−1 let us calculate those remainders explicitly. Finally, a small average guarantees at least one choice whose total remainder is below that lower bound. The doubling exponents show the construction at work before the averaging is needed. The main proof ends in Section 7.2. Appendices A.1–A.4 justify the supporting infinite-sum and divisibility facts; they can be read as needed or after the main proof. The other-base extension and formal-proof correspondence are further reading.

A rational number has a fixed positive gap

Start with 2/52/5. Multiplying it by an integer and subtracting an integer always gives an integer multiple of 1/51/5:

3⋅25−1=15,4⋅25−1=35,5⋅25−2=0,1⋅25−1=−35.3⋅25−1=15,4⋅25−1=35,5⋅25−2=0,1⋅25−1=−35.

A complete example: the doubling exponents

Return to the opening example, 𝐴 ={2,4,8,16,…}A={2,4,8,16,…}. We now know why 255255 cleared its first three terms: it is 28 −128−1, and the exponents 2,4,82,4,8 all divide 88. In our new notation that calculation is

Why averaging works

Larger multipliers need not help.

For the square exponents 𝐴 ={1,4,9,16,…}A={1,4,9,16,…}, both 𝑘 =4k=4 and 𝑘 =8k=8 are divisible by the first two selected exponents 1,41,4. All later selected exponents exceed 88, so their integer parts vanish for both multipliers. Consequently

Average complete attempts.

What the two estimates will do.

We will divide the rows into three groups. An initial finite group stays zero. A further finite group can complete its cycles if we choose 𝑀M appropriately; we will bound each such row’s average by 1/𝑛1/n. For the infinitely many remaining rows, we need a bound valid for every 𝑀M: we will prove 2𝑄/𝑛2Q/n. Once 𝑄Q is fixed, we can make the sum of these bounds as small as needed by moving far enough down the rows: the remaining reciprocals 1/𝑛1/n have arbitrarily small total. Section 7 will choose the two row cutoffs and then 𝑀M so that both nonzero groups have small total averages.

For 𝑛 ≥2n≥2 the 𝑛 −1n−1 positive entries are still increasing and have total less than 11. The beginning-portion argument in Section 6.3 therefore gives the same 2/𝑛2/n bound for consecutive entries starting at 𝑘 =1k=1. Keeping only the sampled entries then gives the bound 2𝑄/𝑛2Q/n for any sampled length 𝑀M. For 𝑛 =1n=1 the fractional parts are all zero: (𝑏𝑘 −1)/(𝑏 −1)(bk−1)/(b−1) is an integer. Both estimates required for the three groups remain valid. First fix 𝑄Q and let the averaging length tend to infinity. Each row’s average tends to its cycle mean: complete cycles have that mean, and the unfinished last cycle has bounded length, so its weight in the average tends to zero. Throughout this limit the row averages are bounded by 2(𝑄 +1)/𝑛2(Q+1)/n.

To pass from the row limits to the whole sum, choose a row cutoff so far out that the sum of these bounds beyond it is small. This is possible because 𝑄Q is fixed and ∑𝑛∈𝐴1/𝑛∑n∈A1/n converges. The same bound holds for each row’s limit, so it controls both the tails before taking the limit and the tail of the limiting row averages. Only finitely many earlier rows remain, and their limits can be taken one at a time. Combining that finite part with the two small tails justifies passing the limit through the sum.

Next consider 𝑄 =lcm⁡(1,…,𝑡)Q=lcm⁡(1,…,t). For every 𝑛 ≤𝑡n≤t, the limiting row average is zero, because 𝑛n divides 𝑄Q. All remaining limiting row averages are bounded by 1/𝑛1/n, independently of 𝑡t. To obtain one witness, the proof chooses a suitable 𝑄Q first and a sufficiently long average second.

Our cutoffs 𝑡,𝑇t,T and number 𝑀M of candidate columns make those choices explicit, using the separately proved estimate 2𝑄/𝑛2Q/n. Under the rationality assumption, both arguments make the integer 𝑞𝑅(𝑘)qR(k) strictly between 00 and 11. The finite reconstruction and its estimates are ordinary proofs provided here; the historical Lean and Comparator receipts establish the linked formal theorem and its own proof.
