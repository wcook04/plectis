Reciprocal Sums and the Sylvester Recurrence: Further Results and Proofs

Erdős #243 By Will Cook September 2026 Authorship, AI use and citation
PDFOpen the PDF, 61 pages TeXLaTeX source Source on GitHub Problem page

Précis. An increasing sequence of positive integers satisfying 𝑎2𝑛/𝑎𝑛+1 =1 +3/𝑛 +𝑜(𝑛−3)an2/an+1=1+3/n+o(n−3) has an irrational reciprocal sum: rationality would force integer tail numerators to agree eventually with a cubic polynomial, which divisibility, a cubic-field calculation, and congruences modulo seven exclude. For rational reciprocal sums under 𝑎𝑛+1/𝑎2𝑛 →1an+1/an2→1, the record also develops sufficient conditions for the eventual recurrence 𝑎𝑛+1 =𝑎2𝑛 −𝑎𝑛 +1an+1=an2−an+1, controlling either increases of an integer tail numerator or a convergent sum at steps setting new maxima. Examples separate numerical growth bounds from the exact recurrences. The cubic proof is distinguished from the theorems formalised in Lean. None of the additional bounds is derived for every rational reciprocal sum in Erdős #243.

This paper owns the complete problem-specific reasoning surface for Erdős #243, including all registered result families and their boundaries.

Finite total relative increase

We can bound a positive integer sequence by multiplying the relative increases of its terms. If their sum converges, that product is finite. Only finitely many upward integer steps can then occur, and descent finishes the argument. No denominator recurrence is needed. For comparison, 𝐶𝑛 =𝑛 +1Cn=n+1 and 𝐶𝑛 =(𝑛 +1)2Cn=(n+1)2 have relative increases tending to zero but divergent sums. The eventual Sylvester recurrence gives an eventually constant numerator, so it satisfies the summability condition.

∞∑𝑛=0(−𝐸𝑛)+𝐶𝑛<∞,∑n=0∞(−En)+Cn<∞,

then 𝐸𝑛 =0En=0 eventually. For an exact reciprocal-tail orbit this implies 𝑎𝑛+1 =𝑎2𝑛 −𝑎𝑛 +1an+1=an2−an+1 eventually. No hypothesis |𝐸𝑛|/𝐶𝑛 →0|En|/Cn→0 is required.

Choose an integer upper bound 𝐾K for 𝐶𝑛Cn. Each strict rise of the integer sequence contributes at least 1/𝐾1/K to ∑𝑛𝛿𝑛∑nδn, so there are only finitely many rises. The remaining positive integer sequence is nonincreasing and therefore stabilises, forcing 𝐸𝑛 =0En=0. The recurrence follows from Theorem 5.7, since 𝐶𝑛 >0Cn>0. ◻

The two indispensable features of this argument are positivity and discrete increments. With 𝐶𝑛 =1 +1/(𝑛 +1)Cn=1+1/(n+1) and 𝐸𝑛 =1/((𝑛 +1)(𝑛 +2))En=1/((n+1)(n+2)), the numerator decreases forever with zero relative-increase sum, but the errors are not integers. With 𝐶𝑛 = −𝑛 −1Cn=−n−1 and 𝐸𝑛 =1En=1, the increments are integers but positivity fails.

It may offset some relative increases; the hypothesis does not require either sum to converge separately. Unlike the scalar criterion above, this argument also uses the pseudo-greedy limit: bounded 𝑈𝑛Un and 𝑉𝑛/𝑈𝑛 =𝐸𝑛/𝐶𝑛 →0Vn/Un=En/Cn→0 make the integer 𝑉𝑛Vn eventually zero, after which 𝜌𝑛𝑈𝑛+1 =𝑈𝑛ρnUn+1=Un gives eventual constancy.
