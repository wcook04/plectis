Factorial Linear Forms and Denominators: Detailed Proofs and Rationality Criteria

Erdős #68 By Will Cook 1 September 2026; revised 7 October 2026 Authorship, AI use and citation
PDFOpen the PDF, 51 pages TeXLaTeX source Source on GitHub Problem page

Précis. The record proves liminf (log LN)/(N3/2 log N) ≥2√2/3≥22/3 for L_N=lcm(2!−1,…,N!−1), by comparing a terminal block of denominators with pairwise gcds rather than an external multiplicity theorem. Specialising a Louwsma–Martino valuation, it exhibits primes 139 and 2593 absent from all later reduced denominators. Coefficient constructions give integer forms MS+k; at fixed moment the remainder changes only by an integer. Rationality criteria use factorial digits and the next integer above a scaled partial sum; the digit argument also recovers the irrationality of e. Two exact calculations give q∤299999! and 𝑞 ≥239990 >1012038q≥239990>1012038. These exclusions do not prove irrationality.

Prime powers in reduced denominators

Let 𝑝p be a prime, and let 𝑣𝑝(𝑎)vp(a) denote the exponent of 𝑝p in a positive integer 𝑎a. We determine which prime powers of 𝐿𝑀LM survive in den⁡(𝐻𝑀)den⁡(HM). At the largest exponent, this follows by specialising Louwsma and Martino’s valuation formula for an elementary symmetric sum [4]. Indeed, ∑𝑖1/𝑥𝑖 =(∑𝑖∏𝑗≠𝑖𝑥𝑗)/∏𝑖𝑥𝑖∑i1/xi=(∑i∏j≠ixj)/∏ixi for positive integers 𝑥𝑖xi. Their lemma evaluates the numerator’s valuation; subtracting the product’s valuation gives the reciprocal-sum form. The proof below uses the least common multiple instead of the product.

The distinction is already visible in 1/3 +1/15 =2/51/3+1/15=2/5: the common denominator contains 33, but the reduced denominator does not. By contrast, 1/9 +1/45 =2/151/9+1/45=2/15 only lowers the exponent of 33 from 22 to 11. The theorem below tests whether the largest exponent is preserved; a failure of that test need not remove the prime completely. The formula is standard. Its mechanism is simple: after clearing by the lcm and reducing modulo 𝑝p, only denominators carrying the largest power of 𝑝p contribute. The factorial examples and later tail comparison concern this particular series.

Two complete cancellations.

We next ask where a prime first divides a denominator 𝑑𝑚dm. This means it divides none of 𝑑2,…,𝑑𝑚−1d2,…,dm−1; it may still divide a later denominator, so uniqueness at its first occurrence gives no uniqueness in every larger block.

Proposition 2.2 (cofinal first prime occurrences). For every integer 𝐵 ≥0B≥0 there are a prime 𝑞q and an integer 𝑚 >𝐵m>B with 𝑚 <𝑞m<q, 𝑞 ∣𝑚! −1q∣m!−1 and gcd(𝑞,𝑘! −1) =1gcd(q,k!−1)=1 for every 𝑘k with 2 ≤𝑘 <𝑚2≤k<m.

Proof. Choose a prime 𝑞 ≥𝐵! +5q≥B!+5. Wilson’s theorem gives (𝑞 −2)! ≡1(mod𝑞)(q−2)!≡1(modq), so there is a least index 𝑚 ≥2m≥2 with 𝑞 ∣𝑚! −1q∣m!−1, and 𝑚 ≤𝑞 −2m≤q−2. If 𝑚 ≤𝐵m≤B, then 𝑞 ≤𝑚! −1 ≤𝐵! −1q≤m!−1≤B!−1, contradicting the choice of 𝑞q. Hence 𝑚 >𝐵m>B, and minimality gives the coprimality assertions. ◻

The construction proves cofinality of first occurrences, but not the inequality involving 𝑞q needed for the additional two-factor test in §5. The sufficient tail inequality there does not require choosing a prime 𝑞q. Wilson reflection limits what can be inferred from a large prime factor alone: for odd 2 ≤𝑛 <𝑞2≤n<q with 𝑞 ∣𝑛! −1q∣n!−1, the identity (𝑞 −1 −𝑛)! 𝑛! ≡( −1)𝑛+1(mod𝑞)(q−1−n)!n!≡(−1)n+1(modq) gives the reflected congruence at 𝑞 −𝑛 −1q−n−1. It gives a second summand denominator only when 𝑞 ≠2𝑛 +1q≠2n+1 and 𝑛 ≤𝑞 −3n≤q−3. At the Wilson endpoint 𝑛 =𝑞 −2n=q−2, it instead returns index 11, where 1! −1 =01!−1=0 is not a denominator of this series. The reflection identity is classical; see Stewart [8].
