Excluding the Bounded Negative Part

Erdős #243 16 pp Equations typeset from the exact TeX
PDFOpen the PDF · 16 pp TeXLaTeX source Source on GitHub Problem dossier

Précis

Clearing denominators turns Erdős #243 into a centred integer error orbit. Normalised vanishing together with an eventually bounded negative part forces eventual Sylvester recurrence; for the canonical orbit, Koizumi supplies normalised vanishing. Absorption and descent are also identified with Koizumi's prior lemmas in canonical coordinates, with no priority claimed over them. Stabilisation of the common divisor and a Chinese remainder block give the final obstruction. The required negative-part bound, or the corresponding weighted record budget in the LCM formulation, remains unproved.

This paper owns the problem-specific exposition for Erdős #243: the centred integer state, negative-part exclusions, conditional theorem, and remaining analytic obligations.

It is not authority for the validity of claims tagged Lean, which belongs to the cited kernel-checked source, or a solution to Erdős #243, which remains open.

Introduction

Conventions and reading route.

Write 𝑧+ =max(𝑧,0)z+=max(z,0), take empty products and least common multiples to be 11, and allow deletion of a finite prefix. In a higher-order rate such as 1 +3/𝑛 +𝑜(𝑛−3)1+3/n+o(n−3), retain the original index 𝑛n: deleting terms does not authorise renumbering that rate. A Sylvester tail means eventual equality 𝑎𝑛+1 =𝑎2𝑛 −𝑎𝑛 +1an+1=an2−an+1, not a finite reciprocal expansion. For the main argument read the next corollary, the integer-state theorem, Section 2 and the transfer in Section 4. For 𝑎𝑛 >1an>1, the recurrence 𝑎𝑛+1 =𝑎2𝑛 −𝑎𝑛 +1an+1=an2−an+1 is singled out by the telescope

Thus a Sylvester tail has the exact value 1/(𝑎𝑛 −1)1/(an−1) beginning at index 𝑛n. The question is whether rationality and the asymptotic relation 𝑎𝑛+1 ∼𝑎2𝑛an+1∼an2 force that exact recurrence. The following result isolates a sufficient one-sided bound on the departure from it.

Corollary 1.1 (original-coordinate bounded defect). Let 𝑎1 <𝑎2 <⋯a1<a2<⋯ be positive integers, 𝑎𝑛+1/𝑎2𝑛 →1an+1/an2→1, and ∑𝑛≥11/𝑎𝑛 ∈ℚ∑n≥11/an∈Q. Put 𝑃𝑛 =∏𝑗<𝑛𝑎𝑗Pn=∏j<naj. If

then 𝑎𝑛+1 =𝑎2𝑛 −𝑎𝑛 +1an+1=an2−an+1 for all sufficiently large 𝑛n.

The upper limit is allowed to be −∞−∞; the condition means an eventual finite upper bound, not a bound on the absolute value. No monotonicity or summability of the growth defect is assumed.

The proof is given in Section 4. The full original-coordinate implication, including the canonical analytic transfer, is also formalised as the original-coordinate bounded-defect theorem. Its hypothesis is an eventual upper bound on the displayed product defect, equivalent here to the finite-upper-limit condition. The integer-state theorem below isolates the arithmetic step; it is not the whole extent of the formalisation.

The obstruction.

Clear the rational reciprocal tail as 𝐶𝑛/𝐷𝑛Cn/Dn; these are the denominator-cleared tail variables of Koizumi’s pseudo-greedy formulation [10]. On a Sylvester tail, 𝐷𝑛 =(𝑎𝑛 −1)𝐶𝑛Dn=(an−1)Cn; the integer error 𝐸𝑛 =𝐷𝑛 −(𝑎𝑛 −1)𝐶𝑛En=Dn−(an−1)Cn measures the failure of this identity. Its update 𝐶𝑛+1 =𝐶𝑛 −𝐸𝑛Cn+1=Cn−En turns a bound on negative error into a bound on upward increments. Small increments alone are insufficient. The arithmetic input is that, after the common divisor stabilises, every old multiplier must remain coprime to every later reduced numerator. The Chinese remainder theorem then produces a block of forbidden heights that no permitted jump can cross. A numerator tending to infinity would have to cross it. Section 6 adapts this crossing argument when only jumps setting new records are charged.

Write ctr⁡(𝑎,𝐷,𝐶) =𝐷 −(𝑎 −1)𝐶ctr⁡(a,D,C)=D−(a−1)C. The following integer-state theorem implies Corollary 1.1; the ordinary tail transfer is proved in Section 4. Its centring hypothesis is redundant under normalised vanishing and is retained to match the source statement.

Theorem 1.2 (bounded negative part). Let 𝑎,𝐶,𝐷 :ℕ →ℕa,C,D:N→N and 𝐸 :ℕ →ℤE:N→Z satisfy

  1. 𝑎𝑛 >1an>1 and 𝐶𝑛 >0Cn>0 for every 𝑛n;

  2. the exact dynamics 𝐶𝑛+1 +𝐷𝑛 =𝑎𝑛𝐶𝑛Cn+1+Dn=anCn and 𝐷𝑛+1 =𝑎𝑛𝐷𝑛Dn+1=anDn;

  3. 𝐸𝑛 =ctr⁡(𝑎𝑛,𝐷𝑛,𝐶𝑛)En=ctr⁡(an,Dn,Cn) for every 𝑛n;

  4. eventual strict centring: |𝐸𝑛| <𝐶𝑛|En|<Cn for all large 𝑛n;

  5. eventually bounded negative part: −𝐵 ≤𝐸𝑛−B≤En for all large 𝑛n, for some integer 𝐵 ≥0B≥0;

  6. normalised vanishing: for every integer 𝐾 ≥1K≥1 there is an 𝑁N with 𝐾 |𝐸𝑛| <𝐶𝑛K|En|<Cn for all 𝑛 ≥𝑁n≥N.

Then 𝐸𝑛 =0En=0 for all sufficiently large 𝑛n: this is the eventual bounded-negative rigidity.

The Lean proof shifts the orbit past the centring and lower-bound thresholds and applies the bounded-negative-part rigidity, with centring and bound at every index.

Problem 1.3 (Erdős #243). Let 1 ≤𝑎1 <𝑎2 <⋯1≤a1<a2<⋯ be a sequence of integers with

Then 𝑎𝑛 =𝑎2𝑛−1 −𝑎𝑛−1 +1an=an−12−an−1+1 for all sufficiently large 𝑛n.

The question is recorded by Erdős and Graham [5] and by Erdős [6], and Bloom’s catalogue lists it as Problem #243 [11].

The additional hypothesis in Theorem 1.2 is the lower bound on 𝐸𝑛En. The growth and rationality assumptions already supply the other state hypotheses after a finite shift. The nonnegative-error case, Badea’s criterion  in Koizumi’s coordinates, is integer descent [10]; the theorem allows arbitrary sign changes with bounded negative depth. The published product-weighted criterion, stated as Koizumi’s Corollary 4(1), assumes a nonpositive upper limit [10], and Corollary 1.1 allows any finite upper limit. Bado’s preprint, posted in September 2026, assumes two-sided bounded errors [7]; Theorem 1.2 needs only an eventual lower bound.

The arithmetic ingredients

Error, absorption and descent

The identities below follow algebraically from Koizumi’s state recurrence [10]. His Lemma 3 gives absorption, and his Proposition 1(2) gives the nonnegative-error descent, credited there to Badea . The integer-remainder method for Cantor series is older [12]. Hančl and Tijdeman use a finite polynomial decomposition and the resulting shifted polynomial sum to characterise rationality of polynomial Cantor series . Their polynomial hypotheses are essential to this comparison; we do not invoke an unrestricted rearrangement of arrays of integer coefficients. These are antecedents of the remainder method, not proofs of the bounded-negative Chinese-remainder obstruction used here.

Write Δ𝑛 =𝑎𝑛+1 −(𝑎2𝑛 −𝑎𝑛 +1)Δn=an+1−(an2−an+1).

𝐶𝑛+1=𝐶𝑛−𝐸𝑛,Δ𝑛𝐶𝑛+1=𝑎2𝑛𝐸𝑛−𝐸𝑛+1.Cn+1=Cn−En,ΔnCn+1=an2En−En+1.

Proof. Substitute 𝐸𝑛 =𝐷𝑛 −(𝑎𝑛 −1)𝐶𝑛En=Dn−(an−1)Cn and use 𝐷𝑛+1 =𝑎𝑛𝐷𝑛Dn+1=anDn, 𝐶𝑛+1 =𝑎𝑛𝐶𝑛 −𝐷𝑛Cn+1=anCn−Dn. ◻

Both identities are the update law and the defect identity.

Theorem 3.2 (absorption and descent). For a positive exact state with strict centring, 𝐸𝑛 =0En=0 implies 𝐸𝑛+1 =0En+1=0. For any positive integer state with 𝐶𝑛+1 =𝐶𝑛 −𝐸𝑛Cn+1=Cn−En, eventual nonnegativity of 𝐸𝑛En implies its eventual vanishing.

Proof. When 𝐸𝑛 =0En=0, the second identity makes 𝐸𝑛+1En+1 a multiple of 𝐶𝑛+1Cn+1; strict centring forces that multiple to be zero. In the second assertion, 𝐶𝑛Cn is eventually a nonincreasing sequence of positive integers, so it stabilises. ◻

Both assertions are absorption of a vanishing centred state and stabilisation of the nonnegative error.

Corollary 3.3 (two zero errors). If 𝐸𝑛 =𝐸𝑛+1 =0En=En+1=0 and 𝐶𝑛+1 ≠0Cn+1≠0, then 𝑎𝑛+1 =𝑎2𝑛 −𝑎𝑛 +1an+1=an2−an+1. Thus eventual zero error in a positive exact state implies the eventual Sylvester recurrence.

Proof. The second error identity has nonzero factor 𝐶𝑛+1Cn+1. ◻

The local and eventual forms are the local rigidity step and the eventual Sylvester recurrence.

Absorption holds after any eventual centring threshold. On a nonterminal tail it removes all subsequent zeros. Then |𝐸𝑛| ≥1|En|≥1, so normalised vanishing gives 𝐶𝑛 →∞Cn→∞.

Lemma 3.4 matches each modulus 𝑚𝑖mi to its own multiple 𝑡 +𝑖t+i inside a window of 𝐵B consecutive integers. The same forbidden block and first-crossing contradiction, under a two-sided bound on the error, appear in the proof of Bado’s Theorem 5.1 . Reduction and stabilisation

A reduced exact tail has positive 𝑢𝑛un, integer 𝑣𝑛 ≥0vn≥0, gcd(𝑢𝑛,𝑣𝑛) =1gcd(un,vn)=1, and

𝑢𝑛+1=𝑎𝑛𝑢𝑛−𝑣𝑛,𝑣𝑛+1=𝑎𝑛𝑣𝑛.un+1=anun−vn,vn+1=anvn.

Proposition 3.6 (persistent coprimality). In a reduced exact tail, gcd(𝑎𝑛,𝑣𝑛) =1gcd(an,vn)=1. Distinct multipliers are pairwise coprime, and every earlier multiplier is coprime to every later numerator.

Proof. A common prime divisor of 𝑎𝑛,𝑣𝑛an,vn would divide both 𝑢𝑛+1un+1 and 𝑣𝑛+1vn+1. Also, 𝑎𝑖 ∣𝑣𝑡ai∣vt for 𝑖 <𝑡i<t, so reducedness gives gcd(𝑎𝑖,𝑢𝑡) =1gcd(ai,ut)=1, and gcd(𝑎𝑡,𝑣𝑡) =1gcd(at,vt)=1 gives gcd(𝑎𝑖,𝑎𝑡) =1gcd(ai,at)=1. ◻

All three conclusions are the step coprimality, the pairwise coprimality, and the whole-modulus avoidance.

Proposition 3.7 (gcd stabilisation). For a positive exact state, if negative errors have bounded magnitudes along a cofinal set of indices, then 𝐺𝑛 =gcd(𝐶𝑛,𝐷𝑛)Gn=gcd(Cn,Dn) eventually stabilises. Division by its stable value gives a reduced exact tail.

Proof. Both updates preserve common divisors, so 𝐺𝑛 ∣𝐺𝑛+1Gn∣Gn+1. Moreover 𝐺𝑛 ∣𝐸𝑛Gn∣En, hence 𝐺𝑛 ≤ −𝐸𝑛Gn≤−En at a negative index. The positive divisibility chain is bounded along a cofinal set and therefore bounded everywhere; it eventually stabilises. Division by the stable value preserves both exact updates and leaves the states coprime. ◻

The stable gcd is gcd stabilisation.

In the main proof, division by the stable gcd preserves divergence and a bounded upward increment. Theorem 3.5 therefore applies to the reduced numerator: this is the eventual reduced exclusion. With normalised vanishing the same obstruction is the exclusion from normalised vanishing. Sparse changes of the gcd without eventual stabilisation are a different statement; the supporting record retains that distinction and its finite-block consequences.

A scalar finite-mass criterion

Theorem 5.1 (finite negative relative mass). Let 𝐶𝑛Cn be positive integers and 𝐸𝑛En integers satisfying 𝐶𝑛+1 =𝐶𝑛 −𝐸𝑛Cn+1=Cn−En. If
