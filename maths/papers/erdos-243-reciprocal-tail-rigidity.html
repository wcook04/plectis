Excluding the Bounded Negative Part

Erdős #243 12 pp Equations typeset from the exact TeX
PDFOpen the PDF · 12 pp TeXLaTeX source Source on GitHub Problem dossier

Précis

Clearing denominators turns Erdős #243 into a centred integer error orbit. Normalised vanishing together with an eventually bounded negative part forces eventual Sylvester recurrence; for the canonical orbit, Koizumi supplies normalised vanishing. Absorption and descent are also identified with Koizumi's prior lemmas in canonical coordinates, with no priority claimed over them. Stabilisation of the common divisor and a Chinese remainder block give the final obstruction. The required negative-part bound, or the corresponding weighted record budget in the LCM formulation, remains unproved.

This paper owns the problem-specific exposition for Erdős #243: the centred integer state, negative-part exclusions, conditional theorem, and remaining analytic obligations.

It is not authority for the validity of claims tagged Lean, which belongs to the cited kernel-checked source, or a solution to Erdős #243, which remains open.

Introduction

For 𝑎𝑛 >1an>1, the recurrence 𝑎𝑛+1 =𝑎2𝑛 −𝑎𝑛 +1an+1=an2−an+1 is singled out by the telescope

1𝑎𝑛−1=1𝑎𝑛+1𝑎𝑛+1−1.1an−1=1an+1an+1−1.

Thus a Sylvester tail has the exact value 1/(𝑎𝑛 −1)1/(an−1) beginning at index 𝑛n. The question is whether rationality and the asymptotic relation 𝑎𝑛+1 ∼𝑎2𝑛an+1∼an2 force that exact recurrence. The following result isolates a sufficient one-sided bound on the departure from it.

Corollary 1 (original-coordinate bounded defect). Let 𝑎1 <𝑎2 <⋯a1<a2<⋯ be positive integers, 𝑎𝑛+1/𝑎2𝑛 →1an+1/an2→1, and ∑𝑛≥11/𝑎𝑛 ∈ℚ∑n≥11/an∈Q. Put 𝑃𝑛 =∏𝑗<𝑛𝑎𝑗Pn=∏j<naj. If

lim sup𝑛→∞𝑃𝑛𝑎𝑛(𝑎2𝑛𝑎𝑛+1−1)<+∞,lim supn→∞Pnan(an2an+1−1)<+∞,

then 𝑎𝑛+1 =𝑎2𝑛 −𝑎𝑛 +1an+1=an2−an+1 for all sufficiently large 𝑛n.

Corollary 1 is an ordinary transfer of the integer-state theorem below, proved in Section 4. The analytic transfer is separate from the linked Lean theorem. The separate public Lean release wcook04/plectis-erdos-lean at commit 52f29ad1 states Corollary 1 itself, with the defect bounded above from some index onward, as original_coordinate_bounded_defect in PalomarCorpus/E243/Challenge.lean and proves it. That repository’s Linux replay of Palomar’s Comparator stage accepted the entry with the Lean kernel and with NanoDa (run 34782407633).

The obstruction.

Clear the rational reciprocal tail as 𝐶𝑛/𝐷𝑛Cn/Dn. On a Sylvester tail, 𝐷𝑛 =(𝑎𝑛 −1)𝐶𝑛Dn=(an−1)Cn; the integer error 𝐸𝑛 =𝐷𝑛 −(𝑎𝑛 −1)𝐶𝑛En=Dn−(an−1)Cn measures the failure of this identity. Its update 𝐶𝑛+1 =𝐶𝑛 −𝐸𝑛Cn+1=Cn−En turns a bound on negative error into a bound on upward increments. Small increments alone are insufficient. The arithmetic input is that, after the common divisor stabilises, every old multiplier must remain coprime to every later numerator. The Chinese remainder theorem then produces a block of forbidden heights that no permitted jump can cross. A numerator tending to infinity would have to cross it. Section 6 adapts this crossing argument when only jumps setting new records are charged.

Write ctr⁡(𝑎,𝐷,𝐶) =𝐷 −(𝑎 −1)𝐶ctr⁡(a,D,C)=D−(a−1)C. The following integer-state theorem implies Corollary 1; the ordinary tail transfer is proved in Section 4. Its centring hypothesis is redundant under normalised vanishing and is retained to match the source statement.

Theorem 2 (bounded negative part). Let 𝑎,𝐶,𝐷 :ℕ →ℕa,C,D:N→N and 𝐸 :ℕ →ℤE:N→Z satisfy

  1. 𝑎𝑛 >1an>1 and 𝐶𝑛 >0Cn>0 for every 𝑛n;

  2. the exact dynamics 𝐶𝑛+1 +𝐷𝑛 =𝑎𝑛𝐶𝑛Cn+1+Dn=anCn and 𝐷𝑛+1 =𝑎𝑛𝐷𝑛Dn+1=anDn;

  3. 𝐸𝑛 =ctr⁡(𝑎𝑛,𝐷𝑛,𝐶𝑛)En=ctr⁡(an,Dn,Cn) for every 𝑛n;

  4. eventual strict centring: |𝐸𝑛| <𝐶𝑛|En|<Cn for all large 𝑛n;

  5. eventually bounded negative part: −𝐵 ≤𝐸𝑛−B≤En for all large 𝑛n, for some 𝐵B;

  6. normalised vanishing: for every 𝐾K there is an 𝑁N with 𝐾 |𝐸𝑛| <𝐶𝑛K|En|<Cn for all 𝑛 ≥𝑁n≥N.

Then 𝐸𝑛 =0En=0 for all sufficiently large 𝑛n: this is the bounded-negative-part rigidity.

The eventual form follows by shifting past the centring and lower-bound thresholds; its exact source is linked in the formal-scope paragraph below.

Problem 3 (Erdős #243). Let 1 ≤𝑎1 <𝑎2 <⋯1≤a1<a2<⋯ be a sequence of integers with

lim𝑛→∞𝑎𝑛𝑎2𝑛−1=1and∑1𝑎𝑛∈ℚ.limn→∞anan−12=1and∑1an∈Q.

Then 𝑎𝑛 =𝑎2𝑛−1 −𝑎𝑛−1 +1an=an−12−an−1+1 for all sufficiently large 𝑛n.

The additional hypothesis in Theorem 2 is the lower bound on 𝐸𝑛En. The growth and rationality assumptions already supply the other state hypotheses after a finite shift. Koizumi’s nonnegative-error case is integer descent ; the theorem allows arbitrary sign changes with bounded negative depth. Bado’s author-posted preprint assumes two-sided bounded errors [7]. The comparisons concern these stated hypotheses and make no priority claim.

Proof of bounded-negative rigidity

The arithmetic ingredients are proved immediately afterwards. Normalised vanishing and integrality give 𝐶𝑛 →∞Cn→∞ on a nonzero tail, while the lower error bound gives 𝐶𝑛+1 ≤𝐶𝑛 +𝐵Cn+1≤Cn+B.

Proof of Theorem 2. Suppose not. We first remove every late zero: by Theorem 6 and its contrapositive form, 𝐸E is nowhere zero beyond the centring threshold. Two cases remain. If 𝐸E is negative cofinally, then (5) bounds those magnitudes, so Proposition 11 makes the tail gcd stabilise and the orbit may be taken reduced past that point. Hypothesis (5) with Proposition 5 gives 𝐶𝑛+1 ≤𝐶𝑛 +𝐵Cn+1≤Cn+B, a bounded rise; hypothesis (6) with 𝐸E nowhere zero gives 𝐶𝑛 →∞Cn→∞. A reduced exact tail with a divergent numerator and bounded rise is excluded by Theorem 9. It remains to treat the case that 𝐸E is eventually positive, and there the descent of Theorem 6 applies and forces 𝐸E to vanish, contradicting nowhere-vanishing. ◻

Corollary 4. Under Theorem 2, the multipliers satisfy 𝑎𝑛+1 =𝑎2𝑛 −𝑎𝑛 +1an+1=an2−an+1 eventually.

Proof. Apply the two-zero implication in Corollary 7 below. ◻

The arithmetic ingredients

Error, absorption and descent

Write Δ𝑛 =𝑎𝑛+1 −(𝑎2𝑛 −𝑎𝑛 +1)Δn=an+1−(an2−an+1).

Proposition 5 (error identities). For an exact integer state,

𝐶𝑛+1=𝐶𝑛−𝐸𝑛,Δ𝑛𝐶𝑛+1=𝑎2𝑛𝐸𝑛−𝐸𝑛+1.Cn+1=Cn−En,ΔnCn+1=an2En−En+1.

Proof. Substitute 𝐸𝑛 =𝐷𝑛 −(𝑎𝑛 −1)𝐶𝑛En=Dn−(an−1)Cn and use 𝐷𝑛+1 =𝑎𝑛𝐷𝑛Dn+1=anDn, 𝐶𝑛+1 =𝑎𝑛𝐶𝑛 −𝐷𝑛Cn+1=anCn−Dn. ◻

Both identities are the update law and the defect identity.

Theorem 6 (absorption and descent). For a positive exact state with strict centring, 𝐸𝑛 =0En=0 implies 𝐸𝑛+1 =0En+1=0. For any positive integer state with 𝐶𝑛+1 =𝐶𝑛 −𝐸𝑛Cn+1=Cn−En, eventual nonnegativity of 𝐸𝑛En implies its eventual vanishing.

Proof. When 𝐸𝑛 =0En=0, the second identity makes 𝐸𝑛+1En+1 a multiple of 𝐶𝑛+1Cn+1; strict centring forces that multiple to be zero. In the second assertion, 𝐶𝑛Cn is eventually a nonincreasing sequence of positive integers, so it stabilises. ◻

Both assertions are absorption of a vanishing centred state and stabilisation of the nonnegative error.

Corollary 7 (two zero errors). If 𝐸𝑛 =𝐸𝑛+1 =0En=En+1=0 and 𝐶𝑛+1 ≠0Cn+1≠0, then 𝑎𝑛+1 =𝑎2𝑛 −𝑎𝑛 +1an+1=an2−an+1. Thus eventual zero error in a positive exact state implies the eventual Sylvester recurrence.

Proof. The second error identity has nonzero factor 𝐶𝑛+1Cn+1. ◻

The local and eventual forms are the local rigidity step and the eventual Sylvester recurrence.

Absorption holds after any eventual centring threshold. On a nonterminal tail it removes all subsequent zeros. Then |𝐸𝑛| ≥1|En|≥1, so normalised vanishing gives 𝐶𝑛 →∞Cn→∞.

The first-crossing obstruction

Lemma 8 (consecutive multiples). For pairwise coprime integers 𝑚0,…,𝑚𝐵−1 ≥2m0,…,mB−1≥2 and every lower bound, there is a larger 𝑡t such that 𝑚𝑖 ∣𝑡 +𝑖mi∣t+i for each 𝑖 <𝐵i<B.

Proof. Solve 𝑡 ≡ −𝑖(mod𝑚𝑖)t≡−i(modmi) by the Chinese remainder theorem, then add multiples of ∏𝑖𝑚𝑖∏imi. ◻

Theorem 9 (bounded-rise obstruction). Let 𝑢𝑛 ∈ℕun∈N tend to infinity with 𝑢𝑛+1 ≤𝑢𝑛 +𝐵un+1≤un+B, for a fixed integer 𝐵 >0B>0. There is no infinite pairwise coprime family 𝑚𝑖 ≥2mi≥2 with gcd(𝑚𝑖,𝑢𝑡) =1gcd(mi,ut)=1 whenever 𝑖 <𝑡i<t.

Proof. Choose 𝑚0,…,𝑚𝐵−1m0,…,mB−1 and use Lemma 8 to find 𝑡 >max(𝑢0,…,𝑢𝐵)t>max(u0,…,uB) with 𝑚𝑖 ∣𝑡 +𝑖mi∣t+i. Since 𝑢𝑛 →∞un→∞, there is a first 𝑛 >𝐵n>B with 𝑢𝑛 ≥𝑡un≥t. Minimality and the rise bound give

𝑡≤𝑢𝑛≤𝑢𝑛−1+𝐵<𝑡+𝐵.t≤un≤un−1+B<t+B.

Hence 𝑢𝑛 =𝑡 +𝑖un=t+i for some 0 ≤𝑖 <𝐵 <𝑛0≤i<B<n, so 𝑚𝑖 ∣𝑢𝑛mi∣un. This contradicts gcd(𝑚𝑖,𝑢𝑛) =1gcd(mi,un)=1. The argument uses first crossing, not monotonicity: the numerator may fall before it reaches the block. ◻

Reduction and stabilisation

A reduced exact tail has positive 𝑢𝑛un, integer 𝑣𝑛 ≥0vn≥0, gcd(𝑢𝑛,𝑣𝑛) =1gcd(un,vn)=1, and

𝑢𝑛+1=𝑎𝑛𝑢𝑛−𝑣𝑛,𝑣𝑛+1=𝑎𝑛𝑣𝑛.un+1=anun−vn,vn+1=anvn.

Proposition 10 (persistent coprimality). In a reduced exact tail, gcd(𝑎𝑛,𝑣𝑛) =1gcd(an,vn)=1. Distinct multipliers are pairwise coprime, and every earlier multiplier is coprime to every later numerator.

Proof. A common prime divisor of 𝑎𝑛,𝑣𝑛an,vn would divide both 𝑢𝑛+1un+1 and 𝑣𝑛+1vn+1. Also, 𝑎𝑖 ∣𝑣𝑡ai∣vt for 𝑖 <𝑡i<t, so reducedness gives gcd(𝑎𝑖,𝑢𝑡) =1gcd(ai,ut)=1, and gcd(𝑎𝑡,𝑣𝑡) =1gcd(at,vt)=1 gives gcd(𝑎𝑖,𝑎𝑡) =1gcd(ai,at)=1. ◻

All three conclusions are the step coprimality, the pairwise coprimality, and the whole-modulus avoidance.

Proposition 11 (gcd stabilisation). For a positive exact state, if negative errors have bounded magnitudes along a cofinal set of indices, then 𝐺𝑛 =gcd(𝐶𝑛,𝐷𝑛)Gn=gcd(Cn,Dn) eventually stabilises. Division by its stable value gives a reduced exact tail.

Proof. Both updates preserve common divisors, so 𝐺𝑛 ∣𝐺𝑛+1Gn∣Gn+1. Moreover 𝐺𝑛 ∣𝐸𝑛Gn∣En, hence 𝐺𝑛 ≤ −𝐸𝑛Gn≤−En at a negative index. The positive divisibility chain is bounded along a cofinal set and therefore bounded everywhere; it eventually stabilises. Division by the stable value preserves both exact updates and leaves the states coprime. ◻

The stable gcd is gcd stabilisation.

In the main proof, division by the stable gcd preserves divergence and a bounded upward increment. Theorem 9 therefore applies to the reduced numerator: this is the eventual reduced exclusion. With normalised vanishing the same obstruction is the exclusion from normalised vanishing. Sparse changes of the gcd without eventual stabilisation are a different statement; the supporting record retains that distinction and its finite-block consequences.

Transfer to a reciprocal series

Let 𝑥𝑛 =∑𝑘≥𝑛1/𝑎𝑘xn=∑k≥n1/ak, suppose 𝑥1 =𝑝/𝑞x1=p/q with 𝑞 >0q>0, and put

Quadratic growth gives 𝑎𝑛 ≥exp⁡(𝑐2𝑛)an≥exp⁡(c2n) eventually for some 𝑐 >0c>0 and

A scalar finite-mass criterion

Theorem 12 (finite negative relative mass). Let 𝐶𝑛Cn be positive integers and 𝐸𝑛En integers satisfying 𝐶𝑛+1 =𝐶𝑛 −𝐸𝑛Cn+1=Cn−En. If

∑𝑛(−𝐸𝑛)+𝐶𝑛<∞,∑n(−En)+Cn<∞,

then 𝐸𝑛 =0En=0 eventually. No denominator dynamics or normalised vanishing is required.

Proof. Set 𝛿𝑛 =( −𝐸𝑛)+/𝐶𝑛δn=(−En)+/Cn. Then

𝐶𝑁≤𝐶0∏𝑛<𝑁(1+𝛿𝑛)≤𝐶0exp(∑𝑛𝛿𝑛).CN≤C0∏n<N(1+δn)≤C0exp(∑nδn).

Choose an integer upper bound 𝐾K for 𝐶𝑛Cn. Each strict rise contributes at least 1/𝐾1/K to ∑𝛿𝑛∑δn, so there are only finitely many rises. The remaining positive integer sequence is nonincreasing and stabilises. ◻

The product argument is the summable relative growth; its specialisation to negative mass is the vanishing from finite negative mass, and the statement giving the recurrence directly is the Sylvester recurrence from summable negative mass. The elementary growth comparison is tail growth.

On an exact reciprocal-tail orbit the conclusion gives the Sylvester recurrence. The scalar criterion and bounded negative error are distinct quantities to estimate; on the common exact-orbit class with normalised vanishing they are each equivalent to the same endpoint.

Weighted first crossings of LCM records

Only steps setting an LCM numerator record need contribute. The first-crossing argument permits subtraction of a fixed baseline and any decreasing weight with divergent integral. The theorem in this section has an ordinary proof.

Set

𝐿𝑛=lcm⁡(𝑞,𝑎1,…,𝑎𝑛−1),𝑀𝑛=𝐷𝑛/𝐿𝑛,𝑈𝑛=𝐶𝑛/𝑀𝑛,𝑉𝑛=𝐸𝑛/𝑀𝑛.Ln=lcm⁡(q,a1,…,an−1),Mn=Dn/Ln,Un=Cn/Mn,Vn=En/Mn.

The rational tail has denominator dividing 𝐿𝑛Ln, so 𝑈𝑛Un and 𝑉𝑛Vn are integers. With 𝜌𝑛 =gcd(𝐿𝑛,𝑎𝑛)ρn=gcd(Ln,an), the exact updates are

𝑀𝑛+1=𝑀𝑛𝜌𝑛,𝜌𝑛𝑈𝑛+1=𝑈𝑛−𝑉𝑛,𝑉𝑛=𝐿𝑛−(𝑎𝑛−1)𝑈𝑛.Mn+1=Mnρn,ρnUn+1=Un−Vn,Vn=Ln−(an−1)Un.

Write 𝑅𝑛 =max𝑗≤𝑛𝑈𝑗Rn=maxj≤nUj and R ={𝑛 :𝑈𝑛+1 >𝑅𝑛}R={n:Un+1>Rn}. Strict centring already gives 𝑈𝑛+1 <𝑈𝑛Un+1<Un when 𝜌𝑛 ≥2ρn≥2, so every sufficiently late strict rise has 𝜌𝑛 =1ρn=1. The stronger eventual bound −𝑈𝑛 ≤2𝑉𝑛−Un≤2Vn, supplied by 𝑉𝑛/𝑈𝑛 =𝐸𝑛/𝐶𝑛 →0Vn/Un=En/Cn→0, gives the quantitative estimate 𝑈𝑛+1 ≤3𝑈𝑛/4Un+1≤3Un/4 when 𝜌𝑛 ≥2ρn≥2. Its actual jump is therefore 𝑑𝑛 =𝑈𝑛+1 −𝑈𝑛 = −𝑉𝑛dn=Un+1−Un=−Vn.

Theorem 13 (weighted record excess). Assume the growth and rationality hypotheses of Problem 3. Let 𝑓 :[1,∞) →[0,∞)f:[1,∞)→[0,∞) be finite and nonincreasing, with ∫∞1𝑓(𝑡) 𝑑𝑡 =∞∫1∞f(t)dt=∞. Then the sequence is eventually Sylvester if and only if, for some integer 𝐵 ≥0B≥0,

∑𝑛∈R(−𝑉𝑛−𝐵)+𝑓(𝑈𝑛)<∞.∑n∈R(−Vn−B)+f(Un)<∞.

A factorial residue reduction for forced orbits

A constant-negative error 𝐸𝑛 = −𝑚En=−m with 𝐶0 =𝑐C0=c produces 𝐶𝑛 =𝑐 +𝑛𝑚Cn=c+nm and the shape equation

In the case 𝑚 =𝑐 =1m=c=1, where (6) reads 𝐷𝑛 +1 =(𝑎𝑛 −1)(𝑛 +1)Dn+1=(an−1)(n+1), each multiplier is determined by its predecessor; we call such an orbit forced. At index 𝑛n the numerator of the next multiplier is

the forced numerator, and the divisor is 𝑛 +2n+2. The orbit survives a step when that division is exact, giving a survival predicate, the survival predicate.

Example 18 (the shape equation running until it fails). The forced orbit from 𝑎 =3a=3 reads this way: num⁡(0,3) =6num⁡(0,3)=6 is divisible by 22 and gives 𝑎1 =3a1=3, while num⁡(1,3) =13num⁡(1,3)=13 is not divisible by 33, so the orbit stops there.

Deciding survival by iteration is expensive because the orbit grows doubly exponentially, and it is unnecessary: survival over a finite horizon depends on the initial value only through a factorial residue.

Theorem 19 (factorial residue reduction). For all ℎh and all integers 𝑎 ≡𝑏(mod(ℎ +1)!)a≡b(mod(h+1)!), the orbit from 𝑎a survives ℎh forced updates exactly when the orbit from 𝑏b does.

Proof. Let 𝑀(0,𝑖) =1M(0,i)=1 and 𝑀(ℎ +1,𝑖) =(𝑖 +2)𝑀(ℎ,𝑖 +1)M(h+1,i)=(i+2)M(h,i+1), an ascending factorial with 𝑀(ℎ,0) =(ℎ +1)!M(h,0)=(h+1)!. The numerator is a polynomial with integer coefficients, so congruences transfer; reducing the modulus gives divisibility by 𝑖 +2i+2 for one exactly when for the other, and cancelling that common factor from both values and modulus leaves the inductive hypothesis at 𝑀(ℎ,𝑖 +1)M(h,i+1). ◻
