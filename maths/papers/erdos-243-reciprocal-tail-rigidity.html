Cubic-Rate Irrationality and Reciprocal-Tail Rigidity

Erdős #243 18 pp By Will Cook September 2026
PDFOpen the PDF · 18 pp TeXLaTeX source Source on GitHub Problem dossier

Précis

Every strictly increasing sequence of positive integers with a_n²/a_{n+1}=1+3/n+o(n^{-3}) has an irrational reciprocal sum. A cubic profile for hypothetical rational-tail numerators is excluded by square specialisation and congruences modulo seven. The zero-indexed theorem has a Lean proof; the paper obtains the one-based statement by an ordinary finite-prefix argument. Separately, if a_{n+1}/a_n²→1 and the sum is rational, an eventual upper bound on increments of 𝑃𝑛/𝑎𝑛Pn/an, with 𝑃𝑛 =∏𝑗<𝑛𝑎𝑗Pn=∏j<naj, forces the Sylvester recurrence eventually. That extra bound is not derived from growth and rationality alone; unrestricted Erdős #243 remains open.

This paper owns the cubic-rate irrationality theorem and the problem-specific exposition for Erdős #243: the centred integer state, negative-part exclusions, conditional Sylvester criterion, and remaining arithmetic estimate.

It is not authority for the validity of claims tagged Lean, which belongs to the cited kernel-checked source, or a solution to Erdős #243, which remains open.

Introduction

For integers 𝑎𝑛 >1an>1, the Sylvester recurrence 𝑎𝑛+1 =𝑎2𝑛 −𝑎𝑛 +1an+1=an2−an+1 gives the telescoping identity

1𝑎𝑛−1=1𝑎𝑛+1𝑎𝑛+1−1.1an−1=1an+1an+1−1.

We call a sequence that satisfies this recurrence eventually a Sylvester tail. Its reciprocal sum from any index 𝑛n in that tail onwards is 1/(𝑎𝑛 −1)1/(an−1). Erdős Problem #243 (Problem 1.3 below) asks whether rationality of ∑1/𝑎𝑛∑1/an and 𝑎𝑛+1 ∼𝑎2𝑛an+1∼an2 force this recurrence.

Theorem 7.2 settles the problem for one class of sequences: every strictly increasing sequence of positive integers with

𝑎2𝑛𝑎𝑛+1=1+3𝑛+𝑜(𝑛−3)an2an+1=1+3n+o(n−3)

has an irrational reciprocal sum. Such a sequence has 𝑎𝑛+1 ∼𝑎2𝑛an+1∼an2 and is never a Sylvester tail, for which 𝑎2𝑛/𝑎𝑛+1 −1 =𝑂(1/𝑎𝑛)an2/an+1−1=O(1/an). If its sum were rational, the integer numerators 𝐶𝑛Cn of Section 4 would eventually agree with a cubic polynomial in 𝑛n; a square condition in a cubic number field and congruences modulo seven exclude every such cubic. For the recurrence itself we prove the following sufficient condition.

Corollary 1.1 (bounded increments of 𝑃𝑛/𝑎𝑛Pn/an). Let 𝑎1 <𝑎2 <⋯a1<a2<⋯ be positive integers, 𝑎𝑛+1/𝑎2𝑛 →1an+1/an2→1, and ∑𝑛≥11/𝑎𝑛 ∈ℚ∑n≥11/an∈Q. Put 𝑃𝑛 =∏𝑗<𝑛𝑎𝑗Pn=∏j<naj. If

lim sup𝑛→∞𝑃𝑛𝑎𝑛(𝑎2𝑛𝑎𝑛+1−1)<+∞,lim supn→∞Pnan(an2an+1−1)<+∞,

then 𝑎𝑛+1 =𝑎2𝑛 −𝑎𝑛 +1an+1=an2−an+1 for all sufficiently large 𝑛n.

The assumption bounds the upward increments of 𝑃𝑛/𝑎𝑛Pn/an; it does not bound 𝑃𝑛/𝑎𝑛Pn/an itself. Indeed,

𝑃𝑛𝑎𝑛(𝑎2𝑛𝑎𝑛+1−1)=𝑃𝑛+1𝑎𝑛+1−𝑃𝑛𝑎𝑛.Pnan(an2an+1−1)=Pn+1an+1−Pnan.

The hypothesis is one-sided: it imposes no lower bound on the increments and does not require monotonicity. The growth hypothesis alone says that the ratio of consecutive terms of 𝑃𝑛/𝑎𝑛Pn/an tends to one; it does not bound their difference.

For a Sylvester sequence starting at 𝑎1 >1a1>1, 𝑎𝑛 −1 =(𝑎1 −1)𝑃𝑛an−1=(a1−1)Pn, so the increments tend to zero. For 𝑎𝑛 =𝑏2𝑛−1an=b2n−1, with integer 𝑏 ≥2b≥2, they are identically zero; since this sequence has no Sylvester tail, the corollary recovers the irrationality of its reciprocal sum. The bound also allows positive limiting increments: the integer sequence 𝑎1 =4a1=4, 𝑎𝑛+1 =⌈𝑛𝑎2𝑛/(𝑛 +1)⌉an+1=⌈nan2/(n+1)⌉, beginning 4,8,43,1387,…4,8,43,1387,…, is such an example. We verify this after the main proof in Section 4. A ratio tending to one alone does not give the bound: the rounded cubic-rate example in Section 7 has increments of order 𝑛2n2.

Write the rational tail as 𝐶𝑛/𝐷𝑛Cn/Dn, clearing denominators without reducing at each step. Up to a finite shift and a common positive factor, these are Koizumi’s tail numerators and denominators . The error 𝐸𝑛 =𝐷𝑛 −(𝑎𝑛 −1)𝐶𝑛En=Dn−(an−1)Cn measures departure from the Sylvester tail, and 𝐶𝑛+1 =𝐶𝑛 −𝐸𝑛Cn+1=Cn−En turns a lower error bound into an upper increment bound. If the error does not vanish eventually, the numerator tends to infinity and negative errors occur arbitrarily late. Each gcd divides every later error, so the bounded negative magnitudes force the gcd to stabilise. Dividing it out gives coprimality to every earlier multiplier. The Chinese remainder theorem then supplies a forbidden block that bounded upward jumps cannot cross without landing inside.

Here is the precise arithmetic statement. For the rational tails, Section 4 supplies every condition except (5). Condition (4) follows from (6) with 𝐾 =1K=1; listing it separately makes the hypothesis used when propagating a zero error explicit.

Theorem 1.2 (bounded negative part). Let 𝑎,𝐶,𝐷 :ℕ →ℕa,C,D:N→N and 𝐸 :ℕ →ℤE:N→Z satisfy

  1. 𝑎𝑛 >1an>1 and 𝐶𝑛 >0Cn>0 for every 𝑛n;

  2. the exact dynamics 𝐶𝑛+1 +𝐷𝑛 =𝑎𝑛𝐶𝑛Cn+1+Dn=anCn and 𝐷𝑛+1 =𝑎𝑛𝐷𝑛Dn+1=anDn;

  3. 𝐸𝑛 =𝐷𝑛 −(𝑎𝑛 −1)𝐶𝑛En=Dn−(an−1)Cn for every 𝑛n;

  4. eventual strict centring: |𝐸𝑛| <𝐶𝑛|En|<Cn for all large 𝑛n;

  5. eventually bounded negative part: −𝐵 ≤𝐸𝑛−B≤En for all large 𝑛n, for some integer 𝐵 ≥0B≥0;

  6. vanishing relative error: for every integer 𝐾 ≥1K≥1 there is an 𝑁N with 𝐾 |𝐸𝑛| <𝐶𝑛K|En|<Cn for all 𝑛 ≥𝑁n≥N.

Then 𝐸𝑛 =0En=0 for all sufficiently large 𝑛n.

Condition (5) bounds upward steps but permits sign changes and places no independent upper bound on positive errors. Condition (6) is |𝐸𝑛|/𝐶𝑛 →0|En|/Cn→0. Absorption needs only the weaker centring bound (4), not −𝐶𝑛/2 ≤𝐸𝑛 <𝐶𝑛/2−Cn/2≤En<Cn/2. The scalar example 𝐶𝑛 =𝑛 +1Cn=n+1, 𝐸𝑛 = −1En=−1 satisfies the update and conditions (5)–(6) without stabilising: the relations involving the denominator are essential.

Problem 1.3 (Erdős #243). Let 1 ≤𝑎1 <𝑎2 <⋯1≤a1<a2<⋯ be a sequence of integers with

lim𝑛→∞𝑎𝑛𝑎2𝑛−1=1and∑1𝑎𝑛∈ℚ.limn→∞anan−12=1and∑1an∈Q.

Then 𝑎𝑛 =𝑎2𝑛−1 −𝑎𝑛−1 +1an=an−12−an−1+1 for all sufficiently large 𝑛n.

The later sections give extensions and state the estimate still needed for the unrestricted problem. Proof under a lower bound on the error

We first assemble the argument. Section 3 proves its arithmetic lemmas; Section 4 constructs the integers from the rational reciprocal sum.

The arithmetic ingredients

We write 𝑧+ =max(𝑧,0)z+=max(z,0) and take empty products and least common multiples to be 11. An exact state means sequences satisfying the two recurrences and the definition of 𝐸𝑛En in Theorem 1.2; positivity and bounds on the error are stated separately. Error, absorption and descent

The error identity gives both the Sylvester recurrence and propagation of a zero error. Descent then handles an eventually nonnegative error.

Proposition 3.1 (error identities). For an exact integer state,

𝐶𝑛+1=𝐶𝑛−𝐸𝑛,(𝑎𝑛+1−𝑎2𝑛+𝑎𝑛−1)𝐶𝑛+1=𝑎2𝑛𝐸𝑛−𝐸𝑛+1.Cn+1=Cn−En,(an+1−an2+an−1)Cn+1=an2En−En+1.

Proof. Substitute 𝐸𝑛 =𝐷𝑛 −(𝑎𝑛 −1)𝐶𝑛En=Dn−(an−1)Cn and use 𝐷𝑛+1 =𝑎𝑛𝐷𝑛Dn+1=anDn, 𝐶𝑛+1 =𝑎𝑛𝐶𝑛 −𝐷𝑛Cn+1=anCn−Dn. ◻

Theorem 3.2 (absorption and descent). For a positive exact state with strict centring, 𝐸𝑛 =0En=0 implies 𝐸𝑛+1 =0En+1=0. For any positive integer state with 𝐶𝑛+1 =𝐶𝑛 −𝐸𝑛Cn+1=Cn−En, eventual nonnegativity of 𝐸𝑛En implies its eventual vanishing.

Proof. When 𝐸𝑛 =0En=0, the second identity makes 𝐸𝑛+1En+1 a multiple of 𝐶𝑛+1Cn+1; strict centring forces that multiple to be zero. In the second assertion, 𝐶𝑛Cn is eventually a nonincreasing sequence of positive integers, so it stabilises. ◻

Corollary 3.3 (two zero errors). If 𝐸𝑛 =𝐸𝑛+1 =0En=En+1=0 and 𝐶𝑛+1 ≠0Cn+1≠0, then 𝑎𝑛+1 =𝑎2𝑛 −𝑎𝑛 +1an+1=an2−an+1. Thus eventual zero error in a positive exact state implies the eventual Sylvester recurrence.

Proof. The second error identity has nonzero factor 𝐶𝑛+1Cn+1. ◻

Absorption uses strict centring at the successor index. After an eventual centring threshold, a zero therefore propagates to every later index. On a tail that is not eventually Sylvester, no zero can occur beyond that threshold. Then |𝐸𝑛| ≥1|En|≥1, so vanishing relative error gives 𝐶𝑛 →∞Cn→∞.

The first-crossing obstruction

Lemma 3.4 (consecutive multiples). For pairwise coprime integers 𝑚0,…,𝑚𝐵−1 ≥2m0,…,mB−1≥2 and every lower bound, there is a larger 𝑡t such that 𝑚𝑖 ∣𝑡 +𝑖mi∣t+i for each 𝑖 <𝐵i<B.

Proof. Solve 𝑡 ≡ −𝑖(mod𝑚𝑖)t≡−i(modmi) by the Chinese remainder theorem, then add multiples of ∏𝑖𝑚𝑖∏imi. ◻

Lemma 3.4 matches each modulus 𝑚𝑖mi to its own multiple 𝑡 +𝑖t+i inside a window of 𝐵B consecutive integers. The same forbidden block and first-crossing contradiction, under a two-sided bound on the error, appear in the proof of Bado’s Theorem 5.1 . The lemma here isolates bounded upward movement, and the gcd stabilisation below makes it applicable with only a lower bound on the error.

For example, the consecutive integers 6𝑘 +2,6𝑘 +36k+2,6k+3 are forbidden to a sequence coprime to both 22 and 33. Upward jumps of size at most 22 cannot cross this pair from below without landing in it. The following statement is the same first-crossing argument with a block supplied by the Chinese remainder theorem.

Theorem 3.5 (Chinese remainder theorem and first crossing). Let 𝑢 :ℕ →ℕu:N→N tend to infinity and let 𝐵 ≥1B≥1 be an integer with 𝑢𝑛+1 ≤𝑢𝑛 +𝐵un+1≤un+B for every 𝑛n. There is no sequence of pairwise coprime integers 𝑚𝑖 ≥2mi≥2 for which gcd(𝑚𝑖,𝑢𝑡) =1gcd(mi,ut)=1 whenever 𝑖 <𝑡i<t.

Proof. Choose 𝑚0,…,𝑚𝐵−1m0,…,mB−1 and use Lemma 3.4 to find 𝑡 >max(𝑢0,…,𝑢𝐵)t>max(u0,…,uB) with 𝑚𝑖 ∣𝑡 +𝑖mi∣t+i. Since 𝑢𝑛 →∞un→∞, there is a first 𝑛 >𝐵n>B with 𝑢𝑛 ≥𝑡un≥t. Minimality and the rise bound give

𝑡≤𝑢𝑛≤𝑢𝑛−1+𝐵<𝑡+𝐵.t≤un≤un−1+B<t+B.

Hence 𝑢𝑛 =𝑡 +𝑖un=t+i for some 0 ≤𝑖 <𝐵 <𝑛0≤i<B<n, so 𝑚𝑖 ∣𝑢𝑛mi∣un. This contradicts gcd(𝑚𝑖,𝑢𝑛) =1gcd(mi,un)=1. The argument uses first crossing, not monotonicity: the numerator may fall before it reaches the block. ◻

Only unboundedness above is needed for the first crossing. The stated hypothesis 𝑢𝑛 →∞un→∞ holds in the application to reduced tails.

Reduction and stabilisation

A reduced exact tail has positive 𝑢𝑛un, integer 𝑣𝑛 ≥0vn≥0, gcd(𝑢𝑛,𝑣𝑛) =1gcd(un,vn)=1, and

𝑢𝑛+1=𝑎𝑛𝑢𝑛−𝑣𝑛,𝑣𝑛+1=𝑎𝑛𝑣𝑛.un+1=anun−vn,vn+1=anvn.

Proposition 3.6 (persistent coprimality). In a reduced exact tail, gcd(𝑎𝑛,𝑣𝑛) =1gcd(an,vn)=1. Distinct multipliers are pairwise coprime, and every earlier multiplier is coprime to every later numerator.

Proof. A common prime divisor of 𝑎𝑛,𝑣𝑛an,vn would divide both 𝑢𝑛+1un+1 and 𝑣𝑛+1vn+1. Also, 𝑎𝑖 ∣𝑣𝑡ai∣vt for 𝑖 <𝑡i<t, so reducedness gives gcd(𝑎𝑖,𝑢𝑡) =1gcd(ai,ut)=1, and gcd(𝑎𝑡,𝑣𝑡) =1gcd(at,vt)=1 gives gcd(𝑎𝑖,𝑎𝑡) =1gcd(ai,at)=1. ◻

Constructing the integers from a reciprocal series

Assume the positivity, strict increase, growth and rationality hypotheses of Corollary 1.1, but not yet its increment bound. We use the denominator-clearing construction of [10], with the following indexing. Let 𝑥𝑛 =∑𝑘≥𝑛1/𝑎𝑘xn=∑k≥n1/ak. Write 𝑥1 =𝑝/𝑞x1=p/q with positive integers 𝑝,𝑞p,q, and put

Finite total relative increase

The next condition concerns the sum of all upward increments of 𝐶𝑛Cn, each divided by the value before that step. A Sylvester tail has no such increments after a finite index. Earlier remainder methods and related problems.

The integer-remainder method predates this argument . Hančl and Tijdeman characterise rationality of polynomial Cantor series by a finite decomposition and a shifted polynomial sum [13]; the polynomial hypotheses justify that rearrangement. Neither comparison supplies the lower-bound CRT argument. The Isabelle/HOL development of Koutsoukou-Argyraki and Li [16] formalises older irrationality criteria of Erdős and Straus; it does not treat Problem #243. The freely selectable series of Problem #270 are discussed in the companion’s Section 3, but are not used for the fixed recurrence here.

Main criterion and arithmetic lemmas.

The Lean proof of eventual vanishing under a lower error bound (Theorem 1.2) shifts past the two thresholds and applies the vanishing result under a bound on the negative part with centring and the lower bound at every index.

Proposition 3.1 corresponds to the update law and the defect identity. Theorem 3.2 uses absorption of a vanishing error and stabilisation of the nonnegative error. The local rigidity step and the eventual Sylvester recurrence give the two forms of Corollary 3.3.

The three parts of Proposition 3.6 are step coprimality, pairwise coprimality, and coprimality to each earlier multiplier. Proposition 3.7 gives eventual constancy of the gcd. The exclusions treat reduced numerators with bounded increments and the case where divergence follows from vanishing relative error. The main proof uses both divergence of the reduced numerator and a bound on every upward increment. A cofinal bound on negative errors suffices for gcd stabilisation, not for that increment bound. Companion Section 11 also distinguishes stabilisation from a density-zero bound on strict gcd increases; the latter is not used here.

The scalar criterion.

The scalar declaration proves vanishing for a positive integer sequence with summable relative increases (Theorem 5.1); its exact-orbit consequence is the Sylvester recurrence under the same assumption. These declarations concern integer 𝐶𝑛Cn, not the real-initial-value observation following the proof. Formulations that also assume denominator dynamics and vanishing relative error give a growth bound for the numerator, eventual zero error under the convergence assumption, and the resulting Sylvester recurrence.

The Lean theorem excluding a periodic negative error (Theorem 10.1 of the companion) assumes that each error magnitude is smaller than its multiplier. For periodic positive magnitudes and multipliers 𝑎𝑛 ≥2an≥2, the companion derives this bound eventually from the exact recurrences. For general errors, vanishing relative error supplies strict centring eventually, but control of the negative part is still needed in Theorem 1.2.

A constant-negative error 𝐸𝑛 = −𝑚En=−m with 𝐶0 =𝑐C0=c produces 𝐶𝑛 =𝑐 +𝑛𝑚Cn=c+nm and the shape equation

In the case 𝑚 =𝑐 =1m=c=1, where (6) reads 𝐷𝑛 +1 =(𝑎𝑛 −1)(𝑛 +1)Dn+1=(an−1)(n+1), each multiplier is determined by its predecessor; we call such an orbit forced. At index 𝑛n the numerator of the next multiplier is

Example B.1 (the shape equation running until it fails). The forced orbit from 𝑎 =3a=3 reads this way: num⁡(0,3) =6num⁡(0,3)=6 is divisible by 22 and gives 𝑎1 =3a1=3, while num⁡(1,3) =13num⁡(1,3)=13 is not divisible by 33, so the orbit stops there.
