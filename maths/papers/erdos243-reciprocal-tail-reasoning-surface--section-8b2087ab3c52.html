Reciprocal Sums and the Sylvester Recurrence: Further Results and Proofs

Erdős #243 By Will Cook September 2026
PDFOpen the PDF, 61 pages TeXLaTeX source Source on GitHub Problem dossier

Précis. An increasing sequence of positive integers satisfying 𝑎2𝑛/𝑎𝑛+1 =1 +3/𝑛 +𝑜(𝑛−3)an2/an+1=1+3/n+o(n−3) has an irrational reciprocal sum: rationality would force integer tail numerators to agree eventually with a cubic polynomial, which divisibility, a cubic-field calculation, and congruences modulo seven exclude. For rational reciprocal sums under 𝑎𝑛+1/𝑎2𝑛 →1an+1/an2→1, the record also develops sufficient conditions for the eventual recurrence 𝑎𝑛+1 =𝑎2𝑛 −𝑎𝑛 +1an+1=an2−an+1, controlling either increases of an integer tail numerator or a convergent sum at steps setting new maxima. Examples separate numerical growth bounds from the exact recurrences. The cubic proof is distinguished from the theorems formalised in Lean. None of the additional bounds is derived for every rational reciprocal sum in Erdős #243.

Construction from the reciprocal sum.

Let 𝑇𝑛 =∑𝑘≥𝑛1/𝑎𝑘Tn=∑k≥n1/ak and suppose 𝑇0 ∈ℚT0∈Q. Put 𝐷0D0 equal to a common denominator and 𝐷𝑛 =𝐷0𝑎0⋯𝑎𝑛−1Dn=D0a0⋯an−1, and set 𝐶𝑛 =𝐷𝑛𝑇𝑛Cn=DnTn. Then 𝐶𝑛 ∈ℤCn∈Z for every 𝑛n, and from 𝑇𝑛 =1/𝑎𝑛 +𝑇𝑛+1Tn=1/an+Tn+1 one gets 𝐶𝑛+1 =𝑎𝑛𝐶𝑛 −𝐷𝑛Cn+1=anCn−Dn, which is the tail update. On Sylvester’s sequence 𝑇𝑛 =1/(𝑎𝑛 −1)Tn=1/(an−1) exactly, so 𝐷𝑛 =(𝑎𝑛 −1)𝐶𝑛Dn=(an−1)Cn and 𝐸𝑛 =0En=0: the error measures deviation from the telescoping tail identity, and it vanishes identically on the Sylvester orbit.

In Lean, the elementary identities are stated for an abstract integer system, and their application to a rational reciprocal sum has separate statements, including the product condition for a rational reciprocal sum. This passage uses the tail realisation, the denominator realisation and the signed update.

Conversely, the recurrences identify a reciprocal sum when the ratio 𝐶𝑛/𝐷𝑛Cn/Dn tends to zero. Regard that ratio as a real number, the tail ratio. Assume 𝑎𝑛 >0an>0, 𝐷𝑛 >0Dn>0, 𝐶𝑛+1 +𝐷𝑛 =𝑎𝑛𝐶𝑛Cn+1+Dn=anCn and 𝐷𝑛+1 =𝑎𝑛𝐷𝑛Dn+1=anDn. Dividing the numerator update by 𝑎𝑛𝐷𝑛anDn gives 𝐶𝑛/𝐷𝑛 =1/𝑎𝑛 +𝐶𝑛+1/𝐷𝑛+1Cn/Dn=1/an+Cn+1/Dn+1, the one-step reciprocal identity. Iterating gives

as in the finite telescoping identity. If 𝐶𝑁/𝐷𝑁 →0CN/DN→0, taking the limit identifies ∑𝑛1/𝑎𝑛 =𝐶0/𝐷0∑n1/an=C0/D0, the series realisation. No growth, error bound or separate rationality hypothesis is used in these three identities. Positivity and the stated limit of 𝐶𝑛/𝐷𝑛Cn/Dn are still required. They prove the direction from recurrences to a series; the construction from a rational reciprocal sum was given above and is also formalised in separate declarations.

Under the relative-error hypothesis used below, the required limit is automatic. Suppose 𝑎𝑛 >1an>1, 𝐶𝑛 >0Cn>0, 𝐷𝑛 ≥0Dn≥0 and 𝐸𝑛/𝐶𝑛 →0En/Cn→0 on an exact integer orbit. If 𝐷0 =0D0=0, then 𝐷𝑛 =0Dn=0 and 𝐸𝑛/𝐶𝑛 =1 −𝑎𝑛 ≤ −1En/Cn=1−an≤−1 at every index, a contradiction. Thus 𝐷0 ≥1D0≥1 and 𝐷𝑛 ≥𝐷02𝑛Dn≥D02n. For each 𝜀 >0ε>0, the update gives 𝐶𝑛+1 ≤(1 +𝜀)𝐶𝑛Cn+1≤(1+ε)Cn eventually. Taking 0 <𝜀 <10<ε<1 shows that 𝐶𝑛/𝐷𝑛 →0Cn/Dn→0, so the telescoping identity realises the reciprocal sum as 𝐶0/𝐷0C0/D0.

The same assumptions also imply the quadratic growth limit. Put 𝜃𝑛 =𝐸𝑛/𝐶𝑛θn=En/Cn. Then 𝑎𝑛 =𝐷𝑛/𝐶𝑛 +1 −𝜃𝑛 →∞an=Dn/Cn+1−θn→∞, and the exact recurrences give

𝑎𝑛+1𝑎2𝑛=11−𝜃𝑛−1𝑎𝑛+1−𝜃𝑛+1𝑎2𝑛⟶1.an+1an2=11−θn−1an+1−θn+1an2⟶1.

In particular, the multipliers are strictly increasing eventually. Thus a global orbit with these positivity and relative-error assumptions already gives a sequence of the type in Problem 1.1 after deletion of a finite prefix. No separate tail-limit estimate is needed for such an orbit; without the relative-error assumption, that estimate remains a condition of the general telescoping argument.

Proposition 4.3 (scaling the numerator and denominator). For every 𝑠,𝑎,𝐷,𝐶 ∈ℤs,a,D,C∈Z,

𝑎(𝑠𝐷)=𝑠(𝑎𝐷),𝑎(𝑠𝐶)−𝑠𝐷=𝑠(𝑎𝐶−𝐷),a(sD)=s(aD),a(sC)−sD=s(aC−D),
𝑠𝐷−(𝑎−1)𝑠𝐶=𝑠[𝐷−(𝑎−1)𝐶].sD−(a−1)sC=s[D−(a−1)C].

Multiplying the numerator and denominator by the same factor therefore preserves the recurrence and scales the error by that factor. When the factor divides all entries, we can divide it out instead. This is used in the finite calculation of Appendix B and in the induction in Theorem 10.1.
