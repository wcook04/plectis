Reciprocal Sums and the Sylvester Recurrence: Further Results and Proofs

Erdős #243 By Will Cook September 2026 Authorship, AI use and citation
PDFOpen the PDF, 61 pages TeXLaTeX source Source on GitHub Problem page

Précis. An increasing sequence of positive integers satisfying 𝑎2𝑛/𝑎𝑛+1 =1 +3/𝑛 +𝑜(𝑛−3)an2/an+1=1+3/n+o(n−3) has an irrational reciprocal sum: rationality would force integer tail numerators to agree eventually with a cubic polynomial, which divisibility, a cubic-field calculation, and congruences modulo seven exclude. For rational reciprocal sums under 𝑎𝑛+1/𝑎2𝑛 →1an+1/an2→1, the record also develops sufficient conditions for the eventual recurrence 𝑎𝑛+1 =𝑎2𝑛 −𝑎𝑛 +1an+1=an2−an+1, controlling either increases of an integer tail numerator or a convergent sum at steps setting new maxima. Examples separate numerical growth bounds from the exact recurrences. The cubic proof is distinguished from the theorems formalised in Lean. None of the additional bounds is derived for every rational reciprocal sum in Erdős #243.

This paper owns the complete problem-specific reasoning surface for Erdős #243, including all registered result families and their boundaries.

It is not authority for the validity of claims tagged Lean, which belongs to the cited kernel-checked source, or a solution to Erdős #243, which remains open.

Bounded increases and coprimality

For nonperiodic errors, the Chinese remainder theorem replaces the fixed period. It produces a block of consecutive forbidden values. An unbounded numerator with bounded upward steps must enter that block when it first crosses its lower end.

Lemma 11.1 (shifted blocks of consecutive multiples). Let 𝑚0,…,𝑚𝐵−1m0,…,mB−1 be pairwise coprime and at least 22. For every bound there is a 𝑡t beyond it with 𝑚𝑖 ∣𝑡 +𝑖mi∣t+i for each 𝑖 <𝐵i<B.

Proof. Solve 𝑡 ≡ −𝑖(mod𝑚𝑖)t≡−i(modmi) simultaneously by the Chinese remainder theorem. Adding a sufficiently large multiple of ∏𝑖𝑚𝑖∏imi puts the solution beyond the prescribed bound. ◻

Lemma 11.1 matches each modulus 𝑚𝑖mi to its own multiple 𝑡 +𝑖t+i inside a window of 𝐵B consecutive integers. Matchings of a set of integers to distinct multiples in an interval are the subject of Erdős Problem #650, solved by van Doorn, Li and Tang: for every set 𝑆S of 𝑘k positive integers, every open interval of length 2max𝑆2maxS contains distinct multiples of at least min(𝑘,⌈2√𝑘 ⌉)min(k,⌈2k⌉) elements of 𝑆S, and this count is optimal [11]. Their extremal construction uses the same Chinese remaindering for moduli that need not be pairwise coprime, with their Claim 3.2 supplying the divisibility of residue differences by greatest common divisors that the generalised theorem requires [11]. The first-crossing argument below needs only the pairwise coprime case.

Example 11.2 (a block of three consecutive multiples). Take 𝐵 =3B=3 and (𝑚0,𝑚1,𝑚2) =(3,4,5)(m0,m1,m2)=(3,4,5). The congruences 𝑡 ≡0(mod3)t≡0(mod3), 𝑡 ≡3(mod4)t≡3(mod4) and 𝑡 ≡3(mod5)t≡3(mod5) hold exactly when 𝑡 ≡3(mod60)t≡3(mod60). At 𝑡 =3t=3 the block is 3,4,53,4,5 with 3 ∣33∣3, 4 ∣44∣4 and 5 ∣55∣5; at 𝑡 =63t=63 it is 63,64,6563,64,65 with 3 ∣633∣63, 4 ∣644∣64 and 5 ∣655∣65. A sequence of natural numbers that starts at 00, tends to infinity and rises by at most 33 at each step cannot step over the block {3,4,5}{3,4,5}: it has a first value at least 33, that value is at most 55, and every member of {3,4,5}{3,4,5} is divisible by one of the three moduli.

Theorem 11.3 (bounded increases and coprimality to earlier moduli). Let 𝑢 :ℕ →ℕu:N→N tend to infinity with 𝑢𝑛+1 ≤𝑢𝑛 +𝐵un+1≤un+B for a fixed integer 𝐵 ≥1B≥1. Then 𝑢u cannot remain coprime to infinitely many fresh pairwise coprime moduli: there is no family of pairwise coprime 𝑚𝑖 ≥2mi≥2, one for each index, such that gcd(𝑚𝑖,𝑢𝑡) =1gcd(mi,ut)=1 whenever 𝑖 <𝑡i<t.

The CRT block must lie above the initial segment where some moduli are not yet available. No monotonicity of 𝑢u is assumed.

Proof. Choose 𝑚0,…,𝑚𝐵−1m0,…,mB−1 and use Lemma 11.1 to find 𝑡 >max(𝑢0,…,𝑢𝐵)t>max(u0,…,uB) with 𝑚𝑖 ∣𝑡 +𝑖mi∣t+i for 0 ≤𝑖 <𝐵0≤i<B. Since 𝑢𝑛 →∞un→∞, there is a first 𝑛 >𝐵n>B with 𝑢𝑛 ≥𝑡un≥t. Minimality and the rise bound give

𝑡≤𝑢𝑛≤𝑢𝑛−1+𝐵<𝑡+𝐵.t≤un≤un−1+B<t+B.

Hence 𝑢𝑛 =𝑡 +𝑖un=t+i for some 0 ≤𝑖 <𝐵 <𝑛0≤i<B<n, so 𝑚𝑖 ∣𝑢𝑛mi∣un. The inequality 𝑖 <𝑛i<n now permits the avoidance hypothesis, which says gcd(𝑚𝑖,𝑢𝑛) =1gcd(mi,un)=1; this contradicts 𝑚𝑖 ≥2mi≥2. The argument uses the first crossing of the CRT block, not monotonicity of 𝑢u. ◻

Only unboundedness above is used to obtain this first crossing. Divergence is stated because it holds for the reduced rational tail in the application. This distinction also explains why the weighted proof in Section 6 works with an unbounded running maximum, without assuming that its numerator tends to infinity.

Divergence forces 𝑢u to reach the forbidden block, and the uniform rise bound prevents it from jumping over the block. A bound only along a subsequence would leave the intervening steps unrestricted. Without a scale restriction on the moduli, Proposition 14.6 gives an unbounded coprime walk with rises 𝑜(log⁡log⁡𝑢𝑛)o(log⁡log⁡un). The same forbidden-block crossing, under a two-sided bound on the error, appears in the proof of Bado’s Theorem 5.1 [8], posted in September 2026.

In the application, 𝑚𝑖mi is the 𝑖ith multiplier. Its coprimality is known only for later numerators, which explains the condition 𝑖 <𝑡i<t.

To apply the preceding theorem, we reduce 𝐶𝑛/𝐷𝑛Cn/Dn to lowest terms and require that no further common factor be cancelled. In that case the reduced numerators and denominators still satisfy the original recurrences. The relevant coprimality is as follows.

Proposition 11.4 (persistent coprimality). Suppose that gcd(𝑢𝑛,𝑣𝑛) =1gcd(un,vn)=1 at every index and

𝑢𝑛+1+𝑣𝑛=𝑎𝑛𝑢𝑛,𝑣𝑛+1=𝑎𝑛𝑣𝑛.un+1+vn=anun,vn+1=anvn.

Then gcd(𝑎𝑛,𝑣𝑛) =1gcd(an,vn)=1, the 𝑎𝑛an are pairwise coprime, and gcd(𝑎𝑖,𝑢𝑡) =1gcd(ai,ut)=1 whenever 𝑖 <𝑡i<t.

Proof. A common prime divisor of 𝑎𝑛an and 𝑣𝑛vn would divide both 𝑢𝑛+1 =𝑎𝑛𝑢𝑛 −𝑣𝑛un+1=anun−vn and 𝑣𝑛+1 =𝑎𝑛𝑣𝑛vn+1=anvn, contradicting their coprimality. Hence gcd(𝑎𝑛,𝑣𝑛) =1gcd(an,vn)=1. For 𝑖 <𝑡i<t, the denominator recurrence gives 𝑎𝑖 ∣𝑣𝑡ai∣vt. Combining this with gcd(𝑎𝑡,𝑣𝑡) =1gcd(at,vt)=1 proves that 𝑎𝑖ai and 𝑎𝑡at are coprime; combining it with gcd(𝑢𝑡,𝑣𝑡) =1gcd(ut,vt)=1 proves that 𝑎𝑖ai and 𝑢𝑡ut are coprime. ◻

These are the step coprimality, the pairwise coprimality, and the coprimality to each earlier multiplier. Applying Theorem 11.3 shows that a numerator satisfying these conditions cannot tend to infinity with uniformly bounded upward increments. This is the reduced exclusion under bounded upward increments, together with its eventual form, the version with an eventual increment bound.

Example 11.5 (coprimality in Sylvester’s sequence). Put 𝑢𝑛 =1un=1 and 𝑣𝑛 =𝐷𝑛 =1,2,6,42,1806,…vn=Dn=1,2,6,42,1806,… as in Example 4.2, with multipliers 𝑎𝑛 =𝑣𝑛 +1 =2,3,7,43,1807,…an=vn+1=2,3,7,43,1807,…. Then gcd(𝑢𝑛,𝑣𝑛) =1gcd(un,vn)=1, 𝑢𝑛+1 +𝑣𝑛 =1 +𝑣𝑛 =𝑎𝑛𝑢𝑛un+1+vn=1+vn=anun and 𝑣𝑛+1 =𝑎𝑛𝑣𝑛vn+1=anvn, so Proposition 11.4 applies and gives the classical fact that Sylvester’s numbers are pairwise coprime. Its numerator is constant, so it satisfies every hypothesis of the exclusion just stated except divergence; since the tail exists, that hypothesis cannot be dropped.

The fraction 𝐶𝑛/𝐷𝑛Cn/Dn in Section 4 need not be in lowest terms. To use the preceding argument, we must show that the common factor stops changing. One uniform bound on the negative magnitudes at arbitrarily late indices suffices: each gcd divides every later gcd, and at a negative index it also divides the nonzero magnitude. Thus a bound at arbitrarily late negative indices bounds the entire gcd sequence. The next proposition justifies dividing by its eventual value.

Proposition 11.6 (the tail gcd stabilises). Let 𝑎,𝐷,𝐶 :ℕ →ℕa,D,C:N→N satisfy 𝐶𝑛+1 +𝐷𝑛 =𝑎𝑛𝐶𝑛Cn+1+Dn=anCn and 𝐷𝑛+1 =𝑎𝑛𝐷𝑛Dn+1=anDn, and put 𝐸𝑛 =𝐷𝑛 −(𝑎𝑛 −1)𝐶𝑛En=Dn−(an−1)Cn. Suppose some fixed integer 𝐵 ≥1B≥1 satisfies −𝐵 ≤𝐸𝑛 <0−B≤En<0 at infinitely many indices. Then gcd(𝐶𝑛,𝐷𝑛)gcd(Cn,Dn) is eventually constant. Dividing 𝐶𝑛,𝐷𝑛Cn,Dn by its eventual value gives coprime sequences satisfying the same recurrences.

Proof. First, 𝐶𝑛 >0Cn>0 at every index. If 𝐶𝑛 =0Cn=0, the nonnegative recurrence forces 𝐶𝑛+1 =𝐷𝑛 =0Cn+1=Dn=0, and both sequences then vanish at all later indices. This contradicts the existence of arbitrarily late negative errors.

The two recurrences give 𝐺𝑛 ∣𝐺𝑛+1Gn∣Gn+1 for 𝐺𝑛 =gcd(𝐶𝑛,𝐷𝑛)Gn=gcd(Cn,Dn), as in the gcd divisibility relation. Also 𝐺𝑛 =gcd(𝐶𝑛,|𝐸𝑛|)Gn=gcd(Cn,|En|), since 𝐷𝑛 =𝐸𝑛 +(𝑎𝑛 −1)𝐶𝑛Dn=En+(an−1)Cn; at a negative index this is the gcd identity with the negative magnitude. For any 𝑛n, choose 𝑡 ≥𝑛t≥n with −𝐵 ≤𝐸𝑡 <0−B≤Et<0. Then

𝐺𝑛≤𝐺𝑡≤|𝐸𝑡|≤𝐵.Gn≤Gt≤|Et|≤B.

The entire positive divisibility chain is therefore bounded and eventually constant, by stabilisation of a bounded divisibility chain. In Lean this is the gcd stabilisation result. Dividing both sequences by the eventual value preserves the recurrences and gives coprime numerator and denominator at every later index. ◻

Other negative errors may exceed 𝐵B in magnitude. Gcd stabilisation uses the bounded witnesses, whereas the CRT application needs a bound on every upward increment.

Vanishing relative error also gives sparsity of the strict gcd increases. The next proposition asserts long finite intervals of constancy, not constancy on an infinite tail. For 𝑎,𝐶,𝐷 :ℕ →ℕa,C,D:N→N satisfying (19) with 𝐶𝑛 >0Cn>0, put

𝐺𝑛=gcd(𝐶𝑛,𝐷𝑛),Γ(𝑁)=#{0≤𝑗<𝑁:𝐺𝑗<𝐺𝑗+1}.Gn=gcd(Cn,Dn),Γ(N)=#{0≤j<N:Gj<Gj+1}.

Proposition 11.7 (vanishing relative error makes strict gcd changes sparse). Let 𝑎,𝐶,𝐷 :ℕ →ℕa,C,D:N→N satisfy 𝐶𝑛+1 +𝐷𝑛 =𝑎𝑛𝐶𝑛Cn+1+Dn=anCn and 𝐷𝑛+1 =𝑎𝑛𝐷𝑛Dn+1=anDn with 𝐶𝑛 >0Cn>0, and put 𝐸𝑛 =𝐷𝑛 −(𝑎𝑛 −1)𝐶𝑛En=Dn−(an−1)Cn, 𝐺𝑛 =gcd(𝐶𝑛,𝐷𝑛)Gn=gcd(Cn,Dn) and Γ(𝑁) =#{0 ≤𝑗 <𝑁 :𝐺𝑗 <𝐺𝑗+1}Γ(N)=#{0≤j<N:Gj<Gj+1}. If |𝐸𝑛|/𝐶𝑛 →0|En|/Cn→0, then Γ(𝑁) =𝑜(𝑁)Γ(N)=o(N). Moreover, for every starting bound 𝐵B and block length 𝐿L, some 𝑛 ≥𝐵n≥B satisfies

𝐺𝑛=𝐺𝑛+1=⋯=𝐺𝑛+𝐿.Gn=Gn+1=⋯=Gn+L.

The first assertion is the sublinear strict-growth theorem; the second is the arbitrarily late constant-block theorem. The proof first converts vanishing relative error into subexponential growth of 𝐶𝑛Cn. Each strict divisibility increase of 𝐺𝑛Gn contributes a factor of at least 22, so 2Γ(𝑁)𝐺0 ≤𝐺𝑁 ≤𝐶𝑁2Γ(N)G0≤GN≤CN. Taking logarithms gives Γ(𝑁) =𝑜(𝑁)Γ(N)=o(N). For 𝐿 =0L=0 the second assertion is immediate. For 𝐿 ≥1L≥1, if every block of 𝐿L successive transitions beyond 𝐵B contained a strict increase, disjoint such blocks would give Γ(𝑁) ≥(𝑁 −𝐵)/𝐿 −𝑂(1)Γ(N)≥(N−B)/L−O(1), a contradiction.

The starting index may depend on the block length; arbitrarily long constant blocks do not give an infinite constant tail. Proposition 11.6 needs additional bounded negative witnesses, which Proposition 11.7 does not supply.
