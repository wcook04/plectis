Reciprocal Sums and the Sylvester Recurrence: Further Results and Proofs

Erdős #243 By Will Cook September 2026
PDFOpen the PDF, 61 pages TeXLaTeX source Source on GitHub Problem dossier

Précis. An increasing sequence of positive integers satisfying 𝑎2𝑛/𝑎𝑛+1 =1 +3/𝑛 +𝑜(𝑛−3)an2/an+1=1+3/n+o(n−3) has an irrational reciprocal sum: rationality would force integer tail numerators to agree eventually with a cubic polynomial, which divisibility, a cubic-field calculation, and congruences modulo seven exclude. For rational reciprocal sums under 𝑎𝑛+1/𝑎2𝑛 →1an+1/an2→1, the record also develops sufficient conditions for the eventual recurrence 𝑎𝑛+1 =𝑎2𝑛 −𝑎𝑛 +1an+1=an2−an+1, controlling either increases of an integer tail numerator or a convergent sum at steps setting new maxima. Examples separate numerical growth bounds from the exact recurrences. The cubic proof is distinguished from the theorems formalised in Lean. None of the additional bounds is derived for every rational reciprocal sum in Erdős #243.

Polynomial extraction at a regular rate

It remains to connect the arithmetic exclusion with an asymptotic rate. For the cubic rate, the natural comparison is 𝑛(𝑛 +1)(𝑛 +2)n(n+1)(n+2); its successive ratio is exactly 1 +3/𝑛1+3/n. For a real parameter 𝜆 >1λ>1, the corresponding comparison sequence is Γ(𝑛 +𝜆)/Γ(𝑛)Γ(n+λ)/Γ(n), with ratio 1 +𝜆/𝑛1+λ/n. The argument first leaves an error 𝑜(𝑛)o(n). Subtracting the two recurrences then shows that the first difference of that error tends to zero. A sufficiently high difference of the original integer sequence is therefore a small integer and must vanish. Its eventual polynomial growth also forces 𝜆λ to be integral.

Lemma 2.11 (integer extraction at a regular rate). Let 𝜆 >1λ>1 and let 𝐶𝑛Cn be positive integers with

𝐶𝑛+1𝐶𝑛=1+𝜆𝑛+𝑜(𝑛−𝜆).(17)(17)Cn+1Cn=1+λn+o(n−λ).

Then 𝜆λ is an integer 𝑑 ≥2d≥2, and there are 𝐴 ∈ℚ>0A∈Q>0 and 𝐵 ∈ℚB∈Q with 𝐶𝑛 =𝐴 𝑛(𝑛 +1)⋯(𝑛 +𝑑 −1) +𝐵Cn=An(n+1)⋯(n+d−1)+B for all large 𝑛n.

Proof. Put 𝐹𝑛 =Γ(𝑛 +𝜆)/Γ(𝑛)Fn=Γ(n+λ)/Γ(n), so that 𝐹𝑛+1/𝐹𝑛 =1 +𝜆/𝑛Fn+1/Fn=1+λ/n exactly and 𝐹𝑛 ∼𝑛𝜆Fn∼nλ by the gamma-ratio asymptotic [20]. Write 𝜀𝑛 =𝑜(𝑛−𝜆)εn=o(n−λ) for the error in (17) and 𝑧𝑛 =𝐶𝑛/𝐹𝑛zn=Cn/Fn, so that 𝑧𝑛+1/𝑧𝑛 =1 +𝜀𝑛/(1 +𝜆/𝑛)zn+1/zn=1+εn/(1+λ/n). Since 𝜆 >1λ>1 the errors are absolutely summable, so the product converges and 𝑧𝑛 →𝐾zn→K for some 𝐾 >0K>0, with 𝑧𝑛 −𝐾 =𝑜(𝑛1−𝜆)zn−K=o(n1−λ). To see the stated error, put 𝜂𝑛 =sup𝑘≥𝑛𝑘𝜆|𝜀𝑘| →0ηn=supk≥nkλ|εk|→0; the tail of the logarithmic product has absolute value at most 𝑂(𝜂𝑛∑𝑘≥𝑛𝑘−𝜆) =𝑜(𝑛1−𝜆)O(ηn∑k≥nk−λ)=o(n1−λ). The factors are positive eventually, so the limiting product is nonzero. Hence

𝛿𝑛:=𝐶𝑛−𝐾𝐹𝑛=𝐹𝑛(𝑧𝑛−𝐾)=𝑜(𝑛).(18)(18)δn:=Cn−KFn=Fn(zn−K)=o(n).

We subtract the recurrences for 𝐶𝑛Cn and 𝐾𝐹𝑛KFn to obtain 𝛿𝑛+1 −𝛿𝑛 =(𝜆/𝑛)𝛿𝑛 +𝜀𝑛𝐶𝑛δn+1−δn=(λ/n)δn+εnCn. The first term is 𝑜(1)o(1) by (18), and the second is 𝑜(1)o(1) because 𝐶𝑛 =𝑂(𝑛𝜆)Cn=O(nλ). Hence Δ𝛿𝑛 →0Δδn→0. Every further difference is a finite linear combination of shifts of this sequence, so Δ𝑗𝛿𝑛 →0Δjδn→0 for all 𝑗 ≥1j≥1.

For the comparison sequence, the Gamma recurrence gives

Δ𝐹𝑛=Γ(𝑛+1+𝜆)Γ(𝑛+1)−Γ(𝑛+𝜆)Γ(𝑛)=𝜆Γ(𝑛+𝜆)Γ(𝑛+1).ΔFn=Γ(n+1+λ)Γ(n+1)−Γ(n+λ)Γ(n)=λΓ(n+λ)Γ(n+1).

Iterating this identity gives

Δ𝑗𝐹𝑛=𝜆(𝜆−1)⋯(𝜆−𝑗+1)Γ(𝑛+𝜆)Γ(𝑛+𝑗),ΔjFn=λ(λ−1)⋯(λ−j+1)Γ(n+λ)Γ(n+j),

whose right side is 𝑂(𝑛𝜆−𝑗)O(nλ−j). Choose an integer 𝑗 >𝜆j>λ; then Δ𝑗𝐹𝑛 →0ΔjFn→0, so Δ𝑗𝐶𝑛 →0ΔjCn→0. These are integers, so Δ𝑗𝐶𝑛 =0ΔjCn=0 for all large 𝑛n. Choose 𝑁N beyond this threshold. The Newton forward-difference formula gives

𝐶𝑁+𝑡=𝑗−1∑𝑖=0(𝑡𝑖)Δ𝑖𝐶𝑁(𝑡≥0),CN+t=∑i=0j−1(ti)ΔiCN(t≥0),

so 𝐶𝑛Cn agrees eventually with a polynomial with rational coefficients. Its growth 𝐶𝑛 ≍𝑛𝜆Cn≍nλ forces the degree to be 𝜆λ, so 𝜆 =𝑑λ=d is an integer, and 𝑑 ≥2d≥2 because 𝜆 >1λ>1. For that integer 𝐹𝑛 =𝑛(𝑛 +1)⋯(𝑛 +𝑑 −1)Fn=n(n+1)⋯(n+d−1) exactly, so (18) says that the difference of two polynomials of degree 𝑑d is 𝑜(𝑛)o(n), hence constant. The leading coefficient of the rational polynomial is 𝐾K, so 𝐾 ∈ℚ>0K∈Q>0; the constant difference is rational as well. This gives the stated shape with 𝐴 =𝐾A=K and 𝐵 ∈ℚB∈Q. ◻

The little-oh error in (17) cannot in general be replaced by a big-oh error. The positive integers 𝐶𝑛 =𝑛(𝑛 +1)(𝑛 +2) +( −1)𝑛Cn=n(n+1)(n+2)+(−1)n satisfy 𝐶𝑛+1/𝐶𝑛 =1 +3/𝑛 +𝑂(𝑛−3)Cn+1/Cn=1+3/n+O(n−3) and are not eventually polynomial. Likewise 𝐶𝑛 =⌈𝑛3/2⌉Cn=⌈n3/2⌉ satisfies 𝐶𝑛+1/𝐶𝑛 =1 +3/(2𝑛) +𝑂(𝑛−3/2)Cn+1/Cn=1+3/(2n)+O(n−3/2): rounding contributes 𝑂(𝑛−3/2)O(n−3/2), while the unrounded ratio has error 𝑂(𝑛−2)O(n−2). These two scalar examples prevent the proposed weakening of Lemma 2.11. Neither is asserted to satisfy the exact reciprocal-tail recurrences.

Lemma 2.12 (comparison of the numerator and denominator ratios). Let 𝑎𝑛an be strictly increasing positive integers with 𝑎𝑛+1/𝑎2𝑛 →1an+1/an2→1 and ∑𝑛1/𝑎𝑛∑n1/an rational, and let (𝐶𝑛,𝐷𝑛)(Cn,Dn) be the integer tail. Write 𝛾𝑛 =𝑎2𝑛/𝑎𝑛+1 −1γn=an2/an+1−1. Then

𝐶𝑛+1𝐶𝑛=1+𝛾𝑛+𝑂(𝑎−1𝑛),𝑎𝑛≥exp⁡(𝑐2𝑛) eventually for some 𝑐>0.Cn+1Cn=1+γn+O(an−1),an≥exp⁡(c2n) eventually for some c>0.

Proof. Since 𝑎𝑛+1/𝑎2𝑛 →1an+1/an2→1 and 𝑎𝑛 →∞an→∞, there is an 𝑁N with 𝑎𝑛+1 ≥𝑎2𝑛/2 ≥2𝑎𝑛an+1≥an2/2≥2an for 𝑛 ≥𝑁n≥N. Iterating 𝑎𝑛+1 ≥𝑎2𝑛/2an+1≥an2/2 from an index where 𝑎𝑛 >2an>2 gives 𝑎𝑛 ≥exp⁡(𝑐2𝑛)an≥exp⁡(c2n) eventually. The same doubling gives, for 𝑚 >𝑁m>N,

1𝑎𝑚 ≤ 𝑥𝑚 = ∑𝑘≥𝑚1𝑎𝑘 ≤ 2𝑎𝑚,1am ≤ xm = ∑k≥m1ak ≤ 2am,

so 𝑥𝑚 =(1 +𝜌𝑚)/𝑎𝑚xm=(1+ρm)/am with 0 ≤𝜌𝑚 =𝑎𝑚𝑥𝑚+1 ≤2𝑎𝑚/𝑎𝑚+1 ≤4/𝑎𝑚0≤ρm=amxm+1≤2am/am+1≤4/am, using 𝑎𝑚+1 ≥𝑎2𝑚/2am+1≥am2/2. Hence

𝐶𝑛+1𝐶𝑛=𝑎𝑛𝐷𝑛𝑥𝑛+1𝐷𝑛𝑥𝑛=𝑎𝑛⋅(1+𝜌𝑛+1)/𝑎𝑛+1(1+𝜌𝑛)/𝑎𝑛=𝑎2𝑛𝑎𝑛+1⋅1+𝜌𝑛+11+𝜌𝑛.Cn+1Cn=anDnxn+1Dnxn=an⋅(1+ρn+1)/an+1(1+ρn)/an=an2an+1⋅1+ρn+11+ρn.

The last factor is 1 +𝑂(1/𝑎𝑛)1+O(1/an) and 𝑎2𝑛/𝑎𝑛+1an2/an+1 is bounded, so the product is 𝑎2𝑛/𝑎𝑛+1 +𝑂(𝑎−1𝑛)an2/an+1+O(an−1). ◻

Proof of Theorem 2.2. The stated rate implies 𝑎𝑛+1/𝑎2𝑛 →1an+1/an2→1 and convergence of the reciprocal series. Suppose its sum were rational, and form the positive integer tail numerators 𝐶𝑛Cn. 𝐶𝑛+1𝐶𝑛=1+𝜆𝑛+𝑜(𝑛−𝜆).Cn+1Cn=1+λn+o(n−λ).

Proof of Theorem 2.1. The hypothesis 𝑎2𝑛/𝑎𝑛+1 =1 +3/𝑛 +𝑜(𝑛−3)an2/an+1=1+3/n+o(n−3) gives 𝑎𝑛+1/𝑎2𝑛 →1an+1/an2→1. No restriction on the rational normalisation was assumed: the proof derives the permitted primitive leading coefficient. The two residue patterns (14) and (15), the image {0,2,5,6}{0,2,5,6} modulo 77 and the condition (𝑟2 +𝑠2)2 ∣48(r2+s2)2∣48 have been calculated above.

The rationality supposition in this proof supplies the integer orbit. The rate implies 𝑎𝑛+1/𝑎2𝑛 →1an+1/an2→1 and excludes the eventual Sylvester recurrence, so the conclusion is irrationality on a restricted class of the sequences in Problem 1.1.

At the cubic rate 𝑃𝑛/𝑎𝑛Pn/an grows like 𝑛3n3 and its increment like 𝑛2n2. Thus neither the bounded-increment criterion nor Koizumi’s nonpositive upper-limit criterion applies. The rate also fails his 1 +𝑜(1/𝑛)1+o(1/n) condition, and Duverney’s signed defect series diverges. We do not decide whether the LCM-weighted criteria apply; that requires information about the repeated factors in the prefix product. Conversely, Theorem 2.3 assumes only positive integer 𝑎𝑛,𝐶𝑛,𝐷𝑛an,Cn,Dn and the recurrences (1), not the near-quadratic growth limit. The two arguments therefore retain distinct hypotheses.

In familiar approximation notation, 𝐶𝑛 =𝑞(∏𝑘<𝑛𝑎𝑘 ⋅∑𝑚1/𝑎𝑚 −∑𝑘<𝑛∏𝑗<𝑛,𝑗≠𝑘𝑎𝑗)Cn=q(∏k<nak⋅∑m1/am−∑k<n∏j<n,j≠kaj) is a linear form in the reciprocal sum with integer coefficients. These integer remainders grow like 𝑛3n3. We use the precision 𝑜(𝑛−3)o(n−3) to determine their polynomial form by finite differences, then exclude that form by the recurrence. The integer example 𝑛(𝑛 +1)(𝑛 +2) +( −1)𝑛n(n+1)(n+2)+(−1)n above explains why an 𝑂(𝑛−3)O(n−3) error alone does not justify that polynomial conclusion.
