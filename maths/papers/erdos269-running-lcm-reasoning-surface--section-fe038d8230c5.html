Running least common multiples: distinct values and multiplicities

Erdős #269 By Will Cook July 2026; revised 5 October 2026 Authorship, AI use and citation
PDFOpen the PDF, 73 pages TeXLaTeX source Source on GitHub Problem page

Précis. The reciprocal three-prime running-LCM kernel has nonsingular minors of every order: row and column rescaling leaves a two-valued matrix, and density supplies the required threshold pattern. The record computes the rank of every finite restriction and the least uniform approximation error over all matrices of finite separated rank. For the repeated {2,3,5} sum it derives an integer-coefficient tail recurrence and a residue criterion equivalent to irrationality. The unproved step is to find such windows for every positive multiplier coprime to 30 and after every prescribed start. It also gives the full two-prime comparison, following Fan's earlier Hecke–Mahler reduction, and identifies the boundary terms that survive a proposed finite-difference cancellation.

This paper owns the complete problem-specific reasoning surface for Erdős #269, including all registered result families and their boundaries.

It is not authority for the validity of claims tagged Lean, which belongs to the cited kernel-checked source, or a solution to Erdős #269, which remains open.

Boundary terms in weighted finite differences

Follow one lattice point through four successive triangles. If it lies in all four and its weight is unchanged, the coefficients (1, −3,3, −1)(1,−3,3,−1) sum to zero. If it first enters the second triangle, its coefficient is −3 +3 −1 = −1−3+3−1=−1; entry in the third gives 3 −1 =23−1=2, and entry in the fourth gives −1−1. Thus a finite difference depends both on which triangles contain the point and on changes of its weight. We separate these two effects below. Avoiding floor crossings controls the weights, but does not remove the newly added strips.

What the Hecke–Mahler comparison does and does not supply.

In the published Luca–Ouaknine–Worrell article [26], the same fixed integer coefficients are applied to shifts of one integer sequence. The resulting nonzero terms must have expanding gaps and uniform polynomial variation; the original sequence must also have polynomial growth. Theorem 6 then gives a fixed-base value criterion. Theorem 8 verifies that condition for 𝑓(⌊𝑚𝜗 +𝜌⌋)f(⌊mϑ+ρ⌋) when 𝑓 ∈ℤ[𝑥]f∈Z[x] is nonconstant, 𝜗,𝜌 ∈(0,1)ϑ,ρ∈(0,1) and 𝜗ϑ is irrational. These interval restrictions belong to that normalised combinatorial statement. Their main value theorem, Theorem 1, allows every real 𝜌ρ and every irrational real 𝜗ϑ, with any algebraic base 𝛽β satisfying |𝛽| >1|β|>1. Claim 10 uses a finite difference of order deg⁡𝑓 +1deg⁡f+1, which vanishes when no floor crossing occurs. This is a polynomial evaluated at a floor, not merely an integer sequence of polynomial growth. Our 𝑚𝑎ma is instead a weighted lattice count. Proposition 12.2 separates floor crossings from its moving boundary, and Example 12.3 shows that the latter can survive a cubic difference even with no crossings.

There is a second distinction. In our displayed shift identity the coefficient of 𝑚𝑎+𝑗𝑟ma+jr is 15𝑐𝑗𝛾𝑎,𝑗𝑟15cjγa,jr, which can depend on 𝑎a. For example, 𝑃1 =2P1=2, 𝑃2 =12P2=12 and 𝑃3 =120P3=120 give 𝛾0,1 =1/3γ0,1=1/3 but 𝛾1,1 =1/5γ1,1=1/5. These factors are required by the denominator chain. A support estimate for 𝐷𝑟,𝑎Dr,a is therefore not by itself a verification of Definition 5 for the sequence (𝑚𝑎)(ma). No fixed-coefficient representation to which that theorem applies has been established here.

For a variable-denominator analogue, a useful hypothesis would be a fixed choice of coefficients and shifts 𝑟𝑛 →∞rn→∞ for which Δ𝑛 ={𝑎 ≥0 :𝐷𝑟𝑛,𝑎 ≠0}Δn={a≥0:Drn,a≠0} is infinite and distinct members are at least 𝜂𝑟𝑛ηrn apart, for a fixed 𝜂 >0η>0. Even this would leave the uniform polynomial-variation requirement, for example

It also requires infinitely many nonzero terms for each selected shift. A small but nonzero term at every index would fail it, and an identically zero difference would also fail the infinitude requirement. The cited paper constructs suitable shifts for the polynomial-floor sequences just described; no such result is proved for our weighted counts. The bound 𝑂((𝑎 +𝑟 +1)2)O((a+r+1)2) controls absolute size: it depends on the location 𝑎a itself. The variation condition instead bounds a later nonzero term by the gap from an earlier one and the size of that earlier term, with constants uniform in the shift. The absolute-size bound alone gives no such comparison. The denominator chain itself is not a fixed-base power sequence. The next proposition shows what is lost in two direct fixed-base recodings. A value criterion for the original chain would need a separate proof, including non-cancellation and the Diophantine height estimates required by a Subspace-Theorem argument. These heights measure the numerators and denominators of the approximating algebraic quantities; they are unrelated to the running LCM HH. The finite set of prime divisors alone does not provide those estimates.

Why a finite alphabet of bases is not enough.

Kebis, Luca, Ouaknine, Scoones and Worrell [27] work with fixed-base series whose coefficients lie in a finite algebraic alphabet. Their echoing condition requires long near-repetitions, separated intervals containing the mismatches, and nonzero weighted mismatch sums on at least two of those intervals. Claim 7 supplies the non-cancellation step in the proof of the value theorem. Merely having finitely many letters does not give these properties. The four-letter radix sequence is not the numerator sequence: the actual 𝑚𝑎ma is unbounded. A recoding would have to preserve the scalar value, identify its coefficient alphabet and verify the echoing conditions. No fixed-base recoding with verified echoing properties is proved here.

Two natural bases expose the tradeoff. The least integer base whose powers clear every 𝑃𝑛Pn is 3030, while base 88 matches the growth of 𝑃𝑛 =H⁡(2𝑛)Pn=H⁡(2n). The former keeps integrality; the latter keeps polynomial size.

A rational number need not have eventually periodic digits when the radices vary. For example, take radix 55 at the indices 1,2,4,8,…1,2,4,8,… and radix 33 elsewhere. This example uses different radices from our four possible bases; it shows why fixed-base eventual periodicity cannot be transferred to a variable-radix expansion. Our digits also depend on the actual tail fractions, not just on the four-letter radix sequence. The construction supplies neither a fixed-base expansion with verified echoing properties nor a bound on its digit complexity.
