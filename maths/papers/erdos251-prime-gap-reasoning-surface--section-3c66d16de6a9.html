Prime-Gap Dyadic Series: Perturbations, Exact Criteria and Certificates

Erdős #251 By Will Cook September 2026 Authorship, AI use and citation
PDFOpen the PDF, 47 pages TeXLaTeX source Source on GitHub Problem page

Précis. A convergent dyadic series with nonnegative integer coefficients can be made rational by sparse nonnegative corrections of upper Banach density zero, eventually bounded by any f(n)→∞, while preserving every fixed modulus and, for a logarithmic allowance, short-block empirical distributions. For prime gaps the cumulative positions remain asymptotic to n log n; no primality conclusion is asserted. Summation by parts reduces the prime series to the gap series, and the tails 𝑇𝑁 =∑𝑗≥1𝑔𝑁+𝑗2−𝑗TN=∑j≥1gN+j2−j satisfy 𝑇𝑁+1 =2𝑇𝑁 −𝑔𝑁+1TN+1=2TN−gN+1. That recurrence characterises rationality and yields finite tests with explicit remainder bounds. An exact finite certificate shows that a rational value of the prime series would have denominator at least 239998 >1012040239998>1012040. The bound does not establish irrationality.

Density of indices with two small tail differences

When the two tail differences have absolute value less than one and the gaps are unequal, the calculation above restricts the gap difference to 22 or −2−2. For each fixed shift, Schlage-Puchta’s lemma gives density zero for both events. We use this to bound the set of possible witnesses, without excluding witnesses at arbitrarily large indices.

Theorem 8.7 (density zero for the small-difference condition). Fix ℎ ≥1h≥1. The set of 𝑁 ≥1N≥1 at which the three hypotheses of Theorem 6.1 hold for the actual prime gaps has density zero. For the same ℎh, the set of 𝑁N with 𝑔𝑁+ℎ+1 =𝑔𝑁+1gN+h+1=gN+1 also has density zero.

Proof. For 𝑁 ≥1N≥1 every gap involved is even, so 𝛿𝑁 =𝑔𝑁+ℎ+1 −𝑔𝑁+1δN=gN+h+1−gN+1 is even. If |𝜎ℎ(𝑁)| <1|σh(N)|<1, |𝜎ℎ(𝑁 +1)| <1|σh(N+1)|<1 and 𝛿𝑁 ≠0δN≠0, then 𝛿𝑁 =2𝜎ℎ(𝑁) −𝜎ℎ(𝑁 +1)δN=2σh(N)−σh(N+1) lies in ( −3,3)(−3,3), so 𝛿𝑁 = ±2δN=±2. Apply Schlage-Puchta’s lemma [18] at index 𝑛 =𝑁 +1n=N+1 with 𝑘 =ℎk=h to the two polynomials 𝑥ℎ −𝑥0 −2xh−x0−2 and 𝑥ℎ −𝑥0 +2xh−x0+2, neither of which vanishes identically: each of {𝑁 :𝛿𝑁 =2}{N:δN=2} and {𝑁 :𝛿𝑁 = −2}{N:δN=−2} has density zero, and the event is contained in their union. The second assertion is the same lemma applied to 𝑥ℎ −𝑥0xh−x0. ◻

Unequal gaps occur at almost every index, a stronger conclusion than Proposition 6.3. But combining unequal gaps with the two small tail differences forces their difference to be exactly 22 or −2−2, which occurs only on a density-zero set. Thus a proof of Problem 10.2 must find arbitrarily large successful indices in that sparse set. A positive-density lower bound for the same event is impossible. An averaging argument that detects a sparse set is not ruled out.

The sum of these block sums is 𝑂ℎ(𝑋log⁡𝑋)Oh(Xlog⁡X) by the prime number theorem, so there are at most 𝑂ℎ(𝑋/log⁡log⁡𝑋)Oh(X/log⁡log⁡X) discarded starts. 𝑂ℎ(𝑋(log⁡log⁡𝑋)2ℎ+2log⁡𝑋)=𝑜ℎ(𝑋log⁡log⁡𝑋).Oh(X(log⁡log⁡X)2h+2log⁡X)=oh(Xlog⁡log⁡X).

The shift from 𝑛 =𝑁 +1n=N+1 changes only the band endpoints. Thus the same upper bound applies to the indices satisfying the three conditions of Theorem 6.1. This is an ordinary consequence of the cited sieve argument, not a new lower bound or an asymptotic formula for the number of successful indices.

The observed proportions in Section 9 decrease across the sampled bands. These finite observations establish neither an asymptotic rate nor the required existence beyond every cutoff. Counting by prime size and counting by gap index also give different cutoffs and must not be compared without converting between them.
