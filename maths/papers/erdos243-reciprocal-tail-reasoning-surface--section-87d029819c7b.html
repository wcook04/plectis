Reciprocal Sums and the Sylvester Recurrence: Further Results and Proofs

Erdős #243 By Will Cook September 2026 Authorship, AI use and citation
PDFOpen the PDF, 61 pages TeXLaTeX source Source on GitHub Problem page

Précis. An increasing sequence of positive integers satisfying 𝑎2𝑛/𝑎𝑛+1 =1 +3/𝑛 +𝑜(𝑛−3)an2/an+1=1+3/n+o(n−3) has an irrational reciprocal sum: rationality would force integer tail numerators to agree eventually with a cubic polynomial, which divisibility, a cubic-field calculation, and congruences modulo seven exclude. For rational reciprocal sums under 𝑎𝑛+1/𝑎2𝑛 →1an+1/an2→1, the record also develops sufficient conditions for the eventual recurrence 𝑎𝑛+1 =𝑎2𝑛 −𝑎𝑛 +1an+1=an2−an+1, controlling either increases of an integer tail numerator or a convergent sum at steps setting new maxima. Examples separate numerical growth bounds from the exact recurrences. The cubic proof is distinguished from the theorems formalised in Lean. None of the additional bounds is derived for every rational reciprocal sum in Erdős #243.

This paper owns the complete problem-specific reasoning surface for Erdős #243, including all registered result families and their boundaries.

It is not authority for the validity of claims tagged Lean, which belongs to the cited kernel-checked source, or a solution to Erdős #243, which remains open.

Zero errors and the Sylvester recurrence

To recover the Sylvester recurrence from a zero error, we eliminate 𝐷𝑛Dn between two successive updates. Write

Δ𝑛=𝑎𝑛+1−(𝑎2𝑛−𝑎𝑛+1),Δn=an+1−(an2−an+1),

the Sylvester defect. The identity below expresses Δ𝑛𝐶𝑛+1ΔnCn+1 through two consecutive errors. If both errors vanish, positivity lets us cancel 𝐶𝑛+1Cn+1 and recover the recurrence. Under the bound |𝐸𝑛| <𝐶𝑛|En|<Cn, it will also make a single zero propagate to the next index.

Theorem 5.1 (defect identity). For all 𝑎,𝑎′,𝐷,𝐶 ∈ℤa,a′,D,C∈Z,

[𝑎′−(𝑎2−𝑎+1)](𝑎𝐶−𝐷)=𝑎2[𝐷−(𝑎−1)𝐶]−[𝑎𝐷−(𝑎′−1)(𝑎𝐶−𝐷)],[a′−(a2−a+1)](aC−D)=a2[D−(a−1)C]−[aD−(a′−1)(aC−D)],

that is, Δ𝑛 𝐶𝑛+1 =𝑎2𝑛𝐸𝑛 −𝐸𝑛+1ΔnCn+1=an2En−En+1.

Proof. A direct expansion: both sides equal 𝑎′𝑎𝐶 −𝑎′𝐷 −𝑎3𝐶 +𝑎2𝐷 +𝑎2𝐶 −𝑎𝐷 −𝑎𝐶 +𝐷a′aC−a′D−a3C+a2D+a2C−aD−aC+D. ◻

No sign or growth hypothesis is needed for the identity itself. Such hypotheses enter only in its consequences below.

Example 5.2 (one defect and the error it creates). Continue Example 4.2 but replace 𝑎3 =43a3=43 by 𝑎3 =44a3=44. The states 𝐷3 =42D3=42 and 𝐶3 =1C3=1 are unchanged, since they depend only on 𝑎0,𝑎1,𝑎2a0,a1,a2, and 𝐸2 =0E2=0 still. The defect is Δ2 =𝑎3 −(𝑎22 −𝑎2 +1) =44 −43 =1Δ2=a3−(a22−a2+1)=44−43=1, and 𝐸3 =𝐷3 −(𝑎3 −1)𝐶3 =42 −43 = −1E3=D3−(a3−1)C3=42−43=−1, so the identity reads

Δ2𝐶3=1⋅1=1=49⋅0−(−1)=𝑎22𝐸2−𝐸3.Δ2C3=1⋅1=1=49⋅0−(−1)=a22E2−E3.

By Proposition 4.1 the numerator then rises, 𝐶4 =𝐶3 −𝐸3 =2C4=C3−E3=2. A single unit of defect at one index has produced a negative error of magnitude 11 at the next, increasing the numerator from 11 to 22.

Theorem 5.3 (two vanishing errors force the step). Let 𝑎,𝑎′,𝐷,𝐶 ∈ℤa,a′,D,C∈Z with 𝑎𝐶 −𝐷 ≠0aC−D≠0. If

𝐷−(𝑎−1)𝐶=0,𝑎𝐷−(𝑎′−1)(𝑎𝐶−𝐷)=0,D−(a−1)C=0,aD−(a′−1)(aC−D)=0,

then 𝑎′ =𝑎2 −𝑎 +1a′=a2−a+1.

Proof. Theorem 5.1 gives [𝑎′ −(𝑎2 −𝑎 +1)] (𝑎𝐶 −𝐷) =0[a′−(a2−a+1)](aC−D)=0. The second factor is nonzero, so the first factor must vanish. ◻

The hypothesis 𝐶𝑛+1 ≠0Cn+1≠0 is not removable: at 𝐶𝑛+1 =0Cn+1=0 the identity gives no information about 𝑎′a′.

Relations among three consecutive numerators

Eliminating the denominator from two successive steps gives a relation among three reduced numerators. Write 𝑢,𝑢1,𝑢2u,u1,u2 for the reduced numerator coordinates, 𝑣,𝑣1v,v1 for the corresponding denominator coordinates, and ℎ,ℎ1h,h1 for the two common factors removed in reduction. If the two steps have multipliers 𝑎,𝑎1a,a1, then the exact hypotheses are

ℎ𝑢1+𝑣=𝑎𝑢,ℎ𝑣1=𝑎𝑣,ℎ1𝑢2+𝑣1=𝑎1𝑢1.hu1+v=au,hv1=av,h1u2+v1=a1u1.

Theorem 5.4 (eliminating the denominator from two steps). Let 𝑎,𝑎1,𝑢,𝑢1,𝑢2,𝑣,𝑣1,ℎ,ℎ1a,a1,u,u1,u2,v,v1,h,h1 be integers satisfying

ℎ𝑢1+𝑣=𝑎𝑢,ℎ𝑣1=𝑎𝑣,ℎ1𝑢2+𝑣1=𝑎1𝑢1.hu1+v=au,hv1=av,h1u2+v1=a1u1.

Then 𝑎2𝑢 +ℎℎ1𝑢2 =ℎ(𝑎 +𝑎1)𝑢1a2u+hh1u2=h(a+a1)u1.

Proof. Multiply the first equation by 𝑎a, replace 𝑎𝑣av using the second equation, and then replace ℎ1𝑢2 +𝑣1h1u2+v1 using the third. The remaining terms factor as the displayed right-hand side. ◻

This is the recurrence obtained by eliminating the denominator. Both cancellation factors remain in the formula. The identity adds no assumption to the two exact steps, but it still contains both multipliers and cannot by itself force 𝑎1 =𝑎2 −𝑎 +1a1=a2−a+1; Lemma 5.6 specifies what it forgets modulo a fixed old denominator.

When neither step cancels a common factor, the same recurrence gives a square identity. Write 𝑝,𝑝1,𝑝2p,p1,p2 for three consecutive numerators, 𝑞 =𝑎𝑝 −𝑝1q=ap−p1 for the denominator before the first step, and 𝑎,𝑎1a,a1 for the two multipliers. Then

𝑝2+𝑎2𝑝=(𝑎+𝑎1)𝑝1.p2+a2p=(a+a1)p1.

The resulting square identity is as follows.

Theorem 5.5 (a square identity without cancellation). Let 𝑎,𝑎1,𝑝,𝑝1,𝑝2,𝑞a,a1,p,p1,p2,q be integers with 𝑞 =𝑎𝑝 −𝑝1q=ap−p1 and 𝑝2 +𝑎2𝑝 =(𝑎 +𝑎1)𝑝1p2+a2p=(a+a1)p1. Then

𝑞2+(𝑝𝑝2−𝑝21)=(𝑎1−𝑎)𝑝𝑝1.q2+(pp2−p12)=(a1−a)pp1.

Proof. Substitute 𝑞 =𝑎𝑝 −𝑝1q=ap−p1 and 𝑝2 =(𝑎 +𝑎1)𝑝1 −𝑎2𝑝p2=(a+a1)p1−a2p:

𝑞2+𝑝𝑝2−𝑝21=(𝑎𝑝−𝑝1)2+𝑝((𝑎+𝑎1)𝑝1−𝑎2𝑝)−𝑝21=(𝑎1−𝑎)𝑝𝑝1.q2+pp2−p12=(ap−p1)2+p((a+a1)p1−a2p)−p12=(a1−a)pp1.

◻

The term 𝑞2q2 is nonnegative, while 𝑝𝑝2 −𝑝21pp2−p12 measures the difference between the product of the outer numerators and the square of the middle one. The right side records the change in multiplier, so the identity gives a necessary algebraic constraint without asserting a sign or a global monotonicity. It is checked as the square identity when no factor is cancelled; no global exclusion of approximate solutions is claimed.

The reduced error update must also be compatible with the denominator recurrence. The following calculation gives the exact compatibility condition. Here 𝑒,𝑒1e,e1 are signed errors in reduced coordinates, not the positive magnitudes denoted by 𝑒𝑛en in the all-negative case. Let ΔΔ be a proposed value of the Sylvester defect. If

ℎ𝑢1=𝑢−𝑒,ℎ𝑒1=𝑎2𝑒−Δ(𝑢−𝑒),hu1=u−e,he1=a2e−Δ(u−e),

then the denominator equality

ℎ((𝑎1−1)𝑢1+𝑒1)=𝑎((𝑎−1)𝑢+𝑒)h((a1−1)u1+e1)=a((a−1)u+e)

holds precisely when the following difference vanishes:

ℎ((𝑎1−1)𝑢1+𝑒1)−𝑎((𝑎−1)𝑢+𝑒)=[𝑎1−(𝑎2−𝑎+1)−Δ](𝑢−𝑒).h((a1−1)u1+e1)−a((a−1)u+e)=[a1−(a2−a+1)−Δ](u−e).

Consequently, when 𝑢 −𝑒 ≠0u−e≠0,

ℎ((𝑎1−1)𝑢1+𝑒1)=𝑎((𝑎−1)𝑢+𝑒)⟺𝑎1=𝑎2−𝑎+1+Δ.h((a1−1)u1+e1)=a((a−1)u+e)⟺a1=a2−a+1+Δ.

The equivalence with the denominator recurrence is formalised using the factored difference between the two recurrences. The hypothesis 𝑢 −𝑒 ≠0u−e≠0 is essential: if 𝑢 −𝑒 =0u−e=0, the factorised mismatch cannot identify 𝑎1a1.

The identities hold under the local hypotheses displayed in their statements. It is their proposed global applications, not the identities, that would need additional information, such as control of cancellation or a sign estimate for 𝑝𝑝2 −𝑝21pp2−p12. No such information is deduced here for an arbitrary rational near-quadratic tail.

Lemma 5.6 (saturation modulo an old denominator). Let 𝑀 ≥2M≥2. Any finite word 𝑟0,…,𝑟𝑘r0,…,rk of units modulo 𝑀M is compatible with the cancellation-free recurrences modulo 𝑀M, with all reduced denominator residues equal to zero. Consequently the eliminated two-step identity alone imposes no further restriction on such unit words when the multiplier residues are free.

Proof. Set 𝑣𝑖 ≡0(mod𝑀)vi≡0(modM) and choose 𝑎𝑖 ≡𝑟𝑖+1𝑟−1𝑖(mod𝑀)ai≡ri+1ri−1(modM). Then 𝑟𝑖+1 +𝑣𝑖 ≡𝑎𝑖𝑟𝑖ri+1+vi≡airi and 𝑣𝑖+1 ≡𝑎𝑖𝑣𝑖vi+1≡aivi. Eliminating 𝑣𝑖vi gives the two-step identity automatically. ◻

This is finite residue compatibility, not existence of a positive integer orbit, much less a rational tail with near-quadratic growth. For 𝑀 ∣𝑣𝑇M∣vT on a tail with no further common-factor cancellation, the actual 𝑢𝑛un are indeed units modulo 𝑀M. Useful further restrictions must therefore retain information discarded here: for example multiplier size, changing moduli, or primes outside the old denominator. The square restriction used in the cubic proof concerns precisely a prime at which a middle numerator vanishes.

Theorem 5.7 (sequence form). Let 𝑎,𝐷,𝐶 :ℕ →ℤa,D,C:N→Z satisfy 𝐷𝑛+1 =𝑎𝑛𝐷𝑛Dn+1=anDn and 𝐶𝑛+1 =𝑎𝑛𝐶𝑛 −𝐷𝑛Cn+1=anCn−Dn. If 𝐸𝑛 =0En=0 for all sufficiently large 𝑛n and 𝐶𝑛+1 ≠0Cn+1≠0 for all sufficiently large 𝑛n, then 𝑎𝑛+1 =𝑎2𝑛 −𝑎𝑛 +1an+1=an2−an+1 for all sufficiently large 𝑛n.

Take the larger of the two thresholds and apply Theorem 5.3 at each later index. This is the final step of the criteria below that force 𝐸𝑛En to vanish eventually.

The same identity shows that a zero error persists whenever |𝐸𝑛| <𝐶𝑛|En|<Cn at the subsequent indices.

Theorem 5.8 (zero is absorbing). Let 𝑎,𝐶,𝐷 :ℕ →ℕa,C,D:N→N satisfy 𝐶𝑛+1 +𝐷𝑛 =𝑎𝑛𝐶𝑛Cn+1+Dn=anCn and 𝐷𝑛+1 =𝑎𝑛𝐷𝑛Dn+1=anDn, and put 𝐸𝑛 =𝐷𝑛 −(𝑎𝑛 −1)𝐶𝑛En=Dn−(an−1)Cn. Suppose that |𝐸𝑛| <𝐶𝑛|En|<Cn for every 𝑛n. If 𝐸𝑛 =0En=0 then 𝐸𝑛+1 =0En+1=0.

Proof. Putting 𝐸𝑛 =0En=0 in Theorem 5.1 gives Δ𝑛𝐶𝑛+1 = −𝐸𝑛+1ΔnCn+1=−En+1, so 𝐶𝑛+1Cn+1 divides 𝐸𝑛+1En+1. A multiple of 𝐶𝑛+1Cn+1 of absolute value smaller than 𝐶𝑛+1Cn+1 is zero, and |𝐸𝑛+1| <𝐶𝑛+1|En+1|<Cn+1 by hypothesis. ◻

Beyond an index from which |𝐸𝑛| <𝐶𝑛|En|<Cn always holds, either 𝐸𝑛En reaches zero and remains zero, or it is never zero. A zero at an earlier index need not persist: in Example 5.2, the altered multiplier gives 𝐸3 = −1E3=−1 and 𝐶3 =1C3=1, so the required strict inequality fails. Sections 9 and 10 address the special all-negative case. The mixed-sign analysis in Sections 12 and 13 separates arbitrarily late negative errors from an eventually nonnegative tail.
