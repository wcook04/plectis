Reciprocal Sums and the Sylvester Recurrence: Further Results and Proofs

Erdős #243 By Will Cook September 2026
PDFOpen the PDF, 61 pages TeXLaTeX source Source on GitHub Problem dossier

Précis. An increasing sequence of positive integers satisfying 𝑎2𝑛/𝑎𝑛+1 =1 +3/𝑛 +𝑜(𝑛−3)an2/an+1=1+3/n+o(n−3) has an irrational reciprocal sum: rationality would force integer tail numerators to agree eventually with a cubic polynomial, which divisibility, a cubic-field calculation, and congruences modulo seven exclude. For rational reciprocal sums under 𝑎𝑛+1/𝑎2𝑛 →1an+1/an2→1, the record also develops sufficient conditions for the eventual recurrence 𝑎𝑛+1 =𝑎2𝑛 −𝑎𝑛 +1an+1=an2−an+1, controlling either increases of an integer tail numerator or a convergent sum at steps setting new maxima. Examples separate numerical growth bounds from the exact recurrences. The cubic proof is distinguished from the theorems formalised in Lean. None of the additional bounds is derived for every rational reciprocal sum in Erdős #243.

Periodic negative errors

Let the negative error have periodic magnitude 𝑒𝑛 = −𝐸𝑛 >0en=−En>0, so 𝑒𝑛+ℎ =𝑒𝑛en+h=en and 𝐶𝑛+1 =𝐶𝑛 +𝑒𝑛Cn+1=Cn+en. Summing over a period gives 𝐶𝑛+ℎ =𝐶𝑛 +𝑀Cn+h=Cn+M, where the positive integer 𝑀 =∑ℎ−1𝑗=0𝑒𝑗M=∑j=0h−1ej is the drift. Periodicity makes this sum independent of the starting index.

At ℎ =1h=1 this says that the magnitude is constant and that 𝑀M is its value, which is the situation of Section 9; Theorem 9.2 already excludes it without using the pointwise bound 𝑒𝑛 <𝑎𝑛en<an in the proof below. The new case is ℎ ≥2h≥2.

For a constant error, the proof used the finitely many prime divisors of its magnitude. For a periodic error we use the prime divisors of the increase 𝑀M over one period. The required divisibility facts are as follows. Every multiplier 𝑎𝑗aj divides each later denominator, by persistence of a multiplier. If a prime divides both 𝐷𝑛Dn and 𝑎𝑛an, the numerator update makes it divide 𝐶𝑛+1Cn+1, the one-step divisibility implication. Once it divides both numerator and denominator, it divides every later numerator, denominator and error, by persistence of a common divisor.

Periodicity carries this divisibility back to every phase. Suppose 𝑑 ∣𝑀d∣M and 𝑑 ∣𝑎𝑖,𝑎𝑗d∣ai,aj with 𝑖 <𝑗i<j. Then 𝑑 ∣𝐷𝑗d∣Dj, and 𝐶𝑗+1 =𝑎𝑗𝐶𝑗 −𝐷𝑗Cj+1=ajCj−Dj gives 𝑑 ∣𝐶𝑗+1d∣Cj+1. The common divisor persists in both sequences and in every later error. For any phase 𝑟r, choose 𝑘k with 𝑟 +𝑘ℎ ≥𝑗 +1r+kh≥j+1; periodicity gives 𝑑 ∣𝑒𝑟+𝑘ℎ =𝑒𝑟d∣er+kh=er. Taking 𝑘ℎ ≥𝑗 +1kh≥j+1 also gives 𝑑 ∣𝐶𝑘ℎ =𝐶0 +𝑘𝑀d∣Ckh=C0+kM, hence 𝑑 ∣𝐶0d∣C0. This is the repeated-divisor implication; it uses the period relation and the increase over successive periods, each iterated through whole periods. A repeated prime can therefore be divided out of the entire system. This explains the induction on 𝑀M in the proof.

Theorem 10.1 (no periodic negative magnitude). Let 𝑎,𝐷,𝐶,𝑒 :ℕ →ℕa,D,C,e:N→N with 𝑎𝑛 ≥2an≥2, 𝑒𝑛 >0en>0 and 𝑒𝑛 <𝑎𝑛en<an for every 𝑛n, satisfying

𝐷𝑛+1=𝑎𝑛𝐷𝑛,𝐶𝑛+1=𝐶𝑛+𝑒𝑛,𝐷𝑛+𝑒𝑛=(𝑎𝑛−1)𝐶𝑛,Dn+1=anDn,Cn+1=Cn+en,Dn+en=(an−1)Cn,

and suppose 𝑒𝑛+ℎ =𝑒𝑛en+h=en and 𝐶𝑛+ℎ =𝐶𝑛 +𝑀Cn+h=Cn+M for some ℎ >0h>0 and 𝑀 >0M>0. This is impossible.

Proof. We use strong induction on the drift 𝑀M. A prime divisor of 𝑀M that recurs among the multipliers need not give an immediate contradiction. It instead divides the whole orbit, allowing us to reduce 𝑀M by that prime and apply the induction hypothesis.

Suppose first that no prime divisor of 𝑀M is a common divisor of 𝐶0C0 and of every magnitude. The repeated-divisor implication applies in the contrapositive: a prime divisor of 𝑀M occurring in two of the multipliers would be exactly such a common divisor, so each prime divisor of 𝑀M occurs in at most one multiplier. On the other hand, every multiplier shares a prime with 𝑀M. Indeed, if gcd(𝑎𝑗,𝑀) =1gcd(aj,M)=1, choose 𝑘 ≥1k≥1 so that 𝑎𝑗 ∣𝐶𝑗 +𝑘𝑀 =𝐶𝑗+𝑘ℎaj∣Cj+kM=Cj+kh. The same multiplier divides 𝐷𝑗+𝑘ℎDj+kh, and periodicity gives 𝑒𝑗+𝑘ℎ =𝑒𝑗ej+kh=ej. The identity at 𝑗 +𝑘ℎj+kh therefore implies 𝑎𝑗 ∣𝑒𝑗aj∣ej, contrary to 0 <𝑒𝑗 <𝑎𝑗0<ej<aj. Thus infinitely many multipliers would require distinct prime divisors of the fixed integer 𝑀M, a contradiction.

Otherwise some prime 𝑝p divides 𝑀M, divides 𝐶0C0, and divides every magnitude. Then 𝑝 ∣𝐶𝑛p∣Cn for every 𝑛n, since 𝐶𝑛+1 =𝐶𝑛 +𝑒𝑛Cn+1=Cn+en and both summands on the right are divisible by 𝑝p, and the same identity 𝐷𝑛 +𝑒𝑛 =(𝑎𝑛 −1)𝐶𝑛Dn+en=(an−1)Cn then gives 𝑝 ∣𝐷𝑛p∣Dn for every 𝑛n. Divide 𝐷D, 𝐶C and 𝑒e by 𝑝p. The multipliers are untouched, so 𝑎𝑛 ≥2an≥2 and 𝑒𝑛 <𝑎𝑛en<an persist and the magnitudes stay positive; the three recurrences are homogeneous in (𝐷,𝐶,𝑒)(D,C,e) and so survive; the period is still ℎh; and the drift becomes 𝑀/𝑝 <𝑀M/p<M. The inductive hypothesis applies. ◻

The first of the two cases above is the exclusion when no prime of 𝑀M divides all phases, the induction is the exclusion at every scale, and the eventual form is the eventual exclusion.

The phase-by-phase proof uses 𝑒𝑛 <𝑎𝑛en<an to prevent a multiplier from dividing its own nonzero phase magnitude. This is not a rescaling of the orbit. Nevertheless, for an infinite solution of (19) with periodic positive magnitudes, the bound follows eventually from the other assumptions. The identity gives 𝐶𝑛 >0Cn>0, and periodicity gives

𝐶𝑛=𝑀ℎ𝑛+𝑂(1),sup𝑛𝑒𝑛<∞,Cn=Mhn+O(1),supnen<∞,

and 𝐷0D0 cannot be zero: otherwise all 𝐷𝑛Dn vanish and the defining identity gives 𝑒𝑛 =(𝑎𝑛 −1)𝐶𝑛 ≥𝐶𝑛en=(an−1)Cn≥Cn, contradicting these bounds. Thus 𝐷𝑛 ≥2𝑛𝐷0Dn≥2nD0, and

𝑎𝑛−1=𝐷𝑛+𝑒𝑛𝐶𝑛⟶∞.an−1=Dn+enCn⟶∞.

Consequently 𝑒𝑛 <𝑎𝑛en<an eventually. Applying the eventual exclusion therefore rules out periodic positive magnitudes without an independent smallness assumption. The numbered theorem retains the pointwise bound used by its checked phase-by-phase proof. The later bounded-negative theorem also excludes this case.

Remark. For the tail numerators of [12], the absolute error satisfies |𝐸𝑛| <𝑎𝑛|En|<an eventually. Indeed, under the dictionary in Section 3, Lemma 4(2) gives −𝐶𝑛/2 ≤𝐸𝑛 <𝐶𝑛/2−Cn/2≤En<Cn/2, hence |𝐸𝑛| ≤𝐶𝑛/2|En|≤Cn/2. The proof of Lemma 4(3) gives 𝐶𝑛+1 ≤32𝐶𝑛Cn+1≤32Cn [12], hence 𝐶𝑛 ≤𝐶𝑁(3/2)𝑛−𝑁Cn≤CN(3/2)n−N beyond a pseudo-greedy starting index 𝑁N. On the other hand, convergence of ∑1/𝑎𝑛∑1/an gives 𝑎𝑛 →∞an→∞, and 𝑎𝑛+1/𝑎2𝑛 →1an+1/an2→1 then gives 𝑎𝑛+1 ≥2𝑎𝑛an+1≥2an eventually. Thus |𝐸𝑛|/𝑎𝑛 →0|En|/an→0. On a negative-error tail this gives 𝑒𝑛 <𝑎𝑛en<an.

For integer solutions of (19), the same conclusion follows when 𝑎𝑛 >1an>1, 𝐶𝑛 >0Cn>0 and 𝐸𝑛/𝐶𝑛 →0En/Cn→0 hold: the calculation in Section 4 already derives the reciprocal series and its growth from these hypotheses. Summability is not an independent assumption in that setting. The bound 𝑒𝑛 <𝑎𝑛en<an is still only eventual, whereas Theorem 10.1 states it at every index; its eventual form, cited above, is therefore the applicable one. For a periodic magnitude, boundedness of 𝑒𝑛en and divergence of 𝑎𝑛an already suffice.
