Reciprocal Sums and the Sylvester Recurrence: Further Results and Proofs

Erdős #243 By Will Cook September 2026 Authorship, AI use and citation
PDFOpen the PDF, 61 pages TeXLaTeX source Source on GitHub Problem page

Précis. An increasing sequence of positive integers satisfying 𝑎2𝑛/𝑎𝑛+1 =1 +3/𝑛 +𝑜(𝑛−3)an2/an+1=1+3/n+o(n−3) has an irrational reciprocal sum: rationality would force integer tail numerators to agree eventually with a cubic polynomial, which divisibility, a cubic-field calculation, and congruences modulo seven exclude. For rational reciprocal sums under 𝑎𝑛+1/𝑎2𝑛 →1an+1/an2→1, the record also develops sufficient conditions for the eventual recurrence 𝑎𝑛+1 =𝑎2𝑛 −𝑎𝑛 +1an+1=an2−an+1, controlling either increases of an integer tail numerator or a convergent sum at steps setting new maxima. Examples separate numerical growth bounds from the exact recurrences. The cubic proof is distinguished from the theorems formalised in Lean. None of the additional bounds is derived for every rational reciprocal sum in Erdős #243.

A factorial modulus for integral recursion

In the case 𝑚 =𝑐 =1m=c=1 of Section 9, the identity (5.1) becomes 𝐷𝑛 +1 =(𝑎𝑛 −1)(𝑛 +1)Dn+1=(an−1)(n+1). It determines the recursion 𝑎𝑛+1 =num⁡(𝑛,𝑎𝑛)/(𝑛 +2)an+1=num⁡(n,an)/(n+2), where

num⁡(𝑛,𝑎)=(𝑛+1)𝑎2−(𝑛+2)𝑎+(𝑛+3),num⁡(n,a)=(n+1)a2−(n+2)a+(n+3),

the forced numerator. We stop at the first nonintegral quotient. The formal survival predicate requires exact division at each step. For 𝑎0 =3a0=3, as in Example 9.1, the first quotient is num⁡(0,3)/2 =3num⁡(0,3)/2=3, but the next quotient is num⁡(1,3)/3 =13/3num⁡(1,3)/3=13/3, so the recursion stops. Direct iteration can produce very large intermediate integers. To decide whether the first ℎh divisions are exact, however, it suffices to know the initial value modulo (ℎ +1)!(h+1)!. Computing a pseudo-greedy orbit through residues modulo a shrinking product modulus is Koizumi’s method [12]; for the forced numerator that product is a factorial.

Theorem B.1 (factorial residue reduction). Let ℎh be a nonnegative integer and let 𝑎,𝑏a,b be integers with 𝑎 ≡𝑏(mod(ℎ +1)!)a≡b(mod(h+1)!). The first ℎh steps of the recursion 𝑎𝑛+1 =num⁡(𝑛,𝑎𝑛)/(𝑛 +2)an+1=num⁡(n,an)/(n+2), starting at index zero, are integral for 𝑎0 =𝑎a0=a if and only if they are integral for 𝑎0 =𝑏a0=b.

Proof. Each exact division consumes a congruence factor. Retain the remaining factors by setting 𝑀(0,𝑖) =1M(0,i)=1 and 𝑀(ℎ +1,𝑖) =(𝑖 +2)𝑀(ℎ,𝑖 +1)M(h+1,i)=(i+2)M(h,i+1). Thus 𝑀(ℎ,0) =(ℎ +1)!M(h,0)=(h+1)!. We prove the stronger statement that, at index 𝑖i, congruent inputs modulo 𝑀(ℎ,𝑖)M(h,i) give the same answer to whether the next ℎh quotients are all integral. There is nothing to prove for ℎ =0h=0.

For the inductive step, suppose 𝑎 ≡𝑏(mod(𝑖 +2)𝑀(ℎ,𝑖 +1))a≡b(mod(i+2)M(h,i+1)). Since num⁡(𝑖, ⋅)num⁡(i,⋅) is an integer polynomial, its values at 𝑎a and 𝑏b are congruent modulo that product. In particular, one is divisible by 𝑖 +2i+2 if and only if the other is. If neither is divisible, both recursions stop. Otherwise their quotients are congruent modulo 𝑀(ℎ,𝑖 +1)M(h,i+1), so the induction hypothesis applies to the remaining ℎh updates at index 𝑖 +1i+1. Taking 𝑖 =0i=0 proves the claim. ◻

The formal factorial residue reduction uses the shrinking-modulus induction, polynomial congruence, and cancellation after exact division. Its modulus is identified by the ascending-factorial formula and its factorial value at the initial index.

For ℎ =1h=1, the modulus is 2! =22!=2. The first quotient is integral if and only if 2 ∣𝑎2 −2𝑎 +32∣a2−2a+3, or equivalently if 𝑎a is odd. For instance, num⁡(0,3) =6num⁡(0,3)=6 and num⁡(0,4) =11num⁡(0,4)=11. Thus the parity of 𝑎a decides the first step, as Theorem B.1 asserts in this case.

Exact enumeration for 2 ≤𝑎0 <50002≤a0<5000 gives a maximum of 1717 successful updates, or 1818 multiplier values including 𝑎0a0. Nine seeds attain it, the least being 15011501. The seed 𝑎0 =1a0=1 is excluded: it gives 𝑎𝑛 =1an=1 at every step and violates the hypothesis 𝑎𝑛 ≥2an≥2. The test certifies exact division, not a positive orbit. Even a surviving seed for every finite horizon would not supply one integer seed surviving forever. Theorem 9.2 proves the infinite exclusion; the residue reduction also applies to other polynomial-division recurrences.
