Reciprocal Sums and the Sylvester Recurrence

Erdős #243 By Will Cook September 2026 Authorship, AI use and citation
PDFOpen the PDF, 21 pages TeXLaTeX source Source on GitHub Problem page

Précis. For strictly increasing positive integers with 𝑎2𝑛/𝑎𝑛+1 =1 +3/𝑛 +𝑜(𝑛−3)an2/an+1=1+3/n+o(n−3), the reciprocal sum is irrational. The argument extracts a cubic polynomial from an integer tail numerator, restricts its coefficients using a square in a cubic field, and excludes the remaining cases modulo seven. The unrestricted Erdős–Graham question remains open.

This paper owns the cubic-rate irrationality theorem and its proof; the long paper develops the separate Sylvester-tail criteria and broader recurrence questions.

It is not authority for the validity of claims tagged Lean, which belongs to the cited kernel-checked source, or a solution to Erdős #243, which remains open.

Introduction

The Sylvester recurrence 𝑎𝑛+1 =𝑎2𝑛 −𝑎𝑛 +1an+1=an2−an+1 gives a rational reciprocal sum by telescoping:

1𝑎𝑛−1=1𝑎𝑛+1𝑎𝑛+1−1.1an−1=1an+1an+1−1.

For an increasing sequence of positive integers that eventually satisfies this recurrence, the tail sum at every sufficiently large index is 1/(𝑎𝑛 −1)1/(an−1). Erdős and Graham asked whether every rational reciprocal sum with 𝑎𝑛+1 ∼𝑎2𝑛an+1∼an2 arises in this way.

Problem 1.1 (Erdős Problem 243). Let 1 ≤𝑎1 <𝑎2 <⋯1≤a1<a2<⋯ be a sequence of integers with

lim𝑛→∞𝑎𝑛𝑎2𝑛−1=1and∑1𝑎𝑛∈ℚ.limn→∞anan−12=1and∑1an∈Q.

Then 𝑎𝑛 =𝑎2𝑛−1 −𝑎𝑛−1 +1an=an−12−an−1+1 for all sufficiently large 𝑛n.

Erdős and Graham [5] asked this question; see also Erdős [6] and Bloom’s catalogue [11]. We prove the following irrationality result for a restricted class of sequences with 𝑎𝑛+1 ∼𝑎2𝑛an+1∼an2.

Theorem 1.2 (cubic-rate irrationality). A strictly increasing sequence of positive integers with

𝑎2𝑛/𝑎𝑛+1=1+3𝑛+𝑜(𝑛−3)an2/an+1=1+3n+o(n−3)

has irrational reciprocal sum.

The eventual Sylvester recurrence gives 𝑎2𝑛/𝑎𝑛+1 −1 =𝑂(1/𝑎𝑛)an2/an+1−1=O(1/an), far smaller than 3/𝑛3/n. Thus Theorem 1.2 rules out rationality in this subclass; none of our arguments treats the general quadratic limit alone.

For example, set 𝑎1 =8a1=8 and

𝑎𝑛+1=⌈𝑛𝑎2𝑛𝑛+3⌉.an+1=⌈nan2n+3⌉.

This gives 8,16,103,5305,…8,16,103,5305,…. From 𝑎𝑛+1 ≥𝑎2𝑛/4an+1≥an2/4 we obtain 𝑎𝑛 ≥4 ⋅22𝑛−1an≥4⋅22n−1 by induction. The error introduced by the ceiling is less than one, and hence

0≤1+3𝑛−𝑎2𝑛𝑎𝑛+1<16𝑎2𝑛=𝑜(𝑛−3).0≤1+3n−an2an+1<16an2=o(n−3).

The theorem applies to this explicitly defined sequence.

Rationality supplies a positive integer tail numerator 𝐶𝑛Cn, but not a bounded one: here it grows cubically. We therefore apply integrality to its finite differences. The exact comparison sequence 𝐹𝑛 =𝑛(𝑛 +1)(𝑛 +2)Fn=n(n+1)(n+2) has 𝐹𝑛+1/𝐹𝑛 =1 +3/𝑛Fn+1/Fn=1+3/n. The precision in the hypothesis forces Δ4𝐶𝑛 →0Δ4Cn→0, hence eventual equality 𝐶𝑛 =𝐴𝐹𝑛 +𝐵Cn=AFn+B. This extraction uses the numerator ratio and integrality, not the exact denominator recurrence.

The denominator recurrence now becomes decisive. It imposes a square condition at every root of a cubic modulo almost every prime. Chebotarev’s theorem turns these residue conditions into a square in a cubic number field, where traces restrict the numerator to two polynomials. A four-term calculation modulo seven excludes both. Section 3 follows these two stages: first extract the polynomial, then test whether it can satisfy the exact recurrence.

For the recurrence criteria we use the integer tail numerators of Koizumi [10]. In Section 4, a stable gcd and the Chinese remainder theorem force eventual Sylvester behaviour for a rational tail whose quotient 𝑎1⋯𝑎𝑛−1/𝑎𝑛a1⋯an−1/an has bounded upward increments. The separate summability criterion of Section 5 needs only integer increments and positivity. For the weighted criterion of Section 6, we instead clear tails by a least common multiple and use the denominator recurrence to control first crossings of new heights. Its convergence hypothesis is not supplied by the general quadratic limit. The companion paper also treats nonintegral rates and proves a stronger cubic disagreement theorem. Its detailed proof locations and the formal sources are listed in Appendix B.
