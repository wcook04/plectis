Reciprocal Sums and the Sylvester Recurrence: Further Results and Proofs

Erdős #243 By Will Cook September 2026 Authorship, AI use and citation
PDFOpen the PDF, 61 pages TeXLaTeX source Source on GitHub Problem page

Précis. An increasing sequence of positive integers satisfying 𝑎2𝑛/𝑎𝑛+1 =1 +3/𝑛 +𝑜(𝑛−3)an2/an+1=1+3/n+o(n−3) has an irrational reciprocal sum: rationality would force integer tail numerators to agree eventually with a cubic polynomial, which divisibility, a cubic-field calculation, and congruences modulo seven exclude. For rational reciprocal sums under 𝑎𝑛+1/𝑎2𝑛 →1an+1/an2→1, the record also develops sufficient conditions for the eventual recurrence 𝑎𝑛+1 =𝑎2𝑛 −𝑎𝑛 +1an+1=an2−an+1, controlling either increases of an integer tail numerator or a convergent sum at steps setting new maxima. Examples separate numerical growth bounds from the exact recurrences. The cubic proof is distinguished from the theorems formalised in Lean. None of the additional bounds is derived for every rational reciprocal sum in Erdős #243.

This paper owns the complete problem-specific reasoning surface for Erdős #243, including all registered result families and their boundaries.

It is not authority for the validity of claims tagged Lean, which belongs to the cited kernel-checked source, or a solution to Erdős #243, which remains open.

Introduction

Sylvester’s sequence 2,3,7,43,1807,…2,3,7,43,1807,… has reciprocal sum 11. Its recurrence 𝑎𝑛 =𝑎2𝑛−1 −𝑎𝑛−1 +1an=an−12−an−1+1 gives the telescoping identity

1𝑎𝑛−1−1=1𝑎𝑛−1+1𝑎𝑛−1.1an−1−1=1an−1+1an−1.

The tail beginning with 1/𝑎𝑛−11/an−1 therefore equals 1/(𝑎𝑛−1 −1)1/(an−1−1). Erdős Problem #243 asks whether every rational reciprocal sum with 𝑎𝑛 ∼𝑎2𝑛−1an∼an−12 must eventually have this form:

Problem 1.1 (Erdős #243). Let 1 ≤𝑎1 <𝑎2 <⋯1≤a1<a2<⋯ be a sequence of integers with

lim𝑛→∞𝑎𝑛𝑎2𝑛−1=1and∑1𝑎𝑛∈ℚ.limn→∞anan−12=1and∑1an∈Q.

Then 𝑎𝑛 =𝑎2𝑛−1 −𝑎𝑛−1 +1an=an−12−an−1+1 for all sufficiently large 𝑛n.

See [5] and [6]. Bloom’s catalogue, accessed 28 July 2026, lists the problem as open [13]. Below we index the same recurrence as 𝑎𝑛+1 =𝑎2𝑛 −𝑎𝑛 +1an+1=an2−an+1, which is the shift the formal sources use; the two forms are the same statement. The polynomial 𝑎2 −𝑎 +1a2−a+1 is called the Sylvester successor.

Clearing a rational reciprocal tail gives positive integers 𝐶𝑛,𝐷𝑛Cn,Dn with 𝐶𝑛+1 =𝑎𝑛𝐶𝑛 −𝐷𝑛Cn+1=anCn−Dn and 𝐷𝑛+1 =𝑎𝑛𝐷𝑛Dn+1=anDn. The first update can also be written 𝐶𝑛+1 =𝐶𝑛 −𝐸𝑛Cn+1=Cn−En, where 𝐸𝑛 =𝐷𝑛 −(𝑎𝑛 −1)𝐶𝑛En=Dn−(an−1)Cn. Quadratic growth makes 𝐸𝑛/𝐶𝑛 →0En/Cn→0. The eventual Sylvester recurrence follows once the integer error itself vanishes.

There are two ways to use this integer numerator. At a precise regular rate it grows, but a high finite difference tends to zero and must vanish. The resulting polynomial is then tested against the denominator recurrence. With bounded upward steps, a different argument applies: a stable common divisor gives reduced tails, whose numerators avoid every earlier multiplier. The Chinese remainder theorem produces a block that bounded rises cannot cross, regardless of intervening decreases. Neither argument replaces the denominator recurrence by a scalar growth estimate.

Section 2 proves cubic and nonintegral-rate irrationality: these rates exclude the eventual Sylvester recurrence. The general question remains open. After the literature comparison in Section 3, Sections 4–5 develop integer tails and zero-error identities for the subsequent criteria.

The later criteria use two distinct normalisations. Section 6 clears tails with an LCM: common numerator factors remain, so congruences forbid short crossings rather than numerator values. Section 7 reduces to lowest terms, where cancellation can remove a prime that persists in the LCM. That argument must track the prime powers which survive. Sections 9–10 treat constant and periodic errors; Sections 11–12 prove the bounded-increase criterion, and Section 13 treats scalar summability. Section 14 separates open estimates from countermodels to weakened hypotheses. The appendices give formal sources, the finite residue calculation and a result map.

We use 𝑧+ =max(𝑧,0)z+=max(z,0). Deleting a finite prefix is harmless for an eventual recurrence, but not for the coefficients in a higher-order rate. In particular, the index 𝑛n in 1 +3/𝑛 +𝑜(𝑛−3)1+3/n+o(n−3) is kept fixed throughout the cubic proof. The recurrence sections also use zero-based indexing, with that convention stated where the integer sequences are introduced.
