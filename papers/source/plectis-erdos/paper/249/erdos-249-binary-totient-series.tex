\begin{abstract}
For integers $k\ge2$ and $e\ge1$, every totient section
$n\mapsto\varphi(k^jn+r)$ with $0\le j\le e$, $0\le r<k^j$,
is an explicit integer multiple of one of $k^e+1$ independent sections.
The resulting two-term relations form an integral basis of all relations
among the indexed sections: their coefficients are recovered without
division. Martin's stronger affine theorem gives the independence;
we also prove it directly by making an evaluation matrix diagonal
modulo a prime. The same separation method classifies rational binary
series formed from dyadic totient residues. It does not settle the
irrationality of the unreduced binary totient series.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:results}
\papersectiontarget{short249-basis}
Splitting the totient sequence into its even and odd subsequences,
then splitting once more, gives seven sequences:
\[
 \varphi(n);\qquad
 \varphi(2n),\ \varphi(2n+1);\qquad
 \varphi(4n),\ \varphi(4n+1),\ \varphi(4n+2),\ \varphi(4n+3).
\]
The identities
\[
 \varphi(4n)=2\varphi(2n),\qquad
 \varphi(4n+2)=\varphi(2n+1)
\]
leave five independent sequences. Thus every identity among the seven
comes from these two substitutions. More precisely, an integral
relation is a unique integral combination of the two displayed
relations. We will prove the same statement at every depth and in
every integer base.

For an integer $k\ge2$, the \emph{totient sections} are the sequences
$n\mapsto\varphi(k^jn+r)$, where $j\ge0$ and $0\le r<k^j$.
Throughout, $n$ ranges over $\N=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}$ and
$\varphi(0)=0$. To describe relations we retain a separate index for
each pair $(j,r)$, even when two pairs give the same sequence.
For example, $\varphi(4n+2)$ and $\varphi(2n+1)$ are equal sequences
but occupy different positions in the list above.

The sections form the $k$-kernel of the totient sequence. A sequence is
$k$-regular when the $\Z$-module generated by its $k$-kernel is finitely
generated~\cite{allouche-shallit}. Coons proved that the totient sequence is
not $k$-regular for any $k\ge2$~\cite[Thm.~3.2]{coons}.
Here we determine those modules at each finite level, including their
integral relations. The theorem gives the basis and the substitutions;
Corollary~\ref{cor:integral-normal-form} explains how to recover every
relation from them. Level zero contains only $\varphi(n)$ and has
dimension one.

\begin{theorem}[A basis through each finite level]
\label{thm:kkernelrank}
Let $k\ge2$ and $e\ge1$ be integers, write $F^{(k)}_{j,r}(n)=\ph(k^jn+r)$, and put
\[
 V_{k,e}=\operatorname{span}_{\Q}
 \{\,F^{(k)}_{j,r}:0\le j\le e,\ 0\le r<k^j\,\}.
\]
Then $\dim_{\Q}V_{k,e}=k^e+1$, and
\[
 \mathcal B_{k,e}
 =\{F^{(k)}_{0,0},F^{(k)}_{1,0}\}
 \cup\{\,F^{(k)}_{j,r}:1\le j\le e,\ 1\le r<k^j,\ k\nmid r\,\}
\]
is a basis.  Every omitted section reduces to a basis element by an explicit
scalar: $F^{(k)}_{j,0}=k^{j-1}F^{(k)}_{1,0}$ for $j\ge1$, and if $r=k^tu$ with
$t=\max\{s:k^s\mid r\}\ge1$ and $k\nmid u$, then
\[
 F^{(k)}_{j,r}=C_k(t,u)\,F^{(k)}_{j-t,u},
 \qquad
 C_k(t,u)=k^t\prod_{\substack{p\mid k\\ p\nmid u}}\Bigl(1-\tfrac1p\Bigr).
\]
\end{theorem}

The condition $k\nmid r$ allows residues with $\gcd(k,r)>1$.
For instance, $r=1$ and $r=2$ are both retained in base six, and the
reductions involving them are
\[
 \varphi(36n+6)=2\varphi(6n+1),\qquad
 \varphi(36n+12)=4\varphi(6n+2).
\]
The factor $2$ is already present in $6n+2$ but not in $6n+1$;
only newly introduced prime divisors contribute factors $1-1/p$.
Thus one removes whole factors of $k$ from the residue, not every
common prime factor. Repeated prime factors of $k$ cause no exception.

Martin's 2006 theorem on simultaneous totient inequalities already
implies independence for every finite family of pairwise
nonproportional affine forms with positive integral slopes
\cite[Thm.~1]{martin-phi-inequalities}. Its conclusion is stronger
than the independence used here. The point of the present description
is the explicit reduction of the sections and the integral relation
basis in Corollary~\ref{cor:integral-normal-form}.
We also give a direct proof that constructs a nonsingular evaluation
matrix. It uses the CRT--Dirichlet separation argument in Yazdani's
proof of Theorem~2, credited there to Shallit
\cite[pp.~652--653]{yazdani2001}. The step that needs care is to keep
the progression for the selected affine form reduced while imposing
prime divisors on all the others.

The proof in Section~\ref{sec:rank} separates scalar reduction from
independence. Section~\ref{sec:base-six-test} recovers the integral
relations, starting in base six. Section~\ref{sec:family} uses prime
isolation for bounded functions of totient residues.
The series
\[
 S=\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{\varphi(n)}{2^n}
\]
has unbounded coefficients. Its irrationality, asked in
Erd\H{o}s Problem~\#249~\cite[p.~61]{erdosgraham1980}, remains open.
The coefficient basis supplies no irrationality conclusion for this
value. Appendix~\ref{app:sources} gives further coefficient
consequences; Appendices~\ref{sec:carry-rank} and~\ref{sec:open}
collect the tail identities, comparison sequences and still-unproved
cancellation estimates relevant to $S$. Their longer proofs are in
\longrecord{long249-r3-complements}{\emph{The Binary Totient Series}}.

\section{Proof of the basis theorem}\label{sec:rank}
\papersectiontarget{short249-proof}
\subsection*{Scalar reductions}
The product formula proves spanning. For independence we first
separate nonproportional affine arguments, then handle the two
proportional arguments $n$ and $kn$ separately. The reductions depend
only on which prime divisors of $k$ are already present.
For a zero residue, $kn$ and $k^jn$ have the same prime divisors when
$n>0$. Hence
$\varphi(k^jn)=k^{j-1}\varphi(kn)$; both sides vanish at $n=0$.
For an omitted positive residue $r=k^tu$ with $t\ge1$ and $k\nmid u$,
we have $t<j$ because $0<r<k^j$. Put $m=k^{j-t}n+u$. A prime $p\mid k$ divides $m$ exactly when it divides
$u$. The product formula for $\varphi(k^tm)/\varphi(m)$ therefore
contributes $k^t$ and one factor $1-1/p$ for each prime $p\mid k$
absent from $u$. The multiplier is an integer: its denominator is a
product of distinct primes dividing $k$, and therefore divides $k^t$.
This gives $C_k(t,u)$ and proves that the stated family spans $V_{k,e}$.

\subsection*{Affine independence}
Independence follows from Martin's theorem. For any ordering of
pairwise nonproportional affine forms and any $C>0$, it gives a set
of positive lower density on which each successive totient ratio
exceeds $C$. In a proposed nonzero relation, place a nonzero
coefficient $c_1$ first and take
$C>\max(1,\sum_{i>1}|c_i|/|c_1|)$. The first term then dominates
the sum of all remaining terms in absolute value, a contradiction
\cite[Thm.~1]{martin-phi-inequalities}. This also proves independence
after any finite prefix is deleted.

The direct proof separates one column at a time: all its competitors
will vanish modulo a prime, while its own entry will not. Let
$L_i(n)=a_i n+b_i$ for $1\le i\le s$, where $a_i>0$ and
$a_i b_j-a_j b_i\ne0$ for $i\ne j$. A shift of $n$ makes all the
$b_i$ positive and leaves these cross determinants unchanged.
Write $g_i=\gcd(a_i,b_i)$ and $A_i=L_i/g_i$. We must make $A_i$
prime, not $L_i$: the fixed divisor $g_i$ may prevent $L_i$ from
taking large prime values. We seek a prime
$\ell$ and points $n_1,\ldots,n_s$ for which
\begin{equation}\label{eq:diagonal-evaluation}
 E=\bigl(\varphi(L_j(n_i))\bigr)_{1\le i,j\le s}
 \equiv
 \begin{pmatrix}
 \varphi(g_1)&0&\cdots&0\\
 0&\varphi(g_2)&\cdots&0\\
 \vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
 0&0&\cdots&\varphi(g_s)
 \end{pmatrix}\pmod\ell,
 \qquad \ell\nmid\prod_i\varphi(g_i).
\end{equation}
Evaluating a rational relation at these points gives $Ec=0$.
But $\det E\not\equiv0\pmod\ell$, hence $E$ is invertible over $\Q$
and $c=0$. It remains to construct its rows.

For row $i$, we require $L_j(n_i)$ to have a prime divisor
$q_{ij}\equiv1\pmod\ell$ whenever $j\ne i$. Then $q_{ij}-1$
divides $\varphi(L_j(n_i))$, giving the off-diagonal zeros in
\eqref{eq:diagonal-evaluation}. For the diagonal entry, we require
$A_i(n_i)$ to be a prime congruent to $2$ modulo $\ell$.
Choose an odd $\ell$ dividing none of the slopes $a_i$, the products
$g_i\varphi(g_i)$ or the nonzero cross determinants. For each fixed
$i$, Dirichlet's theorem supplies distinct primes
$q_{ij}\equiv1\pmod\ell$ ($j\ne i$) outside the same finite exceptional
set. We impose
\[
 A_i(n)\equiv2\pmod\ell,\qquad
 L_j(n)\equiv0\pmod{q_{ij}}\quad(j\ne i).
\]
Each congruence is soluble because its slope is coprime to its
modulus. CRT combines them into $n=n_0+Ru$, where
$R=\ell\prod_{j\ne i}q_{ij}$.

CRT supplies the congruences, but not yet a prime value. Dirichlet
applies to the progression of values of $A_i$, namely
\[
 A_i(n_0+Ru)=A_i(n_0)+(a_i/g_i)Ru.
\]
Its initial value must be coprime to its step. The step has only
three kinds of prime factors: those of $a_i/g_i$, excluded by
primitivity; $\ell$, excluded by the residue $2$; and the imposed
$q_{ij}$. Such a $q_{ij}$ cannot divide
$A_i(n_0)$: it would then divide both $L_i(n_0)$ and $L_j(n_0)$, hence
\[
 a_iL_j(n_0)-a_jL_i(n_0)=a_i b_j-a_j b_i,
\]
contrary to its choice. This is where nonproportionality is used:
the nonzero cross determinant lets us keep each imposed divisor
away from the selected form. We may now choose $p=A_i(n_i)$ prime,
as large as needed,
and in particular with $p\nmid g_i$. Then
\[
 \varphi(L_i(n_i))=\varphi(g_i)(p-1)
       \equiv\varphi(g_i)\not\equiv0\pmod\ell,
\]
while all the other entries of row $i$ vanish modulo $\ell$.
We choose the rows separately, applying Dirichlet's theorem once
more for each selected form $A_i$. Thus no simultaneous primality
assertion is required. The construction proves
\eqref{eq:diagonal-evaluation} with every $n_i$ beyond any prescribed
threshold.

\subsection*{The two zero-residue sections}
The arguments $n$ and $kn$ are proportional, so they escape the
preceding argument. On $n=km+1$ we have
$\varphi(kn)=\varphi(k)\varphi(n)$: this merges their coefficients
and lets affine independence remove all the other sections.
The positive-residue sections become the forms
\[
 k^{j+1}m+k^j+r\quad(k\nmid r),
\]
together with $km+1$. These are pairwise nonproportional: equality of
two intercept-to-slope ratios would give
$r/k^j=r'/k^{j'}$, which for $j'>j$ forces $k\mid r'$.
No positive-residue form is proportional to $km+1$.
If $A,B$ multiply $\varphi(n),\varphi(kn)$, affine independence
therefore leaves $A+B\varphi(k)=0$ and eliminates every other
coefficient. At $n=k$, however, the ratio is $k$, giving $A+Bk=0$.
Since $\varphi(k)<k$, these two equations separate the remaining
coefficients: $A=B=0$. Finally,
\[
 \lvert\mathcal B_{k,e}\rvert
   =2+\sum_{j=1}^e(k^j-k^{j-1})=k^e+1,
\]
which completes the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:kkernelrank}.
For independence on a tail, use a sufficiently large power of $k$
in the last evaluation.

\section{Integral relations}\label{sec:base-six-test}
\papersectiontarget{short249-base-six}
Once independence is known, a linear identity can be tested by
substitution alone: reduce to the retained sections and compare their
coefficients. Consider base six at level two, where the family has
$43$ indices.
The $37$ retained sections are $F_{0,0},F_{1,0}$, the five
$F_{1,r}$ ($1\le r\le5$), and the $30$ sections $F_{2,r}$ with
$1\le r<36$ and $6\nmid r$. The omitted sections satisfy
\[
\begin{aligned}
 F_{2,0}&=6F_{1,0}, & F_{2,6}&=2F_{1,1}, & F_{2,12}&=4F_{1,2},\\
 F_{2,18}&=3F_{1,3}, & F_{2,24}&=4F_{1,4}, & F_{2,30}&=2F_{1,5}.
\end{aligned}
\]
The substitutions test an identity by leaving $37$ retained
coefficients to compare with zero. To recover the identity itself,
we must distinguish the formal symbols $E_{j,r}$ from the sequences
$F_{j,r}$ they represent: the symbols are independent even when the
sequences coincide. For example, the substitution
$F_{2,12}=4F_{1,2}$ corresponds to the decomposition
\[
 x_{1,2}E_{1,2}+x_{2,12}E_{2,12}
 = (x_{1,2}+4x_{2,12})E_{1,2}
   +x_{2,12}(E_{2,12}-4E_{1,2}).
\]
The retained coefficient becomes $x_{1,2}+4x_{2,12}$, while the
coefficient of the removed relation is the original $x_{2,12}$.
Repeating at the six omitted indices both tests the identity and
recovers its unique relation coefficients. In the opening binary
example the relation vectors are $E_{2,0}-2E_{1,0}$ and
$E_{2,2}-E_{1,1}$. The same calculation gives the general result.

\begin{corollary}[Integral coordinates and all integral relations]\label{cor:integral-normal-form}
Let $k\ge2$ and $e\ge1$. The retained family is a $\Z$-basis of the
module generated by the sections through level $e$. Index the sections
by their level and residue, retaining distinct indices even when they
define equal sequences. For each omitted index $i$, write the scalar
reduction as $F_i=a_iF_{j(i)}$, where $j(i)$ is retained and $a_i$ is a
nonnegative integer. In the free abelian group with one generator $E_i$
for each of these indices, the vectors
\[
 R_i=E_i-a_iE_{j(i)}\qquad(i\text{ omitted})
\]
form a $\Z$-basis of the kernel of evaluation $E_i\mapsto F_i$.
In particular, every integral relation has a unique integral expression
in these elementary relations, and their rank is
$\sum_{j=1}^{e-1}k^j$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Since $\prod_{p\mid k,\,p\nmid u}p$ divides $k$, every scalar
$C_k(t,u)$ is an integer. The retained family therefore spans the
section module over $\Z$, and its rational independence makes it a
$\Z$-basis.

For any vector $x=\sum_i x_iE_i$, substitute
$E_i=R_i+a_iE_{j(i)}$ at each omitted index and collect terms:
\[
 x=\sum_{j\text{ retained}}
       \left(x_j+\sum_{\substack{i\text{ omitted}\\j(i)=j}}a_i x_i\right)E_j
       +\sum_{i\text{ omitted}}x_iR_i.
\]
The first sum gives the retained coordinates of the evaluated
sequence; the second evaluates to zero. Thus $x$ is a relation
exactly when the first sum vanishes, in which case
$x=\sum_{i\text{ omitted}}x_iR_i$. This expression is unique:
at omitted index $i$, the coefficient of $\sum_h c_hR_h$ is $c_i$
itself. Each substitution has a unit coefficient at its own omitted
index, so the entire calculation takes place over $\Z$, without division.
Counting omitted indices gives
$(1+k+\cdots+k^e)-(k^e+1)=\sum_{j=1}^{e-1}k^j$.
\end{proof}
This is an integral basis of the \emph{section-generated} module,
not of every integer-valued sequence in its rational span.
For $k=2$, $e\ge2$, the sequence $\varphi(4n+3)/2$ is integer-valued
but has retained coordinate $1/2$. Thus the section module is not
saturated in the ambient group of integer-valued sequences.
There is no conflict with the integral relation basis: that basis
concerns relations among the given generators, not divisibility of
the sequences they generate.

An alternative is to test equality at finitely many inputs. Return
to base six and level two. Order the retained columns as above, with
residues increasing within each level, and evaluate at
$n=0,\ldots,36$ to obtain a $37\times37$ matrix.
Its determinant is $17$ modulo $101$, so it is nonsingular over $\Q$.
Consequently a retained combination is zero if and only if all $37$
of its integer evaluations are zero. Here reduction modulo $101$
certifies the matrix, not the equality of a proposed combination:
$101F_{2,2}$ would vanish modulo $101$ at every input, although its
integer value at $n=0$ is $101$.
The script \texttt{scripts/totient\_kernel\_normal\_form.py} calculates
the coordinates and the first nonzero evaluation using integer arithmetic.

\section{Bounded functions of totient residues}\label{sec:family}
\papersectiontarget{short249-residues}
The matrix argument separates one value from finitely many others.
Now we separate one coefficient from all its neighbours in a long
window. Boundedness turns that local separation into an irrationality
proof: the right zero block forces a rational tail to vanish exactly;
the left makes the isolated nonzero term too small to be rational
with the same denominator. Residues in the first sum below are least
nonnegative representatives, not necessarily binary digits.
\begin{theorem}[Series of totient residues]\label{res:residueseries}
For every $m\ge3$,
\[
 \sum_{n\ge1}\frac{\ph(n)\bmod m}{2^n}\notin\Q.
\]
For $k\ge1$ and $f:\Z/2^k\Z\to\Q$, the series
$\sum_{n\ge1}f(\ph(n)\bmod2^k)2^{-n}$ is rational exactly when $f$ is
constant on the even residue classes. If that constant is $c$, its value is
$3f(1)/4+c/4$.
\end{theorem}
Only $f(1)$ and the even classes are visible to the series:
$\varphi(1)=\varphi(2)=1$ and all later totients are even.
This explains the rational case, including the value $3/4$ modulo
two. The irrational case rests on the pattern
\[
 \underbrace{0,\ldots,0}_{L\text{ terms}},\quad
 a_N\ne0,\quad
 \underbrace{0,\ldots,0}_{L\text{ terms}}.
\]
The coefficients outside the window may have either sign; only their
uniform bound matters.
\begin{lemma}[An isolated nonzero coefficient between long zero blocks]\label{lem:bounded-pulse}
Let $a_n\in\Z$ satisfy $|a_n|\le C$. Suppose that for arbitrarily large $L$
there is $N>L$ such that $a_N\ne0$ and
$a_{N+t}=0$ for $0<|t|\le L$. Then $\sum_{n\ge1}a_n2^{-n}$ is irrational.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Suppose that the sum has denominator $q\ge1$. Multiplying it by
$2^j$ and removing its finite integer prefix shows that every scaled
tail $T_j=\sum_{t\ge1}a_{j+t}2^{-t}$ belongs to $q^{-1}\Z$.
Choose a pattern with $C2^{-L}<1/q$. The block on the right gives
\[
 |T_N|\le C\sum_{t>L}2^{-t}=C2^{-L}<1/q.
\]
The only element of $q^{-1}\Z$ in this interval is zero, so $T_N=0$.
The right block has removed the unknown tail exactly. Starting before
the left block now leaves only the nonzero centre, rescaled:
\[
 T_{N-L-1}=a_N2^{-L-1},\qquad
 0<|T_{N-L-1}|\le C2^{-L-1}<1/q,
\]
contradicting $T_{N-L-1}\in q^{-1}\Z$.
\end{proof}
For example, the indicator of the squares satisfies the lemma.
An eventually periodic sequence with infinitely many nonzero terms
does not have isolated coefficients at arbitrarily large distances.
The uniform bound $C$ is essential to this argument.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{res:residueseries}]
We prescribe a prime at the centre and a different divisor at each
neighbour. Fix a unit $s\pmod m$ and an integer $L\ge1$, and choose
distinct primes $q_t\equiv1\pmod m$, all exceeding $L$, for
$0<|t|\le L$. The congruences
\[
 p\equiv s\pmod m,\qquad p\equiv-t\pmod{q_t}\quad(0<|t|\le L)
\]
define a reduced progression, since $s$ is a unit modulo $m$ and
$0<|t|<q_t$. CRT and Dirichlet's theorem therefore allow us to choose
an arbitrarily large prime $p>L+1$ on this progression, with
$m\mid\varphi(p+t)$ at every neighbour and
$\varphi(p)\equiv s-1\pmod m$ at the centre.

For a rational-valued function $f$ on the residue classes, subtract
$f(0)$ and multiply by a common denominator of its values. Since
$\sum_{n\ge1}2^{-n}=1$, these operations preserve whether the sum is
rational. If $f(s-1)\ne f(0)$, the resulting bounded integer sequence
has an isolated nonzero coefficient for every $L$, so the lemma
applies.
For the least-residue map, $s=-1$ gives the nonzero centre $m-2$
when $m\ge3$. For $m=2^k$, the possible centres $s-1$ run through
\emph{every} even class. Thus any failure of constancy on those
classes supplies a centre with $f(s-1)\ne f(0)$.
Conversely, constancy makes every term after $n=2$ equal $c$, giving
$3f(1)/4+c/4$.
\end{proof}
The dyadic modulus matters to the classification. Modulo $6$, for
example, prime centres of this form see only $s-1\in\{0,4\}$, not
the even class $2$. The least-residue assertion needs just one
nonzero centre; classification of an arbitrary function needs all
relevant classes.

The first assertion is also an application of Erd\H{o}s's sparse-series
criterion~\cite[Lem.~1, p.~213]{erdos1957}, and of Kaneko, Suzuki and
Tachiya~\cite[Thm.~3]{kst2026}. The residues are bounded and nonnegative,
and the prime construction above makes their support infinite.
The nonzero residues occur on a set of density zero: an integer in that
set has no prime divisor
$p\equiv1\pmod m$. Wong gave the case in which the base equals the
modulus~\cite{wong2015}. The isolated-coefficient argument above also
treats signed values of $f-f(0)$, for which those nonnegative
sparse-series criteria need an additional spacing hypothesis.
The \longrecord{long249-r3-residue-extension}{extension to other bases}
treats every integer $t\ge2$ and proves rational linear
independence of $1$ and $\sum_n(\varphi(n)\bmod m)t^{-dn}$, $d\ge1$.

\appendix
\section{Further consequences for the sections}\label{app:sources}
\papersectiontarget{short249-sources}
Every finite set of dyadic sections lies in a single finite level.
Theorem~\ref{thm:kkernelrank} therefore also determines the full kernel;
``span'' and ``relation'' here still mean finite linear combinations.

\begin{corollary}[Dyadic basis]\label{res:basis}
The family
\[
 \{\ph_{0,0},\ph_{1,0}\}
 \cup\{\ph_{j,r}:j\ge1,\ 0<r<2^j,\ r\text{ odd}\},
 \qquad \ph_{j,r}(n)=\ph(2^jn+r),
\]
is a basis for the rational span of the full dyadic kernel. Every rational
relation is generated by the scalar reductions. The complete level-zero
truncation has dimension one.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Each finite subset of this family is contained in some
$\mathcal B_{2,e}$. Theorem~\ref{thm:kkernelrank} gives independence,
and its reductions give spanning. At level zero the sole section
$\varphi(n)$ is nonzero, since $\varphi(1)=1$.
\end{proof}

The affine argument also permits periodic coefficients by restriction
to their common residue classes.
\begin{corollary}[Periodic coefficients]\label{cor:periodic-freezing}
Let $L_1,\ldots,L_s$ be pairwise nonproportional affine forms with integer
coefficients and positive slopes. If $w_1,\ldots,w_s$ are rational-valued
periodic sequences, then
\[
 \sum_i w_i(n)\ph(L_i(n))=0\quad\text{for all sufficiently large }n
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad w_i(n)=0\quad\text{for every }i,n.
\]
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
We choose a common period $Q$ and restrict to $n=r+Qt$. The
coefficients are now the constants $w_i(r)$, and the cross
determinants are $Q(a_i b_j-a_j b_i)\ne0$. By shifting $t$ beyond
the given threshold we can apply affine independence, obtaining
$w_i(r)=0$ for every $i$. We repeat this for each residue $r$;
periodicity then gives the conclusion even at the indices preceding
the threshold.
\end{proof}
Proportional forms are excluded for a reason: the identity
$\varphi(4n+2)=\varphi(2n+1)$ would otherwise contradict the conclusion.
Martin's theorem gives the same corollary on each
progression~\cite[Thm.~1]{martin-phi-inequalities}.

Coons proves nonregularity using the poles of
$\zeta(s-1)/\zeta(s)$~\cite[Cor.~3.1, Thm.~3.2]{coons}.
The ranks above recover it: finite rational kernel dimension would
embed the integral kernel module by evaluations in $\Z^d$, making
it finitely generated; the converse is immediate
(compare~\cite[App.~A]{coons-jsr}).
Allouche, Shallit and Yassawi survey the earlier separation method
\cite[Thm.~3, Ex.~4, Rem.~7]{allouche-shallit-yassawi}.

The generating function $F_\varphi(z)=\sum_{n\ge1}\varphi(n)z^n$
therefore satisfies no $k$-Mahler equation
$\sum_{i=0}^dP_i(z)F_\varphi(z^{k^i})=0$ with $P_i\in\Q[z]$,
$P_0\ne0$. Indeed, $\varphi(n)\le n$ gives logarithmic height at most
$\log n$, and the height theorem of Adamczewski, Bell and Smertnig
would make such a series $k$-regular
\cite[Thm.~1.2(b)]{adamczewski-bell-smertnig2023}.
Bell and Smertnig's multiplicative classification gives the same
conclusion~\cite[Thm.~1.3]{bell-smertnig2026}.
Bell, Bruin and Coons treat related algebraic and $D$-finite series
(those satisfying polynomial-coefficient linear differential equations)
\cite[Thms.~1.5--1.6]{bell-bruin-coons}.
These are statements about the function, not its value at $1/2$.

The section operators $T_i g(n)=g(kn+i)$ satisfy
\[
 T_iF_{j,r}=F_{j+1,k^ji+r},\qquad
 V_{k,e+1}=V_{k,e}+\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}T_iV_{k,e}.
\]
Every residue at level $j+1$ has the form $k^ji+r$, giving the second
identity. A positive retained residue stays retained. For the zero-residue
generators, reduce $T_iF_{0,0}=F_{1,i}$ and $T_iF_{1,0}=F_{2,ki}$ as
before. Each image is a positive integer multiple of a basis element,
but rank growth prevents a finite invariant truncation. Consequently
the growth formula using the joint spectral radius for regular sequences
\cite[Thm.~1, Prop.~4, Cor.~7]{coons-jsr} does not apply.

\section{The binary totient series}\label{sec:carry-rank}
\papersectiontarget{short249-tails}
The proof of Theorem~\ref{res:residueseries} uses boundedness where a long zero
block makes the tail small. For $S$, the estimate
$0\le\varphi(n)\le n$ proves convergence, but forcing
$2^L\mid\varphi(N+L)$ with $N+L>1$ already requires
$2^L\le N+L-1$. The tail bound $(N+L+2)2^{-L}$ then exceeds $1$.
This is a failure of the estimate, not a lower bound on the tail.
The same divisibility that creates the gap can therefore move it too
far out for this bound to be useful. We turn instead to a finite
test for differences of scaled tails.

Put $R_N=\sum_{j\ge1}\varphi(N+j)2^{-j}$ and
$\Delta_h(N)=R_{N+h}-R_N$. Subtracting the finite-prefix identities
for $2^{N+h}S$ and $2^NS$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tail-phase}
 \Delta_h(N)\equiv2^N(2^h-1)S\pmod\Z.
\end{equation}
If $S=a/(2^cv)$, with $v$ odd, then $\Delta_h(N)$ is integral for
$N\ge c$ and $h=\varphi(v)$. The tails are then periodic modulo
integers, not necessarily equal. To disprove rationality it therefore
suffices to find a nonintegral difference beyond every threshold for
each positive $h$.

For $h,N,L\in\N$, define the integer
\[
 D_{h,N,L}=\sum_{j=0}^{L-1}
   \bigl(\varphi(N+h+1+j)-\varphi(N+1+j)\bigr)2^{L-1-j},
 \qquad B=N+h+L+2.
\]
Since $0\le R_M\le M+2$, we have
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tail-error}
 2^L\Delta_h(N)-D_{h,N,L}=R_{N+h+L}-R_{N+L},\qquad
 |2^L\Delta_h(N)-D_{h,N,L}|\le B.
\end{equation}
Nonnegativity gives the larger tail bound, not the sum. Thus
$D_{h,N,L}/2^L$ locates $\Delta_h(N)$ within $B/2^L$.
The error interval contains no integer precisely when the residue
stays more than $B$ from both endpoints:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:r3-certificate}
 B<D_{h,N,L}\bmod2^L<2^L-B.
\end{equation}
This proves $\Delta_h(N)\notin\Z$ and, by \eqref{eq:tail-phase},
excludes every reduced denominator dividing $2^N(2^h-1)$.
For example, $D_{1,12,16}=-143140$ has residue $53468$ modulo $2^{16}$,
and $B=31$, so it excludes denominators dividing $2^{12}$.
In ordinary coordinates the same certificate reads
\[
 \Delta_1(12)\in
 \left[\frac{-143171}{65536},\frac{-143109}{65536}\right]
 \subset(-3,-2).
\]

The test is complete for a \emph{fixed} difference: if
$\delta=\operatorname{dist}(\Delta_h(N),\Z)>0$, eventually
$2^L\delta>2B$, since $B=N+h+L+2$ grows linearly. The same error
estimate gives \eqref{eq:r3-certificate}. Failure at one depth proves
no integrality; the interval may be too wide. Increasing $L$ detects
an existing nonintegral difference, but does not supply one for each
$h$ beyond every threshold.

\subsection{Equivalent residue conditions}\label{sec:frontier}
We write $K(h,N,L)$ for \eqref{eq:r3-certificate}.
\begin{theorem}[Propagation of one nonintegral tail difference]\label{res:fulldepth}
Fix $d\ge1$ and $N\ge0$. If $\Delta_d(N)\notin\Z$, then every sufficiently
late pair $\{t,t+1\}$ contains an $m$ such that $K(md,N,md)$ holds.
Consequently
\[
 \exists t\ge1:\ K(td,N,td)
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\Delta_d(N)\notin\Z.
\]
Requiring this for every $d\ge1,N\ge0$ is equivalent to $S\notin\Q$.
\end{theorem}
Completeness alone lets the depth grow while the shift stays fixed.
Here the two must grow together. The recurrence
$\Delta_{(t+1)d}(N)\equiv2^d\Delta_{td}(N)+\Delta_d(N)\pmod\Z$
provides the extra step: two consecutive failures would make the
fixed nonintegral $\Delta_d(N)$ arbitrarily close to an integer. The estimates are in
\longrecord{long249-r3-ray}{Appendix A of the companion paper}.
A single pair $d,N$ need not imply irrationality; the final assertion
requires every positive $d$ and every $N\ge0$.

For a hypothetical denominator $2^cv$, a second test uses the
modulus $M=(2^H-1)/v$, where $H$ is a positive multiple of $\varphi(v)$.
Write $B_{H,c}=\sum_{j=0}^{H-1}\varphi(c+1+j)2^{H-1-j}$.
\begin{equivform}[A residue test modulo a divisor of $2^H-1$]\label{res:canonicalmersenne}
The series $S$ is irrational if and only if, for every $c\ge0$ and odd
$v\ge1$, some positive multiple $H$ of $\ph(v)$ satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:canonical-gap}
 c+H+1<(-B_{H,c})\bmod M<M-(c+H+1).
\end{equation}
\end{equivform}
The identity $B_{H,c}=M(vR_c)-\Delta_H(c)$ explains the modulus.
Under rationality, $vR_c\in\Z$, so only the small tail difference
remains modulo $M$. Conversely, for irrational $S$, the distance of
the fixed number $vR_c$ from $\Z$ is positive; multiplication by the
exponentially growing $M$ eventually dominates the linear error.
The \longrecord{long249-r3-mersenne}{proof and residue recurrence}
make this comparison explicit.
The required residues have not been shown to exist for every choice
of the parameters in either test. A separate
\longrecord{long249-r3-farey}{Farey-interval calculation} at depth $240$
excludes $S=a/q$ for $0<q\le Q_0\approx7.96\times10^{34}$.

\subsection{Comparison sequences}\label{sec:nogo}\label{sec:signed-interpolation-transfer}
To turn section rank into irrationality, one would need an upper
bound on carry rank under rationality. The following comparison
shows why rationality and linear growth alone cannot give it.
There is an integer
sequence $c$ with $0\le c(n)\le n$, unchanged odd totients and
$|c(n)-\varphi(n)|\le2$ at even indices, for which
$\sum c(n)2^{-n}=5/4$. Its carry is the integer sequence
\[
 u_N=4\sum_{j\ge1}c(N+j)2^{-j}
     =5\cdot2^N-4\sum_{n=1}^{N}c(n)2^{N-n}.
\]
Its recurrence is $2u_{N-1}-u_N=4c(N)$ for $N\ge1$.
For $j\ge1$ and odd $0<r<2^j$, this gives
\[
 4\varphi(2^jn+r)=2u_{2^jn+r-1}-u_{2^jn+r}.
\]
Thus the $2^e-1$ independent odd-residue totient sections lie in the
span of the carry sections through level $e$. This explains how the
rank lower bound survives even though the sum is rational.
The \longrecord{long249-r3-comparisons}{construction and its limitations}
explain why it fails the exact prime-dilation laws of $\varphi$.
The same section retains the cofinal LCM-diagonal criterion, its endpoint
sign conditions and the limits of finite integer combinations of shifts.

The \longrecord{long249-r3-interpolation}{signed interpolation
argument} tests whether preserving specified analytic data fixes the
value. For each integer $\beta\ge2$, sparse even-index corrections
realise every value in an interval about $\sum\varphi(n)\beta^{-n}$,
with a prescribed prefix and all odd coefficients fixed. Errors are
bounded by any prescribed positive $f(n)\to\infty$; their support has
upper Banach density zero. On each fixed arithmetic progression the
Dirichlet-series difference is entire. At roots of unity the
generating-function difference extends smoothly radially, with any
prescribed finite collection of radial derivatives zero. This preserves
Dirichlet singularities, not regular parts or zeros; a finite jet is
not the full radial germ. The proof is not formalised in Lean and
estimates no cancellation for the unchanged totient sequence.

Rational approximation imposes a different requirement. The identity
\[
 \Theta_2:=\sum_{d\ge1}\frac{\mu(d)}{(2^d-1)^2}=S-\frac12
\]
was posted by Amiram Eldar, together with its coprimality interpretation,
on 15 March 2026~\cite{eldar2026oeis}, and by Steve Fan on
16 May 2026~\cite{fan2026totient}.
Consider the rational quotients
\[
 Q(e,Y)=\frac{\bigl(\sum_{d\le Y}\mu(d)/(2^d-1)^{e+2}\bigr)^2}
      {\sum_{d\le Y}\mu(d)/(2^d-1)^{2e+2}}.
\]
\begin{theorem}[A lower bound for the rank-one quotients]\label{res:rankonefloor}
For $e\ge1$ and $Y\ge4$, the denominator of $Q(e,Y)$ is positive, and the
unique minimiser is $(e,Y)=(1,5)$. Every admissible quotient and every
nonempty finite positive weighted average of such quotients satisfies
\[
 Q-\Theta_2>\frac{21}{320}.
\]
The uniform bound $Q-\Theta_2>1/15$ is false already at $(1,5)$.
\end{theorem}
The \longrecord{long249-r3-rankone}{proof} first makes the denominator
uniformly positive. A geometric bound handles all $e\ge2$; at $e=1$,
seven-term prefixes leave only $Y=4,5,6$ to compare directly.
The averaging assertion concerns averages of the quotients themselves.
It leaves open signed combinations, quotients formed after combining
the sums, and higher-rank kernels. For a denominator-clearing
irrationality argument, a sufficient target is
$0<q_j|\Theta_2-p_j/q_j|\to0$, with $p_j/q_j$ in lowest terms.
Indeed, if $\Theta_2=a/b$, any nonzero difference satisfies
$q_j|a/b-p_j/q_j|=|a q_j-bp_j|/b\ge1/b$.
Convergence alone gives no such contradiction.

\section{An exponential-sum question}\label{sec:open}
\papersectiontarget{short249-first-harmonic}
For every $h\ge1$ and every $X_0$, suppose that some integers
$X\ge\max(X_0,1)$ and $L\ge h$ satisfy
\begin{equation}\label{eq:r3-harmonic}
 16(2X+h+L+2)\le2^L,\qquad
 \operatorname{Re}\sum_{N=X}^{2X-1}
       \exp(2\pi iD_{h,N,L}/2^L)\le\frac9{10}X.
\end{equation}
Only a saving in the real part is needed: one must show that the
phases cannot all cluster near $1$. If every residue failed
\eqref{eq:r3-certificate}, each $D_{h,N,L}/2^L$ would be within
$1/16$ of an integer. Each exponential would then have real part at least
$\cos(\pi/8)>9/10$. Some $N$ in the block must therefore satisfy
\eqref{eq:r3-certificate}. For a hypothetical rational value, we choose
its period and then a sufficiently late block, contradicting
\eqref{eq:tail-phase}.

To separate the counting estimates from the cancellation estimate,
we single out one totient in each window. Fix $h,s\ge1$,
$L\ge s+h$, $X\ge1$ and $0<\eta<1$, and put
$t=L-s+1$ and $E_N=\exp(2\pi iD_{h,N,L}/2^L)$.
For $X\le N<2X$, let $p_N$ be the largest prime factor of $N+t$ and
$m_N=(N+t)/p_N$. Define
\[
\begin{aligned}
 \mathcal A&=\{N\in[X,2X):m_N\le\lfloor\sqrt X/2\rfloor,
                      \ p_N>2\lfloor\sqrt X\rfloor\},\\
 \mathcal G&=\{N\in\mathcal A:\varphi(m_N)\ge\eta m_N\}.
\end{aligned}
\]
All indices are integers. On $\mathcal A$, $p_N>m_N$, hence
$\varphi(N+t)=\varphi(m_N)(p_N-1)$. Since $h<t\le L$, this term
occurs in both tails, with net coefficient
$2^{-(t-h)}-2^{-t}=(2^h-1)/2^t$. Write
\[
 z_N=\exp\!\left(\frac{2\pi i(2^h-1)\varphi(m_N)(p_N-1)}{2^t}\right),
 \qquad w_N=E_N/z_N,
\]
and let $\bar z_m$ be the average over the indices in $\mathcal A$
with cofactor $m$, taking the average to be zero for an empty set.
We seek the four bounds
\begin{equation}\label{eq:r3-four-bounds}
\begin{aligned}
 \operatorname{Re}\sum_{N\in\mathcal G}w_N(z_N-\bar z_{m_N})
       &\le\tfrac{14}{25}X,\\
 \left|\sum_{N\in\mathcal G}w_N\bar z_{m_N}\right|
       &\le\tfrac1{100}X,\\
 \left|\sum_{N\in\mathcal A\smallsetminus\mathcal G}E_N\right|
       &\le\tfrac1{100}X,\\
 \left|\sum_{\substack{X\le N<2X\\N\notin\mathcal A}}E_N\right|
       &\le\tfrac8{25}X.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
These are an exact partition: outside $\mathcal G$ use the last two
sums; on $\mathcal G$ write
$w_Nz_N=w_N(z_N-\bar z_{m_N})+w_N\bar z_{m_N}$.
The bounds add to $(14/25+1/100+1/100+8/25)X=9X/10$.
There are two distinct issues. Small group means control the second
sum by the triangle inequality because $|w_N|=1$. They do not
control the first: subtracting $\bar z_m$ cancels an \emph{unweighted}
group sum, not one with varying $w_N$. No independence is assumed.
For example, $z=(1,-1)$ has mean zero, but the weights
$w=(1,-1)$ make $\sum w_N(z_N-\bar z)=2$.
This illustrates the distinction, not the behaviour of the totient phases.
The resulting real-part bound is not a $21X/25$ norm bound.

\begin{problem}[Four estimates for the first-harmonic sum]
\label{prob:firstharmonic}
For each $h\ge1$, do there exist $s\ge1$ and $0<\eta<1$ such that, for every
$X_0$, there are $X,L$ with
\[
 \max(X_0,1)\le X,\qquad h\le L-s,\qquad
 16(2X+h+L+2)\le2^L,
\]
for which those four bounds hold?
\end{problem}

Fix $s,\eta$ after $h$ but before $X_0$; all four estimates must hold
on the same block. Take the least $L\ge s+h$ satisfying
\eqref{eq:r3-harmonic}'s size inequality. The
\longrecord{long249-r3-counts}{smooth-number and small-cofactor estimates}
give the last two bounds for all sufficiently large $X$ when
$0<\eta<1/(300\log4)$. The elementary excluded-cofactor estimate is
$(3\eta\log4+o(1))X$; it needs no prime number theorem.
With the prime number theorem as input, the
\longrecord{long249-group-mean}{group-mean estimate} gives the second
bound for $s=26$, $\eta=1/1000$ at this depth and all large $X$;
the mean-subtracted bound remains open.
At these fixed parameters it would suffice to prove that bound for
arbitrarily large $X$: the other three already hold beyond fixed
thresholds and hence on those same sufficiently late blocks.

There is also a weaker sufficient condition, without a group-mean
input. Since $|\mathcal G|>67X/100$ for large $X$, the open bound
$\operatorname{Re}\sum_{N\in\mathcal G}E_N\le603X/1000$
would put the average on $\mathcal G$ below $9/10$:
$603/1000=(9/10)(67/100)$. Some good index would then pass the test.
This uses an average on $\mathcal G$, not a bound on the full block,
and is required for each $h$ on arbitrarily large blocks.
The \longrecord{long249-r3-interpolation}{ordinary comparison argument}
gives larger first harmonics at all sufficiently large admissible scales,
distinguishing its rational sequences from the cancellation required of
$\varphi$. Its proof is included with the interpolation construction.

Balasubramanian, Giri and Srivastav give correlation estimates applicable
to $\varphi(n)/n$, uniformly for $|h|\le x/2$~\cite[Thm.~2.2]{bgs2016}.
Their correlation is a product of two normalised totient values.
Here the modulus grows with $L$, and $w_N$ is an exponential of the
other, overlapping totient contributions. Uniformity in the shift
of that two-point product does not supply the required weighted
exponential estimate.
