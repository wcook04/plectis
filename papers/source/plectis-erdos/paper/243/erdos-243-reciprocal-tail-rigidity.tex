\begin{abstract}
We prove irrationality of the reciprocal sum when strictly increasing
positive integers satisfy $a_n^2/a_{n+1}=1+3/n+o(n^{-3})$.
Rationality would give integer tail numerators whose fourth differences
eventually vanish. The denominator recurrence then forces a square in a
cubic field; trace identities and a four-term calculation modulo seven
give a contradiction. Under rationality and the quadratic growth limit,
we also give separate criteria for the eventual Sylvester recurrence
using bounded numerator increases and weighted first crossings. The unrestricted Erd\H{o}s--Graham question remains open.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:problem}
The Sylvester recurrence $a_{n+1}=a_n^2-a_n+1$ gives a rational
reciprocal sum by telescoping:
\[
 \frac1{a_n-1}=\frac1{a_n}+\frac1{a_{n+1}-1}.
\]
For an increasing sequence of positive integers that eventually satisfies
this recurrence, the tail sum at every sufficiently large index is
$1/(a_n-1)$. Erd\H{o}s and Graham asked whether every rational
reciprocal sum with $a_{n+1}\sim a_n^2$ arises in this way.

\begin{problem}[Erd\H{o}s Problem 243]\label{res:problem}
Let $1\le a_1<a_2<\cdots$ be a sequence of integers with
\[
 \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{a_n}{a_{n-1}^{2}}=1
 \qquad\text{and}\qquad
 \sum\frac{1}{a_n}\in\Q .
\]
Then $a_n=a_{n-1}^{2}-a_{n-1}+1$ for all sufficiently large $n$.
\end{problem}

Erd\H{o}s and Graham \cite[p.~64]{erdosgraham1980} asked this question;
see also Erd\H{o}s \cite[p.~105]{erdos1988} and Bloom's catalogue
\cite{erdosproblems}. We prove the following irrationality result for a
restricted class of sequences with $a_{n+1}\sim a_n^2$.

\begin{theorem}[cubic-rate irrationality]\label{res:cubicrate}
A strictly increasing sequence of positive integers with
\[
 a_n^2/a_{n+1}=1+\frac3n+o(n^{-3})
\]
has irrational reciprocal sum.
\end{theorem}

The Sylvester recurrence gives $a_n^2/a_{n+1}-1=O(1/a_n)$, so the
cubic-rate hypothesis excludes that behaviour. It imposes much more
than the quadratic limit in Problem~\ref{res:problem}.

For example, set $a_1=8$ and
\[
 a_{n+1}=\left\lceil\frac{n a_n^2}{n+3}\right\rceil.
\]
This gives $8,16,103,5305,\ldots$. From $a_{n+1}\ge a_n^2/4$ we
obtain $a_n\ge4\cdot2^{2^{n-1}}$ by induction. The error introduced
by the ceiling is less than one, and hence
\[
 0\le 1+\frac3n-\frac{a_n^2}{a_{n+1}}<\frac{16}{a_n^2}=o(n^{-3}).
\]
The theorem applies to this explicitly defined sequence.

Koizumi's product criterion \cite[Corollary~4]{koizumi2025} requires
the increments of $P_n/a_n$, where $P_n=\prod_{j<n}a_j$, to have
nonpositive upper limit. Corollary~\ref{res:originalbounded} below allows
any finite upper limit. At the cubic rate, however, $P_n/a_n$ grows
like $n^3$ and its increments like $n^2$; neither criterion applies.
This comparison does not decide whether the LCM criteria apply: those
also depend on repeated factors in $P_n$.

Rationality supplies a positive integer tail numerator $C_n$, but not
a bounded one: here it grows cubically. We therefore apply integrality
to its finite differences. The exact comparison sequence
$F_n=n(n+1)(n+2)$ has $F_{n+1}/F_n=1+3/n$. The precision in the
hypothesis forces $\Delta^4C_n\to0$, hence eventual equality
$C_n=AF_n+B$. This extraction uses the numerator ratio and integrality, not the
exact denominator recurrence.

The denominator recurrence now becomes decisive. It imposes a square
condition at every root of a cubic modulo almost every prime.
Chebotarev's theorem turns these residue conditions into a square in a
cubic number field, where traces restrict the numerator to two
polynomials. A four-term calculation modulo seven excludes both.
Section~\ref{sec:secondaryrate} follows these two stages: first extract
the polynomial, then test whether it can satisfy the exact recurrence.

Sections~\ref{sec:bounded} and~\ref{sec:mass} give bounded-increment
and summability criteria. Section~\ref{sec:lcmrecords} clears tails by
a least common multiple and uses congruences to control first crossings
of new heights; its convergence hypothesis is not derived from the
quadratic limit. The
\href{erdos243-reciprocal-tail-reasoning-surface.pdf}{companion paper}
also treats nonintegral rates and proves a stronger cubic disagreement
theorem. Its proof locations and the formal sources are listed in
Appendix~\ref{app:index}.

\section{Tail numerators}\label{sec:transfer}\label{sec:state}
For the Sylvester sequence $2,3,7,43,\ldots$, whose reciprocal sum is
$1$, the successive tails are $1,1/2,1/6,1/42,\ldots$. Multiplication
by the products $1,2,6,42,\ldots$ leaves the constant integer numerator
$1$. For any rational reciprocal sum, including a denominator of the whole
sum in these products again gives integer numerators. We keep these
fractions unreduced: the exact multiplicative denominator update will
transfer the growth rate to the numerator.

Assume now that $a_n$ are strictly increasing positive integers,
$a_{n+1}/a_n^2\to1$, and $\sum_{n\ge1}1/a_n=p/q$ with positive integers
$p,q$. All indices in this construction start at $1$. Put
\[
 x_n=\sum_{k\ge n}\frac1{a_k},\qquad
 P_n=\prod_{1\le j<n}a_j,\qquad D_n=qP_n,\qquad C_n=D_nx_n.
\]
Here $P_1=1$, $D_1=q$ and $C_1=p$. Since every $a_k$ with $k<n$
divides $P_n$, subtracting the finite prefix from $p/q$ gives
\[
 C_n=pP_n-q\sum_{1\le k<n}\frac{P_n}{a_k}\in\N_{>0}.
\]
Positivity comes from $C_n=D_nx_n$. Removing $1/a_n$ from the tail gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:recurrences}
 C_{n+1}=a_nC_n-D_n,\qquad D_{n+1}=a_nD_n.
\end{equation}
Koizumi constructs these coordinates in Lemma~4
\cite[pp.~11--12]{koizumi2025}. On a pseudo-greedy tail his
$c_n,d_n,e_n$ correspond to $C_n,D_n,D_n-(a_n-1)C_n$. If the tail
is restarted with its value in lowest terms, both coordinates are
divided by the same fixed integer. Reducing at every step would instead
introduce varying cancellation factors into the numerator ratio. We
therefore extract the polynomial before dividing by the eventual fixed
gcd in Section~\ref{sec:cubic-normalisation}.

For large $n$, $a_{n+1}\ge a_n^2/2\ge2a_n$. The terms after
$1/a_{n+1}$ sum to at most $2/a_{n+2}\le4/a_{n+1}^2$, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tail-estimate}
 x_n=\frac1{a_n}+\frac1{a_{n+1}}+O(a_{n+1}^{-2}),
 \qquad
 \frac{C_{n+1}}{C_n}=\frac{a_n^2}{a_{n+1}}+O(1/a_n).
\end{equation}
For the second estimate, the first gives $a_nx_n=1+O(1/a_n)$. Use this in
\[
 \frac{C_{n+1}}{C_n}
 =\frac{a_n^2}{a_{n+1}}\,
  \frac{a_{n+1}x_{n+1}}{a_nx_n}.
\]
The last factor is $1+O(1/a_n)$, and $a_n^2/a_{n+1}\to1$, giving the
stated additive error. Quadratic growth makes $1/a_n=o(n^{-k})$ for every fixed $k$,
so this error preserves the precision required in the cubic theorem.
We keep the original index $n$ throughout that proof. Although an
eventual recurrence survives removal of a finite prefix, the expression
$1+3/n+o(n^{-3})$ changes its lower-order terms under an index shift.

\section{Proof of cubic-rate irrationality}\label{sec:secondaryrate}
Suppose, towards a contradiction, that the reciprocal sum in
Theorem~\ref{res:cubicrate} is rational. Construct $C_n,D_n$ as in
Section~\ref{sec:transfer}. Equation~\eqref{eq:tail-estimate} transfers
the rate without changing its precision:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cubic-ratio}
 \frac{C_{n+1}}{C_n}=1+\frac3n+o(n^{-3}).
\end{equation}
\subsection{An eventual cubic polynomial}\label{sec:cubic-extraction}
We use $F_n=n(n+1)(n+2)$ rather than $n^3$: its successive ratio is
\emph{exactly} $1+3/n$, so dividing by it removes the model without
introducing lower-order errors. Write
\eqref{eq:cubic-ratio} as $C_{n+1}=(1+3/n+\varepsilon_n)C_n$,
where $\varepsilon_n=o(n^{-3})$. Dividing out the exact model gives
\[
 z_n=\frac{C_n}{F_n},\qquad
 \frac{z_{n+1}}{z_n}=1+\frac{\varepsilon_n}{1+3/n}.
\]
The logarithmic increments are $o(n^{-3})$, so their absolute tails
are $o(n^{-2})$. Hence $z_n\to A>0$ with $z_n-A=o(n^{-2})$.
Multiplying by $F_n$ gives
\[
 C_n=AF_n+\delta_n,\qquad \delta_n=o(n).
\]

An $o(n)$ error can still oscillate too much for finite differences.
To control its first difference, subtract the numerator update for $C_n$
and the comparison update for $AF_n$:
\[
 \Delta\delta_n=\frac3n\delta_n+\varepsilon_n C_n=o(1),
 \qquad \Delta h_n=h_{n+1}-h_n.
\]
Here $C_n=O(n^3)$ makes the second term $o(1)$. Three more differences
still tend to zero and kill the cubic model:
\[
 \Delta^4 C_n=\Delta^3(\Delta\delta_n)\longrightarrow0.
\]
Integrality turns this limit into eventual vanishing. Newton
interpolation then expresses $C_n$ as a rational polynomial of degree
at most three. Since its difference from $AF_n$ is $o(n)$, the cubic,
quadratic and linear coefficients agree; only a constant can remain:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cubic-shape}
 C_n=A n(n+1)(n+2)+B,\qquad A\in\Q_{>0},\quad B\in\Q.
\end{equation}

The precision matters at the subtraction step. The integer sequence
$F_n+(-1)^n$ has ratio $1+3/n+O(n^{-3})$ and never agrees eventually
with a polynomial; its error has an oscillating first difference.
This example concerns polynomial extraction alone. It is not asserted
to satisfy the denominator recurrence.

\subsection{Reduction and the constant term}\label{sec:cubic-normalisation}\label{sec:reduction}
We now use the denominator recurrence. Both updates preserve common
divisors, so $G_n=\gcd(C_n,D_n)$ divides $G_{n+1}$ and every later
numerator. On the polynomial tail it therefore divides the third
difference $6A$, a fixed positive integer. The fourth difference gave
polynomiality; the nonzero third difference now bounds cancellation.
Thus $G_n$ stabilises at a positive integer $g$.

Dividing by this fixed $g$ gives $u_n=C_n/g$, $v_n=D_n/g$ in lowest
terms without changing the recurrences. Their coprimality persists as
follows.

\begin{proposition}[persistent coprimality]\label{res:reduced}
Let $a_n,u_n,v_n$ be integer sequences with $u_n>0$, $v_n\ge0$ and
$\gcd(u_n,v_n)=1$ satisfying
\[
 u_{n+1}=a_nu_n-v_n,\qquad v_{n+1}=a_nv_n.
\]
Then $\gcd(a_n,v_n)=1$. The $a_n$ are pairwise coprime, and
$\gcd(a_i,u_t)=1$ whenever $i<t$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
A prime dividing both $a_n$ and $v_n$ would divide $u_{n+1}$ and
$v_{n+1}$, contrary to reducedness. For $i<t$, the factor $a_i$ divides
$v_t$. Reducedness gives $\gcd(a_i,u_t)=1$, and
$\gcd(a_t,v_t)=1$ gives $\gcd(a_i,a_t)=1$.
\end{proof}

In the cubic case, adjacent numerators are also coprime, since
$\gcd(u_n,u_{n+1})=\gcd(u_n,v_n)=1$.
Write the polynomial for $u_n$ as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:normal-cubic}
 Q(n)=\frac m6 n(n+1)(n+2)+c.
\end{equation}
The third difference $m=6A/g$ is a positive integer. Since
$n(n+1)(n+2)/6=\binom{n+2}{3}$ is an integer, any sufficiently late
value $u_n=Q(n)$ gives
\[
 c=u_n-m\binom{n+2}{3}\in\Z.
\]
Adjacent coprimality now restricts this integer $c$.
If a prime $\ell$ divides $c$, choose an arbitrarily late
$n\equiv-1\pmod{6\ell}$. Both three-factor products contain $n+1$,
so division by $6$ leaves a factor $\ell$, even for $\ell=2,3$.
Thus $\ell$ divides both $Q(n)$ and $Q(n+1)$, contradicting adjacent
coprimality. This also excludes $c=0$.
Thus
\[
 m\in\N_{>0},\qquad c\in\{1,-1\}.
\]

Irreducibility follows from persistence of denominator primes.
The pairwise coprime multipliers $a_n>1$ supply infinitely many distinct
primes; each divides every later $v_t$ and no later $u_t$.
A rational root of $Q$ would reduce to a root modulo all but finitely
many primes. Choose a persistent prime outside those exceptions.
Every sufficiently late index in the root's residue class would give
$\ell\mid Q(t)=u_t$, contradicting reducedness. Thus $Q$ has no rational
root and, being cubic, is irreducible.

Set $\kappa=m/6$, $\eta=6c/m$, and
\[
 f(T)=T^3-T+\eta,\qquad Q(n)=\kappa f(n+1).
\]
\subsection{A square forced by a zero numerator}\label{sec:cubic-field}
At a zero of the numerator polynomial modulo a prime, the recurrence
must still relate the two neighbouring nonzero numerators. Eliminating
$v_n$ from two exact updates makes this constraint visible:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:three-tail}
 u_{n+2}=(a_n+a_{n+1})u_{n+1}-a_n^2u_n.
\end{equation}
Whenever $u_k\equiv0\pmod\ell$, it follows that
\[
 -u_{k-1}u_{k+1}\equiv(a_{k-1}u_{k-1})^2\pmod\ell.
\]
Thus the negative product of the neighbouring numerators must be a
square. Since our numerator is a polynomial, its roots modulo $\ell$
tell us exactly where to apply this observation.

Let $\ell\nmid6m$ be prime and let $r\in\mathbb F_\ell$ satisfy
$f(r)=0$. The constant $\eta=6c/m$ is nonzero modulo $\ell$, so
$r\notin\{0,1,-1\}$. Choose a sufficiently late $k$ with
$k+1\equiv r\pmod\ell$. Then $u_k=\kappa f(k+1)\equiv0$, whereas
\[
 f(r-1)=-3r(r-1),\qquad f(r+1)=3r(r+1).
\]
Consequently, in $\mathbb F_\ell$,
\[
 (a_{k-1}u_{k-1})^2=-u_{k-1}u_{k+1}
   =9\kappa^2r^2(r^2-1).
\]
Dividing by the nonzero square $9\kappa^2r^2$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:all-root-square}
 \ell\nmid6m,\quad r\in\mathbb F_\ell,\quad f(r)=0
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad r^2-1\in(\mathbb F_\ell^\times)^2
 \qquad(\ell\text{ prime}).
\end{equation}
The condition holds for \emph{every} root; primes with no root impose
no condition. This quantifier matters in passing to a number field.

Let $\alpha$ be a root of $f$ and put $K=\Q(\alpha)$, a cubic field.
Suppose $\alpha^2-1$ is a nonsquare in $K$, and choose $\beta$ with
$\beta^2=\alpha^2-1$. To contradict the modular condition, we seek a
Frobenius that fixes $\alpha$ but exchanges $\beta$ and $-\beta$.
The nontrivial automorphism of $K(\beta)/K$ does exactly this. Extend
it to $\sigma\in\operatorname{Gal}(M/\Q)$, where $M$ is a Galois
closure of $K(\beta)/\Q$.

Chebotarev's density theorem
\cite[\S3, author-version p.~15]{stevenhagenlenstra1996} supplies
infinitely many unramified rational primes with Frobenius conjugate
to $\sigma$; choosing a prime of $M$ above each makes it equal to
$\sigma$. Omit residue characteristic two, primes dividing $6m$, and
the finitely many primes where $\alpha$ or $\beta$ is nonintegral or
$\beta$ reduces to zero. Frobenius acts by the $\ell$-th power on the
residue field, so
\[
 \bar\alpha^\ell=\bar\alpha,\qquad
 \bar\beta^\ell=-\bar\beta\ne\bar\beta.
\]
Thus $r=\bar\alpha$ lies in $\mathbb F_\ell$ and satisfies $f(r)=0$,
but neither root $\pm\bar\beta$ of $X^2-(r^2-1)$ lies in that field.
The condition for \emph{every} root in \eqref{eq:all-root-square}
applies to this $r$, giving the contradiction. Hence some $\beta\in K$
satisfies $\beta^2=\alpha^2-1$.

\subsection{Traces in the cubic field}\label{sec:cubic-trace}
We now need the square relation to constrain the coefficient $\eta$.
Factoring it produces reciprocal elements, avoiding a separate expansion
of the square root in a basis of $K$:
\[
 (\alpha+\beta)(\alpha-\beta)=1.
\]
Put $z=\alpha+\beta$. Then $z^{-1}=\alpha-\beta$ and
$2\alpha=z+z^{-1}$. Since $z\in K$ and $\alpha\in\Q(z)$, we have
$\Q(z)=K$. Write the minimal polynomial of $z$ as
\[
 p(T)=T^3+bT^2+dT+w,\qquad b,d,w\in\Q,\quad w\ne0.
\]
The minimal polynomial of $z^{-1}$ is the monic reciprocal polynomial
\[
 \frac{T^3p(T^{-1})}{w}
   =T^3+\frac dwT^2+\frac bwT+\frac1w.
\]
We compute the same three traces from $f$ and from the reciprocal
pair $z,z^{-1}$. All traces are from $K$ to $\Q$. The cubic for
$\alpha$ gives:
\[
 \operatorname{Tr}(\alpha)=0,\qquad
 \operatorname{Tr}(\alpha^2)=2,\qquad
 \operatorname{Tr}(\alpha^3)=-3\eta.
\]
Newton's identities for $p$ and its reciprocal give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cubic-traces}
 d=-bw,\qquad b^2+b(w-w^{-1})=1,\qquad
 \eta=\frac{(b^2+1)(w+w^{-1})}{8}.
\end{equation}
For completeness, the first equality follows from
$0=2\operatorname{Tr}(\alpha)=-b-d/w$. After substituting $d=-bw$,
Newton's identities give
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 \operatorname{Tr}(z^2)+\operatorname{Tr}(z^{-2})
   &=2b^2+2b(w-w^{-1}),\\
 \operatorname{Tr}(z^3)+\operatorname{Tr}(z^{-3})
   &=-3(b^2+1)(w+w^{-1}).
 \end{aligned}
\]
The first line is $4\operatorname{Tr}(\alpha^2)-6=2$.
The second is $8\operatorname{Tr}(\alpha^3)=-24\eta$, because the
mixed terms in $(z+z^{-1})^3$ have trace
$3\operatorname{Tr}(z+z^{-1})=0$. These give the other two equalities
in \eqref{eq:cubic-traces}.

Multiplying the middle equation by $w$ and bringing all terms to one
side gives $(bw-1)(b+w)=0$. Write $w=r/s$ in
lowest terms, with $r\ne0$, $s>0$. Substituting $b=-w$ or $b=1/w$
into the last equation and using $\eta=6c/m$ gives respectively
\[
 m=\frac{48c\,r s^3}{(r^2+s^2)^2}
 \qquad\text{or}\qquad
 m=\frac{48c\,r^3s}{(r^2+s^2)^2}.
\]
Here the integer scale $m$ is decisive: coprimality
$\gcd(r^2+s^2,rs)=1$ prevents cancellation with the variable factors
in either numerator, so $(r^2+s^2)^2$ divides $48$. Hence $r^2+s^2$ is $1$, $2$ or $4$.
Both $r$ and $s$ are nonzero, which leaves only $2$: a sum of two
nonzero integer squares cannot equal $1$ or $4$.
Thus $|r|=s=1$, and positivity of $m$ gives $m=12$.
The only remaining numerators are
\[
 Q(n)=2n(n+1)(n+2)+1
 \quad\text{and}\quad
 Q(n)=2n(n+1)(n+2)-1.
\]

\subsection{The contradiction modulo seven}\label{sec:cubic-mod-seven}
The three-term square condition has reduced the possibilities to two
cubics. We now use one more term: the denominator at the middle zero
must also come from the preceding exact update.
In the plus case, choose a late block starting at $n\equiv0\pmod7$;
in the minus case, start at $n\equiv1\pmod7$. The four successive
numerators have residues
\[
 (u_n,u_{n+1},u_{n+2},u_{n+3})\equiv
 \begin{cases}(1,6,0,2)&(c=1),\\(4,5,0,1)&(c=-1).
 \end{cases}
\]
Write $d_0,d_1$ for the residues of $v_n,v_{n+1}$ and
$c_0,c_1,0,c_3$ for the displayed numerator residues. From
$0=a_{n+1}c_1-d_1$ and $c_3=-a_{n+1}d_1$ we get
$d_1^2=-c_1c_3=2$. Thus $d_1\in\{3,4\}$.
The first update, however, gives
\[
 d_1=a_nd_0=\frac{d_0(d_0+c_1)}{c_0}.
\]
Completing the square in these two cases gives, in $\mathbb F_7$,
\[
 d_1+2=
 \begin{cases}
 (d_0+3)^2 &(c=1),\\
 2(d_0-1)^2 &(c=-1).
 \end{cases}
\]
Since $2=3^2$ in this field, $d_1+2$ must be a square in either case.
But $d_1\in\{3,4\}$ would give $d_1+2\in\{5,6\}$, both nonsquares.
The middle three terms admit $d_1=3$ or $4$; neither extends
backwards through the preceding exact update. Both cubics are excluded,
proving Theorem~\ref{res:cubicrate}.

Section~2 of the companion proves positive lower density of
disagreement with each rational cubic of this form. That lower bound
depends on the orbit and the cubic. For Theorem~\ref{res:cubicrate},
exclusion of eventual equality suffices.
\section{Bounded increases}\label{sec:bounded}
We return to the rational tails of Section~\ref{sec:transfer}.
The Sylvester example had a constant integer numerator. To measure
departure from this behaviour, put
\[
 E_n=D_n-(a_n-1)C_n=C_n-C_{n+1}.
\]
Negative errors are rises of $C_n$; zero error gives the tail
$1/(a_n-1)$. Instead of prescribing a polynomial, we now bound the
rises. Quadratic growth makes all steps small relative to $C_n$;
the additional absolute bound applies only to rises, not decreases.

\begin{theorem}[bounded negative part]\label{res:bounded}
Let $a,C,D:\N\to\N$ satisfy $a_n>1$, $C_n>0$, and
\[
 C_{n+1}+D_n=a_nC_n,\qquad D_{n+1}=a_nD_n
 \quad(n\ge0).
\]
Put $E_n=D_n-(a_n-1)C_n$. If
\[
 \frac{E_n}{C_n}\longrightarrow0
 \quad\text{and}\quad E_n\ge-B\quad\text{for all sufficiently large }n
\]
for some integer $B\ge0$, then $E_n=0$ for all sufficiently large $n$.
\end{theorem}
The linked theorem uses the equivalent division-free condition:
for every $K\in\N$, eventually $K|E_n|<C_n$. Under this condition,
absorption either settles the theorem or leaves $C_n\to\infty$.
A stable gcd and a forbidden block will exclude the latter alternative.

\subsection{Zero errors and descent}\label{sec:defect}\label{sec:descent}
Write $z_+=\max(z,0)$. The identities below use
\eqref{eq:recurrences}; positivity and $|E_n|<C_n$ are imposed only
where stated.

\begin{proposition}[error identities]\label{res:update}\label{res:defect}
For integer sequences satisfying \eqref{eq:recurrences},
\[
 C_{n+1}=C_n-E_n,\qquad
 \bigl(a_{n+1}-a_n^2+a_n-1\bigr)C_{n+1}=a_n^2E_n-E_{n+1}.
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The first identity follows by substituting $D_n=E_n+(a_n-1)C_n$
into the numerator recurrence. Substituting this expression at the
next index and using $D_{n+1}=a_nD_n$ gives the second.
\end{proof}

Equation~\eqref{eq:tail-estimate} now gives
$E_n/C_n=1-C_{n+1}/C_n\to0$, so $|E_n|<C_n$ for all sufficiently large $n$.
Koizumi proves this relative-gap limit for an eventual pseudo-greedy
tail \cite[Corollary~3, p.~9]{koizumi2025}. His rounding convention
also gives $-C_n/2\le E_n<C_n/2$, a stronger bound than we need.

Koizumi's Lemma~3 gives zero absorption for pseudo-greedy tails, and
Proposition~1(2) uses descent when their gaps are eventually nonnegative
\cite[p.~10 and p.~14]{koizumi2025}. The following form separates
what the two arguments require.

\begin{theorem}[absorption and descent]\label{res:absorb}\label{res:descent}
Let $a,C,D:\N\to\N$ satisfy $a_n>1$, $C_n>0$ and
\eqref{eq:recurrences}. If $|E_n|<C_n$ for every $n$, then $E_n=0$
implies $E_{n+1}=0$. For any positive integer sequence $C_n$ with
$C_{n+1}=C_n-E_n$, eventual nonnegativity of $E_n$ implies its eventual
vanishing.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
When $E_n=0$, the second identity in Proposition~\ref{res:update}
shows that $C_{n+1}$ divides $E_{n+1}$. The inequality
$|E_{n+1}|<C_{n+1}$ then forces $E_{n+1}=0$. For the second assertion,
$C_{n+1}=C_n-E_n$ makes the positive integer sequence $C_n$ eventually
nonincreasing, hence constant.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[two zero errors]\label{res:step}\label{res:eventual}
For integer sequences satisfying \eqref{eq:recurrences}, if
$E_n=E_{n+1}=0$ and $C_{n+1}\ne0$, then
$a_{n+1}=a_n^2-a_n+1$. In particular, if $a,C,D:\N\to\N$
also satisfy $a_n>1$ and $C_n>0$, eventual zero error implies
the eventual Sylvester recurrence.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Cancel the nonzero factor $C_{n+1}$ in the second error identity.
\end{proof}

Thus a late zero is absorbing. Without late zeros, $|E_n|\ge1$ and
$|E_n|/C_n\to0$ force $C_n\to\infty$. The scalar update permits
$C_n=n+1$, $E_n=-1$ forever; excluding this behaviour requires the
denominator recurrence.

\subsection{A stable gcd and a forbidden block}\label{sec:barrier}
\begin{proposition}[gcd stabilisation]\label{res:gcdstab}
Let $a,C,D:\N\to\N$ satisfy $a_n>1$, $C_n>0$ and
\eqref{eq:recurrences}. If some fixed integer $B\ge1$ satisfies
$-B\le E_n<0$ at infinitely many indices, then
$G_n=\gcd(C_n,D_n)$ is eventually constant. Dividing $C_n,D_n$ by
its eventual value gives coprime sequences satisfying the same recurrences.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
We have $G_n\mid G_{n+1}$ and $G_n\mid E_n$. Given $n$, choose $t\ge n$
with $-B\le E_t<0$. Then $G_n\le G_t\le -E_t\le B$.
Hence the positive divisibility chain stabilises. Division by its
stable value preserves the recurrences and gives coprime numerators and denominators.
\end{proof}
There are two distinct uses of the bound. Arbitrarily late negative
errors in a fixed interval suffice to stabilise the gcd. The crossing
argument below needs \emph{every} sufficiently late upward step bounded;
otherwise a single large rise could jump over the forbidden block.

A sequence avoiding multiples of $2$ and $3$ cannot cross the pair
$6k+2,6k+3$ from below with upward steps at most two: its first value
at least $6k+2$ would be one of those two integers. Decreases before
that crossing do not help. For a general step bound $B$, the Chinese
remainder theorem supplies a forbidden block of length $B$.

\begin{lemma}[consecutive multiples]\label{res:crt}
For pairwise coprime integers $m_0,\ldots,m_{B-1}\ge2$ and every lower
bound, there is a larger $t$ such that $m_i\mid t+i$ for each $i<B$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Solve $t\equiv-i\pmod{m_i}$ by the Chinese remainder theorem and add
multiples of $\prod_i m_i$ to exceed the prescribed bound.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Chinese remainder theorem and first crossing]\label{res:barrier}
Let $u:\N\to\N$ tend to infinity and let $B\ge1$ be an integer with
$u_{n+1}\le u_n+B$ for every $n$. There is no sequence of pairwise coprime
integers $m_i\ge2$ for which $\gcd(m_i,u_t)=1$ whenever $i<t$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Apply Lemma~\ref{res:crt} to $m_0,\ldots,m_{B-1}$, choosing
$t>\max(u_0,\ldots,u_B)$. There is a first $n>B$ with $u_n\ge t$.
Then
\[
 t\le u_n\le u_{n-1}+B<t+B.
\]
Thus $u_n=t+i$ for some $i<B<n$, and $m_i\mid u_n$, contrary to
$\gcd(m_i,u_n)=1$.
\end{proof}

Bado proves a two-sided bounded-error criterion by this forbidden-block
argument \cite[Theorem~5.1, pp.~4--5]{bado2026}. The gcd stabilisation
above allows us to use only the lower error bound.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{res:bounded}]
Suppose the conclusion fails. Absorption excludes late zeros;
$|E_n|\ge1$ and the relative limit then give $C_n\to\infty$.
Integer descent forces infinitely many late negative errors, where
$1\le-E_n\le B$. Thus $B\ge1$ and Proposition~\ref{res:gcdstab}
gives a stable gcd $g$. Restrict to a tail on which the gcd is $g$
and $E_n\ge-B$, then reindex that tail. Dividing by $g$ gives
$u_n=C_n/g\to\infty$ and
\[
 u_{n+1}-u_n=-E_n/g\le B.
\]
Proposition~\ref{res:reduced} makes the multipliers $a_i\ge2$ pairwise
coprime and coprime to every later numerator, contradicting
Theorem~\ref{res:barrier}.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{res:cor}
Under Theorem~\ref{res:bounded}, the multipliers satisfy
$a_{n+1}=a_n^2-a_n+1$ eventually.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Theorem~\ref{res:bounded} gives eventual zero error; positivity of $C_n$
and Corollary~\ref{res:step} give the recurrence.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The condition in the original sequence}\label{sec:product-bound}
Let $\gamma_n=a_n^2/a_{n+1}-1$. For $t_n=P_n/a_n$,
\[
 t_{n+1}-t_n=t_n\gamma_n.
\]
We bound upward increments, not values or decreases. The relation
$C_n\sim qt_n$ does not control increments; instead an exact identity
will give $E_n>-qt_n\gamma_n$, turning an upper increment bound into
a lower error bound.

\begin{corollary}[bounded increments of $P_n/a_n$]\label{res:originalbounded}
Let $a_1<a_2<\cdots$ be positive integers, $a_{n+1}/a_n^2\to1$, and
$\sum_{n\ge1}1/a_n\in\Q$.  Put $P_n=\prod_{j<n}a_j$.  If
\[
 \limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{P_n}{a_n}
 \left(\frac{a_n^2}{a_{n+1}}-1\right)<+\infty,
\]
then $a_{n+1}=a_n^2-a_n+1$ for all sufficiently large $n$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
For the canonical integer tail, direct substitution gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:canonical-dictionary}
 E_n+q\frac{P_n}{a_n}\gamma_n
 =qP_n\left(\frac1{a_{n+1}}
 -(a_n-1)\sum_{k\ge n+2}\frac1{a_k}\right).
\end{equation}
The tail estimate bounds the subtracted term by
$4(a_n-1)/a_{n+1}^2<1/a_{n+1}$ eventually. The positive remainder gives
$E_n>-qt_n\gamma_n$, so the assumed upper bound bounds $E_n$ below.
Only the remainder's sign is needed here. The canonical tails satisfy
the exact recurrences and, by \eqref{eq:tail-estimate}, $E_n/C_n\to0$.
Theorem~\ref{res:bounded} and Corollary~\ref{res:cor} finish.
\end{proof}

Koizumi's proof of Corollary~4(1) uses this product-weighted comparison
\cite[(11)--(12), p.~15]{koizumi2025}; that corollary assumes a
nonpositive upper limit, whereas the argument here allows any finite
upper limit. A corresponding LCM comparison appears below.
For $a_n=b^{2^{n-1}}$, $b\ge2$, the increments of $P_n/a_n$ are zero.
Since the sequence does not eventually satisfy the Sylvester recurrence,
the corollary gives irrationality.
For a Sylvester sequence starting at $a_1>1$,
$a_n-1=(a_1-1)P_n$ and the increments tend to zero.

Dividing by $n$ removes the nonsummable $1/n$ term. A summable upper
error then suffices, without a lower bound on $\gamma_n$.
\begin{corollary}[an inclusive one-sided $1/n$ bound]\label{res:inclusiveone}
Let $a_1<a_2<\cdots$ be positive integers with $a_{n+1}/a_n^2\to1$
and $\sum_n1/a_n\in\Q$. Suppose that for some $K\ge0$ and $\varepsilon>0$,
\[
 \gamma_n:=a_n^2/a_{n+1}-1\le \frac1n+\frac{K}{n^{1+\varepsilon}}
 \quad\hbox{for all large }n.
\]
Then the sequence is eventually Sylvester. In particular, the conclusion
holds under the pointwise eventual bound $\gamma_n\le1/n$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
The exact model $n$ has successive ratio $1+1/n$. Thus
$(t_{n+1}/(n+1))/(t_n/n)=(1+\gamma_n)/(1+1/n)
\le1+K/[n^\varepsilon(n+1)]$.
The convergent product of these upper bounds bounds $t_n/n$ above;
no positive limit is needed. Hence $t_n=O(n)$ and
$t_n\gamma_n\le t_n(\gamma_n)_+=O(1)$, so
Corollary~\ref{res:originalbounded} applies.
\end{proof}

The weaker condition $\limsup n(\gamma_n)_+\le1$ does not suffice
for the estimate $t_n=O(n)$. For example, the scalar ratios
$1+\gamma_n=1+(1+1/\log n)/n$ give a product of order $n\log n$.
This scalar product refutes the intermediate linear estimate under
the weaker rate, not the Sylvester conclusion for reciprocal tails.

For positive limiting increments, take
$a_1=4$ and $a_{n+1}=\lceil n a_n^2/(n+1)\rceil$, beginning
$4,8,43,1387$. Here $a_n\ge2\cdot2^{2^{n-1}}$ and
\[
 0\le1+\frac1n-\frac{a_n^2}{a_{n+1}}<\frac4{a_n^2}.
\]
Consequently $t_n\sim K n$ and $t_{n+1}-t_n\to K>0$.
The corollary proves irrationality because $\gamma_n\sim1/n$ is
incompatible with the eventual Sylvester recurrence.

For the introduction's $3/n$ ceiling example, division by
$n(n+1)(n+2)$ and summability of the rounding errors give
$t_n\sim K_3n^3$ and $t_{n+1}-t_n\sim3K_3n^2$, with $K_3>0$.
Its irrationality follows from Theorem~\ref{res:cubicrate}, beyond the
bounded-increase criterion.
\section{Finite total relative increase}\label{sec:mass}
Summable relative increases first bound $C_n$ by an infinite product,
then allow only finitely many rises; integer descent finishes.
No denominator recurrence is needed. Relative increases tending merely
to zero still allow $C_n=n+1$ or $C_n=(n+1)^2$.

\begin{theorem}[a convergent sum of relative increases]\label{res:massscalar}\label{res:mass}
Let $C_n$ be positive integers and $E_n$ integers satisfying
$C_{n+1}=C_n-E_n$. If
\[
 \sum_n\frac{(-E_n)_+}{C_n}<\infty,
\]
then $E_n=0$ eventually. Neither denominator dynamics nor vanishing relative error is required.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Set $\delta_n=(-E_n)_+/C_n$. Then
\[
 C_N\le C_0\prod_{n<N}(1+\delta_n)
 \le C_0\exp\!\left(\sum_n\delta_n\right).
\]
Choose an integer $K$ above this bound. A strict rise of the integer
sequence has size at least one, so it contributes at least $1/K$ to
$\sum\delta_n$. Hence only finitely many rises occur. Thereafter
$C_n$ is a nonincreasing positive integer sequence, and its eventual
constancy gives $E_n=0$.
\end{proof}

The proof also allows positive real $C_n$ with integer $E_n$: the values
lie in $C_0+\Z$, whose bounded positive part is finite. Discrete increments
are essential to this argument. With real errors, $C_n=1+1/(n+1)$
decreases forever with zero relative-increase sum; without positivity,
$C_n=-n-1$, $E_n=1$ does so too. The companion's Section~13 gives the details.

On an exact reciprocal-tail orbit the conclusion gives the Sylvester
recurrence. For the gap sequence of the pseudo-greedy expansion, the same
criterion, with the same product bound and integer descent, appears in the
Erd\H{o}s Problem a Day working report on Problem~\#243, dated 12 August 2026
\cite[``A global termination criterion'']{erdosproblemaday243}; the statement
above is for any positive integer sequence with $C_{n+1}=C_n-E_n$.
This summability condition and the bound in Theorem~\ref{res:bounded}
are different estimates. For exact reciprocal tails with $|E_n|/C_n\to0$,
each is equivalent to eventual vanishing of $E_n$; neither estimate has
been derived here from the unrestricted problem.

\section{New maxima of an LCM numerator}\label{sec:lcmrecords}

The least common multiple retains only the largest power of each prime
among $q,a_1,\ldots,a_{n-1}$. It preserves integrality, not coprimality:
a numerator may now take values forbidden in Section~\ref{sec:barrier}.
The recurrence will instead forbid short positive jumps from selected
multiples. Put
\[
 L_n=\operatorname{lcm}(q,a_1,\ldots,a_{n-1}),\quad
 M_n=D_n/L_n,\quad U_n=L_nx_n=C_n/M_n,\quad V_n=E_n/M_n.
\]
The denominator of $x_n$ divides $L_n$, so $U_n\in\Z$ and
$V_n=L_n-(a_n-1)U_n\in\Z$. The fraction $U_n/L_n$ need not be reduced.
With $\rho_n=\gcd(L_n,a_n)$, the exact updates are
\[
 M_{n+1}=M_n\rho_n,\qquad
 \rho_nU_{n+1}=U_n-V_n,\qquad V_n=L_n-(a_n-1)U_n.
\]
These coordinates and updates also appear in Bado's September 2026
preprint. With his denominator parameter equal to $q$, his $M_{n-1}$,
$\Delta_{n-1}$, $K_{n-1}$, $u_n$ and $g_n$ are respectively our $L_n$,
$M_n$, $U_n$, $V_n$ and $\rho_n$; his (19) is the middle update above
\cite[Proposition~7.1 and (16)--(20), pp.~6--7]{bado2026}.

Since $L_n\mid qP_n$ and $V_n=(L_n/(qP_n))E_n$, multiplying
\eqref{eq:canonical-dictionary} by $L_n/(qP_n)$ gives, eventually,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:general-clearance-dictionary}
 0<V_n+\frac{L_n}{a_n}\left(\frac{a_n^2}{a_{n+1}}-1\right)
 \le\frac{L_n}{a_{n+1}}.
\end{equation}
The lower bound transfers an upper bound on the growth defect to a
lower bound on $V_n$. The upper bound will let us compare weighted
series with a summable error.

Write $R_n=\max_{j\le n}U_j$ and
$\mathcal R=\{n:U_{n+1}>R_n\}$. Since $V_n/U_n=E_n/C_n\to0$,
eventually $|V_n|<U_n$ and $-U_n\le2V_n$. When $\rho_n\ge2$, the
first bound gives $U_{n+1}<U_n$; the second sharpens this to
$U_{n+1}\le3U_n/4$. Thus every late rise, in particular every record,
has $\rho_n=1$: its multiplier is coprime to $L_n$. At such a record
write the full jump as $d_n=U_{n+1}-U_n=-V_n>0$; at a contracting
step that equality need not hold.

\begin{theorem}[a convergent weighted sum over new maxima]\label{res:weightedrecord}
Assume the growth and rationality hypotheses of Problem~\ref{res:problem}.
Let $f:[1,\infty)\to[0,\infty)$ be finite and nonincreasing, with
$\int_1^\infty f(t)\,dt=\infty$. Then the sequence is eventually Sylvester
if and only if, for some integer $B\ge0$,
\[
 \sum_{n\in\mathcal R}(-V_n-B)_+f(U_n)<\infty.
\]
\end{theorem}

At a late record, the summand is $(d_n-B)_+f(U_n)$, where
$d_n=U_{n+1}-U_n$.

The full jump matters: with previous record $10$, a step from $5$ to
$12$ first crosses $11$. Its jump is $7$, but the record increases
by only $2$. This illustrates the two increments, not a denominator
sequence satisfying \eqref{eq:recurrences}.

For $B=0$, the integral comparison
\[
 \int_{R_n}^{R_{n+1}}f(t)\,dt
 \le (R_{n+1}-R_n)f(U_n)\le d_nf(U_n)
\]
already charges for every newly reached height. The arithmetic is
needed to retain divergence after subtracting a fixed $B$ at
\emph{each} record. We shall find a progression of heights whose first
crossings still cost at least one unit of excess jump.

The weights $1/t$ and $1/[t\log(et)]$ are allowed; $1/t^2$ is not.
One admissible weight and one fixed $B$ suffice; $B$ cannot grow
with the record. The limit $V_n/U_n\to0$ alone gives no summability.

\begin{proof}
Suppose the sequence is not eventually Sylvester. Absorption excludes
late zeros of $E_n$, so $V_n=E_n/M_n$ is eventually a nonzero integer.
Hence
\[
 \frac1{U_n}\le\frac{|V_n|}{U_n}=\frac{|E_n|}{C_n}\longrightarrow0.
\]
Thus $U_n\to\infty$. Infinitely many late records supply pairwise
coprime multipliers: each is coprime to its $L_n$, which is divisible by
every earlier multiplier. Since $a_n\to\infty$, for any fixed $B\ge1$
choose $B$ of them, $m_0,\ldots,m_{B-1}>B$. Take $T$ beyond their
indices and the threshold for $\rho_n=1$ at records.

Put $P=\prod_i m_i$ and choose $x$ with $m_i\mid x+i$ by the
Chinese remainder theorem. We select the heights
$\tau=x+B+kP>R_T$, $k\in\Z$. They are spaced $P$ apart.

At the first crossing of a selected height,
$U_n\le R_n<\tau\le U_n+d_n$. In particular this is a record step.
If $d_n\le B$, then $U_n\in[\tau-B,\tau)$, so
\[
 U_n=\tau-B+i=x+kP+i\qquad\text{for some }0\le i<B.
\]
The choice of $x$ gives $m_i\mid U_n$. This is allowed: $U_n/L_n$
need not be reduced. But $m_i\mid L_n$ too, so
$m_i\mid d_n=(a_n-1)U_n-L_n$, contradicting $0<d_n\le B<m_i$.
It is the short positive jump, not the numerator value, that is impossible.

A jump may first cross several selected heights. To charge all of them
to its single excess $d_n-B$, let their number be $h\ge1$. Their spacing gives
$(h-1)P<d_n$. Write $r=d_n-B\ge1$. Since $P\ge B+1$,
$d_n=B+r\le Pr$, and hence $h\le r$. Each of those heights exceeds
$U_n$, so monotonicity gives
\[
 \sum_{\substack{\tau\text{ first crossed}\\\text{at step }n}}f(\tau)
 \le(d_n-B)f(U_n).
\]
Each selected height above $R_T$ has one first crossing. The heights are
$P$ apart, with the first at most $R_T+P$. For $R_N\ge R_T+P$, the
intervals $[\tau,\min\{\tau+P,R_N\}]$ with $\tau\le R_N$ cover $[R_T+P,R_N]$.
Monotonicity bounds each integral by $P f(\tau)$, so the crossing bounds give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:weightedcrossing}
 \sum_{\substack{T\le n<N\\n\in\mathcal R}}(-V_n-B)_+f(U_n)
 \ge\frac1P\int_{R_T+P}^{R_N} f(t)\,dt.
\end{equation}
Since $R_n\to\infty$, the right side diverges for every $B\ge1$;
$B=0$ follows by domination. Conversely, the eventual Sylvester recurrence
gives by telescoping
$x_n=1/(a_n-1)$, so $V_n=0$ eventually.
\end{proof}

For example, $f(t)=1/[t\log(et)]$ gives the sufficient condition
\[
 \sum_{n\in\mathcal R}\frac{(-V_n-B)_+}{U_n\log(eU_n)}<\infty.
\]
Its finite lower bound in~\eqref{eq:weightedcrossing} is
$P^{-1}\log\bigl(\log(eR_N)/\log(e(R_T+P))\bigr)$.
Further fixed iterated logarithmic factors are allowed whenever the integral
still diverges. All these weights use the same crossing theorem.

For a series, sign control alone is not enough: we bound the total
change in its summands. Put $\gamma_n=a_n^2/a_{n+1}-1$ and
$\theta_n=E_n/C_n$. The defect identity gives
\[
 \gamma_n+\theta_n=
 \frac{(1-\theta_n)(a_n-1+\theta_{n+1})}{a_{n+1}},
 \qquad 0<\gamma_n+\theta_n<3/a_n
\]
eventually. Positive parts do not increase absolute differences.
Since $V_n=U_n\theta_n$, the summands
$U_nf(U_n)(\gamma_n-B/U_n)_+$ and $(-V_n-B)_+f(U_n)$ differ by at most
$3U_nf(U_n)/a_n\le3f(1)C_n/a_n$. This is the error \emph{after weighting}.
Now $C_n/a_n\sim qP_n/a_n^2$, since $a_nx_n\to1$, and
\[
 \frac{P_{n+1}/a_{n+1}^{2}}{P_n/a_n^{2}}
 =\frac{a_n^3}{a_{n+1}^{2}}\longrightarrow0.
\]
The ratio test therefore makes the weighted error summable, even over
all indices. The remainders in~\eqref{eq:canonical-dictionary}
and~\eqref{eq:general-clearance-dictionary} are likewise absolutely
summable: each is eventually between zero and
$qP_n/a_{n+1}=O(P_n/a_n^2)$.
Thus Theorem~\ref{res:weightedrecord} is equivalent to finiteness of
\begin{equation}\label{eq:weightedgrowth}
 \sum_{n\in\mathcal R}U_nf(U_n)
 \left(\frac{a_n^2}{a_{n+1}}-1-\frac B{U_n}\right)_+
\end{equation}
for some $B$. This is a reformulation of the extra hypothesis, not a
proof of it from quadratic growth. Both series use the full jump
$U_{n+1}-U_n$, including recovery from earlier decreases. Replacing it
by $R_{n+1}-R_n$, or merely proving that individual summands tend to
zero, would not establish the required finiteness.

\begin{corollary}[a bound using the least common multiple]\label{res:lcmbounded}
Assume the hypotheses of Problem~\ref{res:problem}. Write
$A_n=\operatorname{lcm}(a_1,\ldots,a_{n-1})$ with $A_1=1$. If
\[
 \limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{A_n}{a_n}
 \left(\frac{a_n^2}{a_{n+1}}-1\right)<\infty,
\]
then the sequence is eventually Sylvester.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Since $L_n/A_n=q/\gcd(q,A_n)$ lies in $[1,q]$, an upper bound on the
expression in the corollary gives an upper bound on
$(L_n/a_n)(a_n^2/a_{n+1}-1)$; negative terms remain negative.
Equation~\eqref{eq:general-clearance-dictionary} therefore bounds the
negative part of $V_n$. Choose an integer $B$ above
that eventual bound. The record series then vanishes after a finite prefix,
so Theorem~\ref{res:weightedrecord} applies.
\end{proof}

The expression in Erd\H{o}s and Straus's Theorem~3 is
\[
 \frac{[a_1,\ldots,a_k]}{a_{k+1}}
 \left(\frac{a_{k+1}^2}{a_{k+2}}-1\right),
\]
with a nonpositive upper limit \cite[p.~132]{erdosstraus1964}.
Setting $n=k+1$ gives exactly $(A_n/a_n)\gamma_n$: the prefix ends
before $a_n$, and the growth ratio begins at $a_n$.
Tijdeman and Yuan extend this type of criterion to positive numerators
\cite{tijdemanyuan2002}. Here any finite upper bound suffices under
the quadratic-limit assumption. Since $A_n\mid P_n$,
the LCM hypothesis is no stronger than the product hypothesis in
Corollary~\ref{res:originalbounded}: if the product expression is at most
$B$, the LCM expression is at most $\max(B,0)$. When the earlier terms
are pairwise coprime the two weights agree; repeated prime factors can
make the LCM much smaller. This comparison does not assert the existence
of a non-Sylvester rational example satisfying one bound but not the other.


The finite upper limit means an eventual upper bound; it does not require
$q$ to divide the LCM of the preceding denominators.
For positive integer summand numerators $b_n$, the modified error is
$V_n=b_nL_n-(a_n-1)U_n$. The companion, Section~6 under ``Integer
coefficients'', separately proves boundedness from a lower bound on
$V_n$ and eventual constancy when $V_n/U_n\to0$ is also assumed.
The latter conclusion must not be read into the first hypothesis alone.
The same section links finite examples separating them; compare Badea
\cite[p.~316]{badea1993} and Tijdeman--Yuan \cite{tijdemanyuan2002}.
The companion's Section~3 compares Duverney's signed-series criterion
\cite[Corollary~3.2, p.~287]{duverney2001}, whose printed condition is
one-sided, with the stronger absolute-convergence specialisation used
there. It also records the different growth hypotheses for irrationality
exponents in \cite[Theorem~1, author-version p.~2]{duverneykurosawashiokawa2020}.

\section{Further questions}\label{sec:open}
\begin{proposition}[necessary profile]\label{res:frontier}
The integer tail of a sequence satisfying Problem~\ref{res:problem}'s
hypotheses but not its conclusion has $E_n\ne0$ eventually,
$|E_n|/C_n\to0$, unbounded negative magnitudes along negative indices,
and
\[
 \sum_n\frac{(-E_n)_+}{C_n}=\infty.
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Absorption excludes late zeros, descent excludes an eventually
nonnegative error, and Theorems~\ref{res:bounded} and~\ref{res:massscalar}
exclude the two finiteness conditions.
\end{proof}

For $B\ge0$ put
\[
 F_B(X)=\sum_{\substack{n\in\mathcal R\\U_n\le X}}(-V_n-B)_+.
\]
We count record steps by their \emph{starting numerator} $U_n$, not
by time or the running maximum. Every selected height at most $X$ is first crossed
by a jump starting below $X$, even if it lands above $X$. Thus a
non-Sylvester orbit satisfies $F_B(X)\ge X/P_B-O_B(1)$, with an
orbit-dependent CRT modulus $P_B$; $U_n\to\infty$ also makes $F_B(X)$
finite. It would therefore suffice to prove from growth and rationality
that, for some fixed integer $B\ge0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:remaining-record-budget}
 \liminf_{X\to\infty}\frac{F_B(X)}X=0.
\end{equation}
The next lemma constructs an admissible weight from this vanishing
lower ratio: enumerate records by $j$ and put $u_j=U_n$,
$w_j=(-V_n-B)_+$. The resulting weighted series is finite, contradicting
Theorem~\ref{res:weightedrecord}. The estimate remains unproved; the
lemma changes what one must estimate, not the hypotheses available.

\begin{lemma}[weights and linear density]\label{res:weights}
Let $u_j$ be positive integers, let $w_j\ge0$, and put
$F(X)=\sum_{u_j\le X}w_j$, with the sum allowed a priori to be $+\infty$. Then $\liminf_{X\to\infty}F(X)/X=0$ if and only if
there is a finite nonincreasing $f:[1,\infty)\to[0,\infty)$ with
$\int_1^\infty f(t)\,dt=\infty$ and $\sum_jw_jf(u_j)<\infty$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Suppose the lower limit vanishes. Since $F$ is constant on $[m,m+1)$, choose
integers $X_1<X_2<\cdots$ with $X_k\ge2^k$ and
$F(X_k)\le2^{-k}X_k$. Set
\[
 f(t)=\sum_{k\ge1}\frac{\mathbf 1_{[1,X_k]}(t)}{X_k}.
\]
This function is nonincreasing and bounded by $\sum_k2^{-k}$.
Each cutoff contributes $1-1/X_k$ to its integral, but at most $2^{-k}$
to the weighted sum. Interchanging nonnegative sums therefore gives
\[
 \int_1^\infty f(t)\,dt=\sum_k(1-1/X_k)=\infty,
 \qquad
 \sum_jw_jf(u_j)=\sum_k\frac{F(X_k)}{X_k}\le1.
\]

Conversely, suppose $f$ is such a weight and $F(m)\ge cm$ for some
$c>0$ and all integers $m\ge M$. Monotonicity and the divergent integral
force $f(X)>0$, so $F(X)\le f(X)^{-1}\sum_jw_jf(u_j)<\infty$.
Thus partial summation applies; the nonnegative differences
$f(m)-f(m+1)$ preserve the lower bound on $F$. For $N>M$,
\[
 \sum_{u_j\le N}w_jf(u_j)
 =F(N)f(N)+\sum_{m=1}^{N-1}F(m)\bigl(f(m)-f(m+1)\bigr)
 \ge c\Bigl(Mf(M)+\sum_{m=M+1}^{N}f(m)\Bigr),
\]
every term being nonnegative. The right side diverges as $N\to\infty$, because
$\sum_{m\ge1}f(m)\ge\int_1^\infty f(t)\,dt=\infty$.
\end{proof}


\subsection{Arithmetic compatibility}\label{sec:scalar-limit}
The remaining estimate requires the denominator recurrence as well as
control of the error. For example, $C_n=n^2+1$, $E_n=-(2n+1)$ has vanishing
relative error and the necessary scalar pattern. But at $n=2,3,4$ its
numerators are $5,10,17$. The update $10=5a_2-D_2$ forces $5\mid D_2$,
hence $5\mid D_3=a_2D_2$; the next update would force $5\mid17$.
Thus these numerators cannot satisfy \eqref{eq:recurrences}. The companion's Section~14 also constructs
sequences avoiding sparse fixed moduli and distinguishes that avoidance
from compatibility with all later denominators. Neither property alone
supplies \eqref{eq:remaining-record-budget}.

Conversely, suppose integer sequences indexed from zero satisfy
\eqref{eq:recurrences}, with $a_n>1$, $C_n>0$, $D_n\ge0$ and
$E_n/C_n\to0$. Section~4 of the companion proves that $D_n>0$,
$C_n/D_n\to0$, $\sum_n1/a_n=C_0/D_0$ and $a_{n+1}/a_n^2\to1$.
The vanishing remainder $C_n/D_n\to0$ turns the finite reciprocal
identities into an infinite series. A scalar profile or finite prefix
does not supply it.

\begin{problem}[growth of repeated denominator factors]\label{res:lcmheight}
For every rational-tail orbit satisfying the hypotheses but not the conclusion of Problem~\ref{res:problem},
must
\[
 \limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log M_n}{n}>0?
\]
Equivalently, must there be a $K\ge1$ for which
\[
 2^n\le M_n^K
\]
at infinitely many indices?
\end{problem}
Section~\ref{sec:transfer} gives $C_{n+1}/C_n\to1$; averaging logarithms
therefore gives $\log C_n=o(n)$. Since $1\le M_n\le C_n$, every such
orbit already satisfies $\log M_n/n\to0$. A positive answer therefore
holds exactly when no such orbit exists: proving it from failure of
Sylvester behaviour would exclude every counterexample.

\section{A factorial modulus for integral recursion}\label{app:residue}

A constant-negative error $E_n=-m$ with $C_0=c$ gives $C_n=c+nm$ and the identity
\begin{equation}\label{eq:shape}
 D_n+m=(a_n-1)C_n.
\end{equation}
In the case $m=c=1$, where~\eqref{eq:shape} reads $D_n+1=(a_n-1)(n+1)$, each
multiplier is determined by its predecessor. The recursion is
$a_{n+1}=\fnum(n,a_n)/(n+2)$, where
\[
 \fnum(n,a)=(n+1)a^{2}-(n+2)a+(n+3),
\]
the
\lword{Erdos243/FiniteHorizonResidue.lean}{22}{forcedNumerator}{forced
numerator}. We stop at the first nonintegral quotient. This procedure
checks divisibility only; it does not impose the positivity conditions
required of an infinite reciprocal-tail sequence. The
\lword{Erdos243/FiniteHorizonResidue.lean}{75}{ForcedSurvives}{formal exact-division condition}
requires exact division at each step.
\begin{example}[a nonintegral second step]\label{ex:shape}
Starting from $a_0=3$, the first numerator $\fnum(0,3)=6$ is
divisible by $2$, giving $a_1=3$. The next numerator
$\fnum(1,3)=13$ is not divisible by $3$, so the recursion stops.
\end{example}
Direct iteration can produce very large intermediate integers. To
decide whether the first $h$ divisions are exact, however, it suffices
to know the initial value modulo $(h+1)!$.
Computing a pseudo-greedy orbit through residues modulo a shrinking product
modulus is Koizumi's method \cite[Remark~2 and Algorithm~1,
pp.~13--14]{koizumi2025}; for the forced numerator that product is a
factorial.

\begin{theorem}[factorial residue reduction]\label{res:residue}
Let $h$ be a nonnegative integer and let $a,b$ be integers with $a\equiv b\pmod{(h+1)!}$.
The first $h$ steps of the recursion
$a_{n+1}=\fnum(n,a_n)/(n+2)$, starting at index zero, are integral
for $a_0=a$ if and only if they are integral for $a_0=b$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Division by $i+2$ consumes that factor of the modulus; no modular
inverse is required. Define $M(0,i)=1$ and
$M(h+1,i)=(i+2)M(h,i+1)$. Induct on $h$, allowing any starting
index $i$: inputs congruent modulo $M(h,i)$ either both complete
$h$ integral steps or neither does. The case $h=0$ is immediate.

If $a\equiv b\pmod{(i+2)M(h,i+1)}$, the integer polynomial
$\fnum(i,\cdot)$ preserves this congruence. Its values are therefore
either both divisible by $i+2$ or neither is. In the latter case both
recursions stop. In the former, exact division leaves the quotients
congruent modulo $M(h,i+1)$, and induction supplies the remaining
$h$ steps. Finally $M(h,0)=(h+1)!$.
\end{proof}

For $h=1$, the condition is $2\mid a^2-2a+3$, or equivalently that $a$ is odd.
This illustrates Theorem~\ref{res:residue} with modulus $2!=2$.

The enumeration in Example~9.1 of the companion, over
$2\le a_0<5000$, gives at most $17$ successful updates ($18$ values
including $a_0$). The seed $a_0=1$ is excluded because it is fixed
and violates $a_n\ge2$. This finite calculation establishes only the
stated range. The infinite
\lword{Erdos243/ReciprocalTailRigidity.lean}{286}{no_constantNegative_orbit}{constant-negative exclusion}
requires the separate argument under the hypotheses given there.
